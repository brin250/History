## P1 Theme I · Origins of the Cold War

*How did the Cold War develop? How did the Cold War impact global developments after 1945? Guiding questions: Why/how did US–USSR relations alternate between tension and cooperation? Whose action was most responsible, and how were superpower actions related (action-reaction)?*

Topic 1 traces the emergence of the Cold War after WWII. It is examinable only through the source-based case study (no separate essay). The notes define the Cold War as extreme tension short of all-out war, marked by mutual hostility, propaganda, (mis)perceptions of security threats and a race for allies that divided Europe into competing blocs, with confrontation mainly USA vs USSR from 1949.

The roots lie in the 1917–1941 ideological/systemic action-reaction cycle. The Grand Alliance (1941–1945) was an “alliance of convenience” held together by Hitler; once Germany was defeated, new leaders, the atomic bomb, the end of Lend-Lease and the German and Polish problems exposed radically different postwar visions in a newly bipolar world.

The manifestations run chronologically: Yalta and Potsdam (1945) sowed the seeds; Sovietisation, the Long Telegram and the Iron Curtain speech (1945–46) hardened attitudes; the Truman Doctrine, Marshall Plan, Berlin Blockade and COMECON (1947–49) divided Europe, with 1948–49 the climax of Cold War I; NATO (1949) and the Warsaw Pact (1955) completed the division across ideological, political, economic, geographic and military dimensions.

The main line of argument: each side professed its actions as defensive while the other perceived them as aggressive. Security concerns (Soviet insecurity “real, repeated and recent”, US insecurity more pre-emptive) outweighed economic ones, and ideology was the lens through which the action-reaction cycle was interpreted and intensified. Four historiographical schools disagree over who was responsible.

**(A) Substantive issues: political shape of Europe & economics**

- Poland: Lublin (Soviet-backed) vs London (pro-West) governments; Stalin saw Poland as key to Russian security; the West was aggrieved the Red Army allegedly withheld help from the 1944 Warsaw Uprising
- Eastern Europe: Stalin expected influence given the Red Army's presence vs the US wanting Wilsonian self-determination
- USA reversed course from weakening Germany to rehabilitating it (Bizonia 1947 → Trizonia, 1948 currency reform, West Germany into NATO 1955) to serve export markets and stave off a postwar downturn
- USSR wanted Germany kept weak, extracted heavy reparations, and saw US aid as spreading capitalism; Stalin barred satellites from Marshall aid
- Key insight: an apparently economic conflict was really a manifestation of differing security perceptions

**Security and strategic interests**

- USA: had intervened in Europe twice in a generation; wanted a Wilsonian Europe (Atlantic Charter 1941, DLE 1945); feared the USSR dominating the Eurasian landmass (Leffler 2005); needed open European markets
- Yet US intelligence (Joint Intelligence Staff 1945; CIA 1947) believed the USSR would avoid war for 5–10 years — US insecurity based more on pre-emption than a real threat, exaggerated by hawkish politicians (Leffler)
- USSR: Stalin's overriding concern was state security; the Nazi invasion again exposed vulnerability via Central/Eastern Europe → buffer-zone sphere of influence
- Soviet insecurity was “real, repeated and recent”; US insecurity arose from a position of strength (fear of losing its W. European sphere and markets, memory of the Great Depression)

**(B) The action-reaction cycle and ideology**

- Wartime actions set off the cycle: Nazi-Soviet Pact, delayed Second Front, alleged refusal to aid the Warsaw Uprising, the atomic bomb on Japan — intensifying 1945–1955
- McCauley (1995): the stronger US could have recognised the Soviet sphere, shared atomic control and helped rebuild the Soviet economy, but misread Soviet security interests as expansionism → vicious, self-perpetuating cycle
- Originators professed actions as defensive; recipients perceived them as aggressive/provocative
- Varsori (2013): the Cold War “confirmed” pre-existing beliefs and prejudices; ideology was the lens through which the cycle was interpreted
- Over time economic reasons became subordinated to strategic-security reasons, and political considerations grew more significant

**Motivations by category (Section 23.2)**

- Ideological: USA — Wilsonian idealism, liberalism, capitalism, self-determination; USSR — class struggle, increasingly secondary to realpolitik security after 1945
- Political: stronger for the democratic USA (Roosevelt→Truman, Democrats vs Republicans, doves vs hawks); weaker/less overt in Stalin's authoritarian system
- Border security: less important for the USA (flanked by oceans); more important for the USSR (historical invasions via Eastern Europe) — hence Sovietisation/Stalinisation
- Economic: relatively less important for the USA; more important for the devastated USSR (reparations, COMECON), though by 1947 secondary to border security

### Case studies

#### Ideological/systemic action-reaction cycle (1917–1941)

From the Bolshevik Revolution onward, liberal-capitalist West and the communist USSR each saw the other's actions as hostile, though 1917 is not counted as the Cold War's start.

- 1917 not the start: no clear two-bloc ideological split yet (fascism, National Socialism, military dictatorships still competed)
- Soviet actions seen as aggressive: socialist Russia (1917), USSR with 14 non-Russian republics (1924), COMINTERN, Trotsky's “Permanent Revolution”, Nazi-Soviet Pact (1939)
- Western actions seen as hostile: Wilson's Fourteen Points (1917), intervention in the Russian Civil War (1919–1921), League of Nations (1919) excluding the USSR, sanctions in the 1920s
- Western appeasement of Hitler (1930s) pushed the USSR into a pact with Germany, which then made the West see the USSR as aggressive

**Why it matters:** Shows the ideological/systemic origins of mistrust and the earliest action-reaction pattern; supports arguments that ideology fuelled mutual suspicion (Varsori).

#### The Grand Alliance and the percentages agreement (1941–1944)

The common German threat after Operation Barbarossa drew the USA, UK and USSR into wartime cooperation, and the Big Three could get along.

- Edwards (2002): “the German invasion of the Soviet Union in June 1941 transformed US-Soviet relations”
- Roosevelt let the Red Army liberate Eastern Europe to keep Stalin in the alliance
- Churchill's percentages agreement (Oct 1944): Romania 90:10 USSR, Greece 10:90 UK, Yugoslavia/Hungary 50:50
- Stalin kept his promise on Greece

**Why it matters:** Evidence that factors for cooperation coexisted with tension; useful against views of Stalin as inherently expansionist.

#### Demise of the Grand Alliance (1943–1945)

Once the German threat receded, pre-existing tensions resurfaced, worsened by new leaders, new issues and disagreement over postwar Europe.

- Williamson (2001): “the real glue was Hitler”; Calvocoressi (2001): a “marriage of convenience”
- 1945: Attlee replaced Churchill; the more aggressive Truman replaced Roosevelt (Philips 2001: inexperience, desire to appear strong, hawkish advisors)
- New issues from 1944: atomic bomb, delayed Second Front, Red Army in Eastern Europe, UN formation (Soviets felt inadequately consulted), abrupt end of Lend-Lease
- German problem (partition, reparations) and Polish problem (Lublin vs London Poles, Katyn Forest massacre, 1944 Warsaw Uprising)
- Structural shift from multipolar to bipolar order as the USA and USSR filled the power vacuum

**Why it matters:** Supports structural explanations (bipolarity, end of common threat) and the role of personalities; Edwards (2002) stresses radically different postwar visions.

#### Yalta Conference and the Declaration of Liberated Europe (Feb 1945)

The Big Three agreed on occupying Germany and on the DLE, but differences over Soviet security and Poland were masked rather than resolved.

- Germany and Berlin to be divided into four occupation zones; all three agreed to join the UN
- Stalin appeared willing to broaden the Lublin government and agreed the USSR would enter the Pacific war after Germany's defeat
- Stalin secured a westward shift of the USSR/Poland borders
- DLE: interim governments broadly representative of all democratic elements, pledged to free elections
- The US accused the USSR of violating the DLE through “People's Democracies” and rigged elections

**Why it matters:** Failure to recognise Soviet security concerns and the USSR's failure to abide by the DLE laid the foundation for a disagreeable Potsdam; 2023 SBCS topic.

#### Between Yalta and Potsdam (Feb–Jul 1945)

Sovietisation pressures, Truman's arrival and the end of Lend-Lease worsened relations before Potsdam.

- People's Democracies continued forming in Red Army-liberated areas
- Members of the Polish Provisional Government-in-Exile were lured to Moscow for talks and arrested
- Truman succeeded Roosevelt (Apr 1945); ended Lend-Lease to the UK/USSR (May 1945) — seen as designed to weaken the USSR
- Churchill lost the general election; replaced by Attlee

**Why it matters:** Shows the action-reaction cycle accelerating as leadership changed; balances blame between both sides.

#### Potsdam Conference and the atomic bomb (Jul–Aug 1945)

Disputes over reparations and Germany's future surfaced openly, and the US use of the atomic bomb was seen by the USSR as reneging on agreements.

- The USSR demanded $10 billion in reparations; compromise: each side extracted from its own zone, with Western zones handing over a percentage of capital equipment
- The US feared German economic anarchy would retard European recovery; the USSR wanted Germany weak
- Atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki (Aug 1945) ended the Pacific war abruptly, limiting Soviet gains — a U-turn from earlier insistence the USSR join (Sakhalin/Kurile promised)
- Truman hoped the bomb would boost his bargaining power → hastened Sovietisation afterward

**Why it matters:** The US sought an order in which it could thrive, the USSR one in which it could be secure; these “fateful conferences” sowed the seeds of post-1945 tension. Revisionists cite the bomb as intimidation.

#### Sovietisation and Stalinisation of Eastern Europe (1945–1948)

A gradual, piecemeal process (“slicing the salami”) in which communist control was imposed on Eastern Europe through force, manipulation and intimidation.

- First wave (before 1947): Poland, Bulgaria, Romania; second wave (1947–48): Hungary, Czechoslovakia — a response to hardening US attitudes and Yugoslavia's break
- “Mini-Stalins”: Bierut & Gomułka (Poland), Gottwald & Slánský (Czechoslovakia), Rákosi (Hungary); Czech PM Masaryk found dead
- Seton-Watson's model: genuine coalitions → sham coalitions → total communist control
- Edwards (2002): little evidence in 1945–46 Stalin wanted to control all of Eastern Europe — free elections in Czechoslovakia (May 1946)
- Painter (1999): Soviets discouraged revolution in France, Italy, Greece, Spain; COMINFORM (1947) was the turning point

**Why it matters:** Key traditionalist evidence of Soviet expansionism and DLE violation, but the nuance supports security-first and post-Cold War “no master plan” views.

#### Kennan's Long Telegram and Article X (Feb 1946 / Jul 1947)

US diplomat George Kennan analysed Soviet conduct as internally driven and proposed long-term containment, lending support to a firmer Truman policy.

- Attributed Soviet behaviour to ideological fanaticism and commitment to world revolution, historical insecurity and “neurotic” leaders
- Expanded into “Article X: The Sources of Soviet Conduct” (Jul 1947) — a “splendid analysis” per Byrnes
- Containment: “confront the Russians with unalterable counter-force at every point...” — cornerstone of US policy for 4+ decades
- Kennan later disavowed the military reading: he wrote (1984) the danger was political, not military

**Why it matters:** Shows US perception driving policy; useful for evaluating sources on US hawkishness and misperception; Kennan is also a traditionalist author.

#### Churchill's Iron Curtain speech (Mar 1946)

At Fulton, Churchill publicly acknowledged the Grand Alliance schism with the image of an iron curtain across Europe.

- “From Stettin in the Baltic to Trieste in the Adriatic, an iron curtain has descended across the Continent”
- Hardened US public opinion, justified hawkish Western policy, increased Soviet fears of encirclement
- Less alarming than believed: “I do not believe that Soviet Russia desires war. What they desire is the fruits of war...”
- Churchill was no longer PM; superpower cooperation continued for a time

**Why it matters:** A classic SBCS source: powerful imagery but limited authority; useful for the “professed defensive vs perceived aggressive” evaluation.

#### Truman Doctrine (Mar 1947)

After Britain withdrew support from Greece, Truman used the Greek and Turkish situations to frame a global ideological struggle of freedom vs oppression — the beginning of containment.

- Irony: Stalin had been neutral in the Greek Civil War; Greek/Turkish governments were themselves corrupt/authoritarian
- Ended the Grand Alliance officially (“the point of no return”); broke the isolationist tradition; “imperial presidency” unchallenged until the War Powers Act (1973)
- Triggered COMINFORM and Zhdanov's “Two Camps” speech (Sep 1947); accelerated the second wave of Sovietisation
- US build-up: atomic stockpile 13→50 bombs by 1948, B-50 bombers, National Security Act 1947 (DoD/NSC/CIA)

**Why it matters:** Central to revisionist arguments that the US escalated; shows the action-reaction cycle in its clearest form.

#### Marshall Plan (European Recovery Programme) and Soviet response (Jun 1947 / Apr 1948)

Marshall announced $17 billion of aid over 4 years for European recovery — the economic half of “the same walnut” as the Truman Doctrine — which the USSR rejected as “dollar imperialism”.

- Announced 5 June 1947; $13 billion from 1948–1952
- Stated aim: fight “hunger, poverty, desperation and chaos”; Edwards (2002): “the real function...was the containment of communism” (French/Italian communists held ~20% of the vote)
- 75% of aid spent on US goods; recipients' industrial output rose 35% (agriculture +10%) by 1952
- Stalin barred satellites (incl. Czechoslovakia) from accepting; Molotov Plan → COMECON (Jan 1949)
- By Sep 1947 the division of Europe was clear

**Why it matters:** Supports both economic (revisionist) and containment/security readings; shows economics subordinated to security as the USSR rejected aid it needed.

#### Berlin Blockade and Airlift (Jun 1948 – May 1949)

After Western moves to rehabilitate Germany, culminating in the Operation Bird Dog currency reform, the USSR blockaded West Berlin; the West responded with an airlift until Stalin backed down.

- Context: US “reverse course” from the Morgenthau Plan; Byrnes's Stuttgart speech; Bizonia; communist coup in Czechoslovakia (Mar 1948)
- Operation Bird Dog (Jun 1948) introduced the Deutsche Mark, revitalising West Germany but causing severe inflation in East Germany
- 24 June 1948: road and rail access cut, power and coal supplies stopped; Stalin aimed to cancel West German state plans or force the West out (“prodding the capitalist world with the tip of a bayonet”)
- Airlift: 275,000 flights over 11 months; blockade called off May 1949
- Consequences: FRG (Apr 1949) and GDR (Oct 1949), division lasting to 1989; hastened NATO; Cold War began “travelling” outside Europe (Korea, 1950)

**Why it matters:** The climax of Cold War I, closest to hot war; the USSR saw a repudiation of Yalta, the US saw Soviet aggression. 2020 SBCS topic.

#### COMECON and the Soviet atomic bomb (Jan & Aug 1949)

The USSR formed COMECON as its answer to the Marshall Plan and tested its own atomic bomb, beginning the nuclear arms race.

- COMECON (Jan 1949) strengthened economic ties among socialist states; Stalin used enforced economic specialisation to control satellites
- Soviet atomic bomb tested Aug 1949
- The USSR remained inferior in nuclear strength until parity in the late 1960s/early 1970s (leading eventually to SALT I, 1972)
- Confrontation was mainly USA vs USSR from 1949, when the USSR obtained the bomb

**Why it matters:** Traditionalists cite COMECON and the A-bomb test as evidence of Soviet responsibility; shows the economic and nuclear division of the blocs.

#### Formation and expansion of NATO (Apr 1949 – 1955)

Twelve Western states formed a military alliance with mutual assistance under Article 5, later militarised and expanded to include West Germany.

- Founded Washington DC, 4 Apr 1949, 12 founding members; Article 5: an attack on one is an attack on all
- Partly a response to the Berlin Blockade, but a logical progression from the Dunkirk Treaty (1947), Brussels Defence Treaty (1948) and Vandenberg Resolution (1948)
- Edwards (2002): a Soviet invasion of Western Europe was “never regarded as a real possibility”
- Expanded to Greece & Turkey (1952), West Germany (1955), Spain (1982); Korean War spurred militarisation and CENTO/SEATO
- Admitting West Germany was a major U-turn showing the depth of Western fear of communism

**Why it matters:** Revisionist evidence of Western provocation (the USSR saw NATO as aggressive), but also shows Western fear; ironic that Soviet actions forced the West to consolidate and revive Germany.

#### Warsaw Pact (May 1955)

The USSR created the Warsaw Pact as a belated response to West Germany's NATO accession, completing the division of Europe.

- Created after West Germany formally joined NATO (May 1955)
- Originally defensive against a Western threat that never materialised; later used to invade Hungary (1956) and Czechoslovakia (1968)
- By 1955 Europe's division was complete across ideological, political, economic, geographic and military dimensions
- Eisenhower replaced Truman and Khrushchev succeeded Stalin, helping stabilise the Cold War

**Why it matters:** Endpoint of Topic 1: strategic-security concerns remained the key driver, with the same defensive-vs-offensive action-reaction pattern.

### Historians

- **Dockrill (1988):** The Cold War was extreme superpower tension stopping short of all-out war, with mutual hostility and covert/proxy warfare.
- **Dockrill (1998):** The Cold War stayed “cold” because nuclear weapons made war a suicidal enterprise.
- **Calvocoressi (2001):** Mutual hostility and fear rooted in historical/political differences; the 1941–45 alliance was a “marriage of convenience”.
- **Geoffrey Roberts:** A sharp, unexpected deterioration in US–Soviet relations from 1945, each blaming the other for dividing Europe into competing blocs.
- **Edwards (2002):** The 1941 invasion transformed US-Soviet relations; postwar visions differed (US global, Soviet limited); Stalin did not seek all of Eastern Europe in 1945–46; the Marshall Plan's real function was containment; a Soviet invasion of Western Europe was never a real possibility.
- **Williamson (2001):** Alliances need a common danger or shared principles; postwar Europe had neither — “the real glue was Hitler”.
- **Philips (2001):** Truman's anti-Soviet stance stemmed from inexperience, a desire to appear strong and hawkish advisors.
- **Painter (1999):** The Soviets discouraged revolution by Western communist parties; COMINFORM (1947) ended accommodation with the non-communist world.
- **Seton-Watson:** Sovietisation moved from genuine coalitions to sham coalitions to total communist control.
- **Kennan (1946–47; 1984):** Soviet aggression was internally driven and needed long-term containment, though he later said the danger was political, not military.
- **Leffler (2005):** The US feared Soviet domination of Eurasia, but its insecurity was pre-emptive rather than based on a real threat.
- **McCauley (1995):** The stronger US misread legitimate Soviet security interests as expansionism, starting a vicious action-reaction cycle.
- **Varsori (2013):** The Cold War confirmed pre-existing cultural, psychological and ideological prejudices on both sides.
- **Traditionalists (Feis, Schlesinger, Kennan):** Stalin's ideologically driven expansionism caused the Cold War; the US merely defended itself.
- **Revisionists (W.A. Williams, J. & G. Kolko, McCormick):** US anti-communism and economic expansionism (“US imperialism”) share or bear primary blame; Stalin had limited security aims.
- **Post-Revisionists (Gaddis 1983, Leffler):** Both sides pursued strategic interests, but Stalin bears more blame; US “empire by invitation” vs Soviet “empire by imposition”.
- **Post-Cold War (Gaddis, We Now Know; Westad):** Using Soviet archives, Stalin's paranoid security obsession bears responsibility, though he had no master plan and was opportunistic.
- **Painter & Leffler (2005):** Interpretations were grounded in ideological differences and shaped by the ongoing Cold War.
- **Kissinger:** The superpowers behaved like two heavily armed blind men, each believing himself in mortal peril from the other.
- **Westad (2010):** The Cold War must be placed in the larger context of chronological time and geographical space.

### Using this topic in the exam

- Topic 1 is SBCS-only (no separate essay); past-year topics: 2020 The Cold War and the Berlin Crisis, 2023 Yalta and Potsdam Conferences
- Common Part (b) angles: relative responsibility of the superpowers — use the four schools' evidence lists (traditionalist: DLE breach, Sovietisation, COMINFORM, COMECON, Berlin Blockade; revisionist: delayed Second Front, A-bomb, Truman Doctrine, Marshall Plan, Bird Dog, NATO)
- Use the “professed defensive vs perceived aggressive” framework to evaluate sources; very negative/one-sided sources are likely to be disputed via contextual knowledge, other sources and provenance
- Categorise causes as political shape of Europe / economic / security-strategic / action-reaction / ideology — and argue the economic conflict was really about security
- Avoid treating 1917 as the start or the Iron Curtain speech as a decisive break; note cooperation and tension coexisted in every phase and the balance was rarely permanent
- Know key figures exactly: $10 billion reparations demand, $17 billion Marshall aid, 275,000 airlift flights over 11 months, 12 NATO founders, atomic stockpile 13→50 bombs by 1948

## P1 Theme I · Korea, Cuba, Vietnam

*How did the Cold War develop? How did the Cold War impact global developments after 1945? For each case: who or what caused the conflict, what were each player’s aims and how did they change by phase, and was it a Cold War (proxy) conflict or a civil/local one — when and when not?*

Topic 2 follows the Cold War as it “travelled” out of Europe after the Berlin Blockade: to Asia with Korea (1950–53) and Vietnam (1946/1959–1975), and into the USA’s own hemisphere with Cuba (1962). Each is examined as its own source-based case study; the syllabus does NOT require you to compare the three against each other.

Every case starts as a local struggle — Korean reunification, Vietnamese anti-colonial nationalism, Cuban anti-imperialist revolution — that the superpowers then turned into a Cold War contest. The key analytical move is to periodise: when did outside powers drive events, and when did local actors? Korea was internationalised on 25–27 Jun 1950 and intensified by rollback and China’s entry; Cuba became a Cold War issue with Mikoyan’s 1960 visit; Vietnam began as more of a proxy conflict but local nationalism increasingly dominated.

The same dynamics recur: containment militarised and globalised by NSC-68; action–reaction spirals (invasion → intervention → rollback → Chinese entry; Bay of Pigs/Mongoose → missiles → blockade); domino logic carried from Korea into Indochina; and consistent superpower restraint short of direct hot war, especially once nuclear weapons were in play.

Impacts must be judged as change vs continuity: Korea militarised the Cold War and extended containment to Asia; the Cuban Missile Crisis was a “turning point… however” for arms control and détente; Vietnam produced Soviet Third World gains, the Vietnam Syndrome, détente and Sino-US rapprochement amid the Sino-Soviet split.

**Cold War conflict or local conflict?**

- Korea: initial motivations lay within Korea (Kim and Rhee both wanted reunification; border raids from 1949) — internationalised by US/UN entry after 25 Jun 1950, intensified by rollback and China’s entry.
- Proxy-war debate on Korea: local actors became subordinate to great-power aims, but the US and PRC committed their own troops, so it was less a classic proxy war.
- Cuba: the Revolution was fundamentally anti-imperialist and nationalist; Mikoyan’s Feb 1960 visit arguably turned a US–Cuba antagonism into a Cold War conflict by transgressing spheres of influence.
- Vietnam can be read three ways: a long nationalist struggle for independence/reunification; a proxy war (Hanoi’s hands tied by sponsors); and, for the USSR/PRC, an opportunity to use rapprochement with the US against the rival communist power.
- Overall Vietnam was more of a proxy conflict at the outset, but the local/nationalist dimension increasingly dominated, forcing the US to concede there was no purely military solution.

**Roles and responsibility of the players**

- Korea: Kim the initiator; Stalin the enabler; Mao minimal at outbreak (enabler via troop training); the USA indirectly via ambiguity (Acheson’s speech) and MacArthur’s rollback; Rhee shared unification ambitions but was restrained by Washington.
- Cuba: Khrushchev triggered the Crisis by deploying missiles; Kennedy escalated via the blockade but negotiated in good faith; Castro had a limited direct role and was excluded from the settlement.
- Vietnam: US containment, domino logic, credibility and policy inertia drove Americanisation (to 1968); military failure and domestic politics drove Vietnamisation; Hanoi’s reliance on Moscow/Beijing and their desire for better US relations shaped the end.
- The US consistently saw nationalist communists as part of a monolithic, Moscow-directed movement — reinforced by the “loss of China,” the Sino-Soviet Treaty and McCarthyism.

**Recurring dynamics (Topic 1 links)**

- Containment: Long Telegram/Truman Doctrine → NSC-68 militarised and globalised it; Korea turned it into a funded rearmament programme extending into Asia (SEATO 1954).
- Action–reaction cycles in Korea and Cuba: each side’s “defensive” act was read by the other as “aggressive.”
- Superpower restraint: MacArthur’s dismissal, blockade rather than air strikes/invasion, USSR never in direct combat in Vietnam.
- Sino-Soviet rivalry: deepened cooperation then tensions in Korea; open competition for Hanoi in Vietnam; Cuba valued by Moscow partly to stop it drifting to China.

**Impact on the Cold War: change vs continuity**

- Korea: militarisation (US defence spending 5% → 14.2% of GDP, $13.1bn → $50.4bn), pacts across Asia, West German rearmament/NATO entry (1955) → Warsaw Pact; Peaceful Coexistence (1953–58) owed more to MAD and Stalin’s death than to Korea.
- CMC: Hot Line (1962), PTBT (1963), NPT (1968), SALT I/ABM (1972) — however the arms race continued (MIRVs, Soviet parity by late 1960s, India’s 1974 test) and détente was arguably superficial.
- Vietnam: Soviet client states grew from 3 (1964) to 16; domino theory discredited; Vietnam Syndrome and War Powers Resolution (1973); détente (SALT I, Helsinki 1975, SALT II 1979) and Nixon’s 1972 China visit.
- Who won? In each case outcomes were mixed — status-quo results in Korea, a both-won/both-lost CMC, and in Vietnam a costly US failure but a strategic win for the North.

### Case studies

#### Division of Korea and two rival regimes (1945–1949)

After Japanese colonial rule, Korea was hastily divided at the 38th Parallel and two hostile regimes emerged from the Soviet and US occupation zones.

- Aug 1945: US proposes the 38th Parallel as a hasty, arbitrary line of occupation; Stalin agrees
- Nov 1947: UN Temporary Commission; North/USSR refuse to participate; elections in the South only
- Aug 1948 ROK (Syngman Rhee) and Sep 1948 DPRK (Kim Il-sung) established
- Soviet troops withdraw Dec 1948, US troops Jun 1949; periodic border raids from 1949 verge on civil war
- US Joint Chiefs (1947): “little strategic interest” in Korean bases; Stalin willing to stop at the 38th Parallel

**Why it matters:** Shows the war’s local roots (both leaders sought reunification by force) and that Korea was not a priority for any external power before 1949 — key for “civil war vs Cold War” questions.

#### Vietnamese independence and the First Indochina War (1945–1954)

Ho Chi Minh’s Viet Minh declared independence in 1945, the US declined to back it, and the Viet Minh fought France until the decisive defeat of the French at Dien Bien Phu.

- 2 Sep 1945: Ho proclaims independence at Ba Dinh Square, echoing the American Declaration of Independence
- US shelved support after Roosevelt’s death (12 Apr 1945) because it needed France and Britain as bulwarks in Europe
- War sparked 20 Nov 1946 at Haiphong; Mar 1949 French install Bao Dai as Head of State
- By 1954 the US paid roughly three-quarters of the French war effort (~$3 billion, 1950–54); China sent advisors and arms from 1950; Stalin ambivalent
- Giap’s attacks on Dien Bien Phu began 13 Mar 1954; French effort all but lost by 7 May

**Why it matters:** Supports the continuity argument that the conflict was fundamentally decolonisation, yet the US viewed the nationalist Viet Minh in Cold War terms — so even the First Indochina War could not be extricated from the Cold War.

#### China’s emergence as a communist power and NSC-68 (1949–1950)

The CCP’s victory and the Sino-Soviet Treaty fed the US “loss of China” narrative and NSC-68’s call for massive militarised containment.

- Mao proclaimed the PRC on 1 Oct 1949; Nationalists retreated to Taiwan
- Sino-Soviet Treaty, 14 Feb 1950: $300 million Soviet credit over 5 years, Soviet specialists — product of Mao’s “leaning to one side”
- “Loss of China” fuelled Republican attacks and McCarthyism; NYT called the PRC a Soviet “satellite”
- NSC-68 (7 Apr 1950, led by Paul Nitze): USSR “animated by a new fanatic faith”; recommended massive conventional/nuclear build-up, initially opposed by Kennan
- The Korean invasion settled the debate: US defence spending 5% → 14.2% of GDP, 1950–53

**Why it matters:** The precondition in Asia: it explains US fear of monolithic communism and why Korea converted containment from words into a rearmament programme (link to Topic 1 Long Telegram/Truman Doctrine).

#### Outbreak of the Korean War (25 Jun 1950)

North Korea invaded the South with Stalin’s approval and Mao’s support, after Kim persuaded Stalin the US would not intervene.

- Kim rebuffed in Moscow Mar 1949; asked Stalin three times; formal Soviet support Apr 1950, Chinese Jun 1950
- Acheson’s “Defence Perimeter” speech (12 Jan 1950) did not explicitly include Korea
- Soviet aid more than tripled 1949–50: 258 T-34 tanks, 178 warplanes, 1,600 artillery pieces; Stalin: “If you should get kicked in the teeth, I shall not lift a finger”
- Invasion spearheaded by 90,000 troops and 150 T-34 tanks; ~50% of troops crossing had Chinese Civil War training
- Khrushchev: “the war wasn’t Stalin’s idea, but Kim Il-sung’s. Kim was the initiator”

**Why it matters:** Core of the 2024 SBCS (outbreak of war in Korea, Jun 1950): assign relative responsibility — Kim initiator, Stalin enabler (Lee: a “critical step towards full-scale war”), Mao enabler via training, USA indirect via ambiguity.

#### Phase 1: US and UN intervention (25 Jun–15 Sep 1950)

The UN Security Council, with the USSR absent, authorised assistance to the South, and Truman committed US forces under a UN Command led by MacArthur.

- Resolution passed because Stalin had walked out of the UN over its refusal to seat the PRC
- UN Command (Jul 1950) under MacArthur with forces from 17 nations
- Motives: unprovoked aggression, US “prestige” after the “loss” of China (Acheson), McCarthyism and Nov 1950 Congressional elections
- By early Aug 1950 the North held almost the whole peninsula except the Pusan Perimeter

**Why it matters:** Marks the true internationalisation of the Cold War; directly answers the 2024 Part (b) on whether the US went to war mainly to protect South Korea (vs prestige, domestic politics, containment).

#### Phase 2: Inchon and rollback to the Yalu (15 Sep–24 Nov 1950)

MacArthur’s Inchon landings outflanked the North and Truman authorised crossing the 38th Parallel, shifting US aims from containment to rollback.

- 15 Sep 1950 Inchon landings — “the most dramatic and fluid phase of the conflict” (Edwards); Seoul recaptured within two weeks
- Joint Chiefs had reservations about Inchon but MacArthur insisted
- 7 Oct 1950 UN troops cross the 38th Parallel and push toward the Yalu on erroneous intelligence that the PRC/USSR would not intervene

**Why it matters:** Represents the intensification (not just internationalisation) of the Cold War — US aims became removing communism from Asia; a key US responsibility point in the phase-responsibility table.

#### Phase 3: Chinese intervention (25 Oct 1950–Jan 1951)

Chinese “Volunteers” crossed the Yalu and launched a massive counter-offensive, forcing the UN into retreat and creating a direct US–China confrontation.

- Chinese troops crossed the Yalu 25 Oct 1950; ~300,000 counter-attacked two days after MacArthur’s 24 Nov “end of war” offensive
- China deployed 2.3 million troops over the war, suffering 360,000 casualties
- Motives: security (S.J. Ball: China felt “its own revolution was threatened”), resentment over the Seventh Fleet in the Formosa Strait, reassurance from the Soviet A-bomb
- USSR refused to send troops, offering only limited air cover

**Why it matters:** China’s entry turned the tide and entrenched the international dimension; central to the “was Korea a proxy war?” debate.

#### Phase 4: Stalemate, MacArthur’s dismissal and the armistice (Jan 1951–27 Jul 1953)

The front stabilised near the 38th Parallel; Truman chose limited war and sacked MacArthur, and an armistice followed Eisenhower’s election and Stalin’s death.

- Seoul changed hands for the last time on 7 Mar 1951 under Gen. Ridgway
- 30 Nov 1950 Truman refused to rule out nuclear weapons; Attlee flew to Washington to urge caution
- MacArthur’s letter read in Congress — “there is no substitute for victory”; dismissed Apr 1951
- Eisenhower threatened to unleash Chiang and hinted at nuclear escalation; Stalin died Mar 1953; POW issue settled by voluntary repatriation
- Armistice signed at Panmunjom, 27 Jul 1953, close to the original 38th Parallel division

**Why it matters:** Evidence of superpower restraint (limited war by limited means) and of local actors losing the initiative; supports a status-quo, no-clear-winner verdict.

#### Geneva Accords, SEATO and the domino theory (1954)

Geneva partitioned Vietnam pending 1956 elections; the US refused to be bound and extended containment to Southeast Asia through SEATO and domino logic.

- Accords signed 20 Jul 1954: partition at the 17th Parallel, elections in July 1956, no new foreign alliances or bases
- USA and South Vietnam “acknowledged” but did not sign; Viet Minh signed under Soviet and Chinese pressure
- Eisenhower: the US “had not itself been party to or bound by the decisions taken” at Geneva
- SEATO (Sep 1954): USA, France, Britain, Australia, New Zealand, Thailand, Pakistan, the Philippines; Indochina protected by protocol
- Eisenhower, Apr 1954: the “falling domino” principle

**Why it matters:** Shows Vietnam was shaped by superpower interests at the outset (Viet Minh pressured by sponsors) and provides the Cold War logic for US support of a non-communist dictator in the South.

#### Ngo Dinh Diem’s regime and Kennedy’s escalation (1955–Nov 1963)

US-backed Diem built an unpopular, nepotistic dictatorship; Kennedy poured in advisors, then allowed a coup in which Diem was killed.

- 1955: Diem refused talks on the 1956 elections; won a fraudulent 98.2% referendum (600,000 votes from a district with 450,000 eligible voters)
- US aid 1955–60 almost $7 billion; advisors rose from 875 (1961) to 16,623 (1963)
- Strategic Hamlets (1962), modelled on the Briggs Plan, resented and corrupt
- 11 Jun 1963: Thich Quang Duc’s self-immolation in Saigon
- 1 Nov 1963 coup — Diem and his brother murdered; JFK assassinated 22 Nov 1963

**Why it matters:** Supports the argument that South Vietnamese weakness pushed non-communists toward the NLF (Ruane) and that US credibility made it more, not less, entrenched — a mutually-reinforcing US–RVN relationship.

#### Cuban Revolution and Cuba’s turn to the USSR (1953–1960)

Castro overthrew the US-backed Batista; his reforms and the US response produced an action–reaction spiral ending in a formal Cuban alignment with the USSR.

- By 1959 US firms owned ~90% of Cuban telephone/electric services and took 90% of sugar/tobacco exports
- Batista fled 1 Jan 1959; Castro arrived in Havana 9 Jan
- First Agrarian Reform Law (May 1959) removed ~40% of arable land from foreign/corporate hands
- Feb 1960 Mikoyan visit: $100 million Soviet credit, sugar-for-oil deal
- Eisenhower cut sugar purchases by 95%; Cuba seized $1 billion in US assets; Sep 1960 “bear hug” with Khrushchev at the UN

**Why it matters:** Edwards: “The origins of the missile crisis lie in the Cuban Revolution.” Useful for the 2021 SBCS on origins and for arguing persistent US exploitation provoked the Crisis.

#### North Vietnam’s turn to armed struggle (1959–1961)

After restraint in 1954–58, Hanoi declared a “people’s war” in 1959, built the Ho Chi Minh Trail and founded the NLF/Viet Cong.

- 1954–58: DRV focused on nation-building; Khrushchev pushed peaceful co-existence and in 1957 proposed admitting both Vietnams to the UN
- March 1959: Ho declared a ‘people’s war’ to unite Vietnam, driven by Diem’s oppression and Southern revolutionaries’ need for help
- Dec 1960: NLF founded; Viet Cong (1961) ~16,000 troops and ~200,000 informal volunteers by end-1961

**Why it matters:** Shows local initiative (Duiker) rather than Moscow/Beijing direction — supports the nationalist/civil-war reading of the conflict’s emergence.

#### Bay of Pigs and Operation Mongoose (Apr 1961–Oct 1962)

A failed CIA-organised exile invasion and a covert sabotage campaign convinced Castro the US meant to overthrow him and pushed Havana closer to Moscow.

- 17 Apr 1961: ~1,400 Cuban exiles; 114 killed, over 1,100 captured within a day
- Kennedy’s last-minute refusal of US air support among the failures
- Edwards: it “unintentionally solidified Castro’s regime”
- Mongoose (Nov 1961–1962): propaganda, sabotage, assassination plots; suspended Oct 1962

**Why it matters:** Key evidence that the Crisis was provoked by US actions — Kennedy had a limited direct role in the outbreak but his administration’s policies set the stage (action–reaction).

#### Khrushchev’s decision and Operation Anadyr (May–Oct 1962)

Khrushchev decided to deploy MRBMs/IRBMs to Cuba in secret, for strategic, prestige and ideological reasons.

- Missile gap bluff (“like sausages”) exposed Feb 1961; Cuban missiles would roughly triple Soviet strike capability and answer Jupiters in Turkey
- Berlin failure — Mason: “What began in Berlin in 1958 ended in Cuba in 1962”
- Operation Anadyr: ~43,000 Soviet personnel, July–October 1962, to be revealed after the Nov 1962 midterms
- Most Western historians (Sewell, Ball, Naftali, Blight) say the initiative was Khrushchev’s, persuading a reluctant Castro

**Why it matters:** Supports Soviet responsibility/Khrushchev’s insecurity for the development phase; balance with Cuba’s genuine fear of invasion and Khrushchev’s ideological romanticism.

#### The Thirteen Days: discovery, blockade and the deal (14–28 Oct 1962)

U-2 photos revealed the missiles; Kennedy chose a naval quarantine, and after two Khrushchev letters and a U-2 shootdown, a backchannel deal ended the Crisis.

- U-2 (Maj. Richard Heyser) photographed sites; ExComm convened 16 Oct
- 22 Oct blockade announced; DEFCON 3 (SAC to DEFCON 2); 24 Oct Rusk: “the other fellow just blinked”
- 26 Oct first letter (non-invasion pledge); 27 Oct second letter (Jupiters in Turkey) and a U-2 shot down
- RFK–Dobrynin backchannel: public non-invasion pledge + quiet Jupiter withdrawal; Khrushchev accepted 28 Oct via Radio Moscow
- Castro not consulted — furious at Cuba being treated as an “insignificant island”; last Jupiters left Turkey Apr 1963

**Why it matters:** Both leaders bear responsibility for ending it (willingness to negotiate, fear of accidental escalation); Sewell’s three names show different perspectives. Basis for the “who won?” debate (Dobbs: it guaranteed Castro’s hold on power).

#### Americanisation: Gulf of Tonkin, Rolling Thunder and the ground war (Aug 1964–1968)

The Gulf of Tonkin Resolution gave Johnson a “blank cheque”; sustained bombing and a ground war of Search-and-Destroy followed.

- Maddox and Turner Joy reported attacks 2 and 4 Aug 1964; Resolution 7 Aug — “all necessary measures,” no declaration of war
- Rolling Thunder from 2 Mar 1965: 864,000 tons of bombs by end-1967, more than the whole Korean War (653,000 tons)
- Infiltration south rose from 35,000 to 90,000 despite the bombing
- US forces ~80,000 (1965) to a peak of 543,000 (1969); body count the measure of progress
- Operation Ranch Hand sprayed over 20 million gallons of herbicides (incl. Agent Orange)

**Why it matters:** Responsibility for Americanisation lies with the US foreign-policy framework, policy inertia and Johnson (Logevall: haunted by Munich); shows US conventional tactics ill-suited to guerrilla war.

#### Chinese and Soviet aid and Sino-Soviet competition for Hanoi (1955–1975)

Beijing and Moscow both backed the DRV for ideological and strategic reasons, increasingly as rivals competing for Hanoi’s allegiance after the split.

- 1955: Chinese $200 million grant (18 projects); Soviet $100 million grant (23 projects)
- Over 320,000 Chinese troops deployed in North Vietnam 1965–71 (170,000 in 1965)
- Feb 1965 Soviet defence treaty; by late 1960s over three-quarters of DRV military equipment Soviet
- Deng reportedly offered 1 billion yuan if Hanoi refused Soviet aid; PRC rejected Kosygin’s ‘united action’ (Feb 1965)
- 1971–75: average 41.5% of China’s aid budget went to the DRV (48.67% in 1972)

**Why it matters:** Supports reading Vietnam as a proxy war and as an arena of Sino-Soviet rivalry where competition sometimes mattered more than solidarity with Hanoi.

#### Tet Offensive and My Lai (Jan–Mar 1968)

A nationwide communist offensive failed militarily but shattered US claims of progress, while the My Lai massacre undermined the war’s morality.

- Attacks on over 100 cities and outposts; 36 of 44 provincial capitals captured; Hue held three weeks; US Embassy compound breached
- Communists lost 60,000 lives and no uprising materialised — yet it was LBJ’s political “death knell”
- Cronkite (Feb 1968): war “mired in stalemate”; 31 Mar 1968 LBJ limited bombing and would not seek re-election
- My Lai (16 Mar 1968): as many as 504 unarmed villagers killed by Charlie Company (Lt. Calley); exposed by Seymour Hersh Nov 1969

**Why it matters:** Illustrates why “victory” in Vietnam was measured psychologically and by public opinion; a key turning point and evidence for domestic factors driving US withdrawal.

#### Nixon Doctrine, Vietnamisation and expanded bombing (1969–1972)

Nixon withdrew US ground troops and shifted the fighting to the ARVN while widening the bombing and using triangular diplomacy to pressure Hanoi.

- Nixon Doctrine (1969): aid rather than ground troops
- US troops 540,000 (1968) to 24,000 (1972); critics: ‘changing the colour of the corpses’
- Bombing expanded into Cambodia (1970) and Laos (1971); mining of Haiphong (May 1972) and Christmas bombing (Dec 1972)
- Anti-war movement: 100,000+ marched in New York (Apr 1967); Black Americans almost 20% of combat deaths (1961–66)
- US exploited the Sino-Soviet split; the USSR held the 1972 summit even as bombing of Haiphong resumed

**Why it matters:** Explains reasons for US withdrawal (military failure, domestic legitimacy collapse, media, triangular diplomacy) — by the late 1960s domestic priorities outweighed Cold War calculations.

#### Paris Peace Accords and the Fall of Saigon (27 Jan 1973–30 Apr 1975)

Kissinger’s diplomacy produced a US exit in 1973 with no enforcement; North Vietnam invaded again in 1975 and took Saigon, reunifying Vietnam.

- Kissinger leaned on Zhou Enlai and Gromyko while talking secretly with Le Duc Tho
- 23 Jan 1973 Nixon announced “peace with honour”; Accords signed four days later, to Thieu’s dismay
- Mar 1975 NVA invasion; War Powers Act meant no US airstrikes
- 30 Apr 1975 tanks entered Saigon; reunified by 1976, Saigon renamed Ho Chi Minh City
- Wider dominoes did not fall; Vietnam Syndrome; War Powers Resolution (1973)

**Why it matters:** Shows the end was shaped by Hanoi’s reliance on sponsors and their desire for better US relations (détente, Sino-US rapprochement); the North’s strategic win vs a belated, costly US exit.

### Historians

- **Stueck (2002):** Korea was “quite possibly the most important event since World War II” — a substitute for World War III that stabilised yet intensified and militarised the Cold War.
- **Noam Chomsky (2012):** The phrase “the loss of China” is revealing: “It is only possible to lose something that one owns.”
- **Lee:** Stalin’s support of Kim was a critical step towards full-scale war, though Stalin did not correctly anticipate its impact.
- **S.J. Ball:** China intervened in Korea because it felt its own revolution was threatened, not out of concern for the Korean revolution; Cuba was chosen to demonstrate Soviet power rather than for the Cuban revolutionaries’ good.
- **Edwards (2002):** The origins of the missile crisis lie in the Cuban Revolution; the Bay of Pigs solidified Castro’s regime; the Crisis was “a decisive event” that brought home the dangers of nuclear brinkmanship.
- **Sewell (2002):** The Crisis meant different things to each player — the ‘Cuban Missile’, ‘Caribbean’ and ‘October’ Crisis — and for Cubans was part of a longer crisis over the revolution’s fate.
- **Sewell, Ball, Naftali, Blight:** The initiative for placing missiles in Cuba came from Khrushchev, who had to persuade a reluctant Castro.
- **Mason (1996, 2001, 2003):** “What began in Berlin in 1958 ended in Cuba in 1962”; the CMC contrasted with the near-absence of arms control in 1957–62; the US lost Vietnam despite overwhelming resources, as Hanoi could sustain 10:1 losses.
- **Michael Dobbs:** The Crisis “guaranteed Castro’s hold on power… for more than four decades.”
- **Best et al. (2012):** The war’s origins lay in the Vietnamese struggle against French colonial rule; Diem’s nepotism and refusal of reform worsened his position.
- **Logevall (2001):** Johnson, haunted by Munich, would not reward ‘aggression’ in Vietnam with ‘appeasement’; the French showed scant interest in Indochinese political institutions.
- **Kevin Ruane (2000):** US aid gave Diem the means to protect a dictatorship, and his contempt for democracy and corruption pushed non-communists into the NLF/Viet Cong orbit.
- **Duiker (1993):** In early 1959 Hanoi’s leaders decided revolutionary victory in the South required violent struggle, with the masses’ political force supported by armed forces.
- **Truong Nhu Tang (1985):** The Party moved toward alliance with the Soviets from 1969, and by 1974 the pro-Soviet faction of Le Duan and Le Duc Tho had won.

## P1 Theme I · End of the Cold War

*How did the Cold War develop? How did the Cold War impact global developments after 1945? Guiding questions: What is the end of the Cold War and when did it end? What were the reasons for, features and impact of Reagan’s revived containment? What declined in the USSR, when and why? How did the 1980s Eastern European movements lead to the end of the Cold War? Who or what was responsible, according to the different historical interpretations?*

The end of the Cold War was a process through the 1980s, not a single event. It culminated in the Malta Declaration (Dec 1989), and it combined top-down change (Reagan and Gorbachev coming together) with bottom-up pressure from the people. Keep it distinct from the collapse of the USSR (Dec 1991). The two were parallel processes with similar roots, but the end of the Cold War did not require the USSR to break up.

The topic follows the periodisation 2nd Cold War (1979–85) → New Détente (1985–91). After the limits of détente and Soviet provocation (Afghanistan, SS-20s), Reagan revived containment: a military build-up, INF deployment, SDI, the Reagan Doctrine, economic pressure and propaganda. The Reagan Victory School claims this bankrupted the USSR. Its critics point out that there is no direct evidence the Soviets bent under this pressure before 1985.

From 1985 Gorbachev’s New Thinking, perestroika and glasnost drove the shift to cooperation. They were rooted in long-term Soviet economic decline that started well before Reagan. The results were summits, the INF Treaty, unilateral cuts and the Sinatra Doctrine. Reagan sustained the process but was largely reacting to Gorbachev. In 1989 popular movements in Poland and East Germany used the space Gorbachev had opened to end communist rule and tear down the Iron Curtain.

The notes’ synthesis is that no single factor works on its own. US pressure interacted with existing Soviet weaknesses. Gorbachev’s agency turned those weaknesses into concrete policy shifts, and those shifts created the political space that popular movements exploited. The August 1991 coup and Yeltsin’s takeover then brought about the USSR’s dissolution, which Gorbachev never intended.

**USA / Reagan: renewed containment (1981–85) and accommodation (1985–89)**

- Reasons for renewed containment: limits of détente (a ‘gentleman’s agreement’, seen as a façade), provocative Soviet actions (Helsinki violations, ICBM build-up, Afghanistan 1979) and the need to restore American prestige after Vietnam, Watergate and Iran.
- Features: the military build-up ($171bn → $376bn, 1981–86), INF deployment and the Zero Option, SDI (Mar 1983), the Reagan Doctrine (6 Feb 1985), economic containment through oil prices, and the propaganda war (‘Evil Empire’).
- Reasons for the shift to cooperation after 1985: re-election politics (against Mondale), Able Archer 83, and personalities (Shultz, the meeting with Gromyko in 1984, rapport with Gorbachev at Geneva in 1985).
- Verdict: any explanation must involve Reagan, but in a qualified way. He mattered less in starting the process and was crucial in sustaining it. He also let it finish by staying a largely passive observer of Eastern Europe.

**USSR / Gorbachev: decline and shifts in Soviet foreign policy**

- Long-term causes of decline (1945–84): the structure and make-up of the USSR, inefficiencies of the planned economy, weak policies under Khrushchev and Brezhnev, the nomenklatura social divide, and external costs (defence at 15–17% of GNP, the cost of empire).
- Economic problems began before 1985 (from the 1960s), yet Soviet foreign policy stayed confrontational until Gorbachev. Economic decline alone did not cause the shift.
- Perestroika and glasnost made the decline worse. They brought ethnic factors to the fore and destroyed authority without offering an alternative (Hosking, Hobsbawn).
- New Thinking: confrontation is counter-productive, security must be shared, all states are interdependent, and the USSR is a ‘normal state’. The Sinatra Doctrine replaced the Brezhnev Doctrine.
- Gorbachev was highly regarded abroad but despised and isolated at home. His well-intentioned reforms had unintended outcomes he could not manage.

**The People: Eastern European movements and revolutions**

- Causes: self-determination denied since WWII (earlier uprisings in 1953, 1956, 1968 and 1980), disgust at oppressive communist rule that had lost its moral legitimacy, and failure to deliver living standards.
- The movements exploited the opportunities created by glasnost and multi-party elections. When Gorbachev chose not to suppress them, they grew bolder and spread.
- Case studies: Poland (Solidarity) and East Germany (Leipzig, the fall of the Berlin Wall).
- People Power alone is not enough. Gorbachev’s repudiation of the Brezhnev Doctrine removed the key lever of repression, and the US contribution “lay mainly in what it did not do.”

**End of the Cold War vs. collapse of the USSR**

- End of the Cold War: 1989 (Malta, Dec 1989), a mutual relaxation of tensions and the end of Europe’s bloc division.
- Collapse of the USSR: Dec 1991, the end of the unified state, of CPSU rule and of Gorbachev’s leadership. It was largely an outcome of the end of the Cold War, not its cause.
- The break-up removed the USA’s object of containment and gave the Cold War its definitive end.
- Ending the Cold War was desirable for Gorbachev, but the USSR’s collapse was not. He hoped ending the Cold War would keep the USSR going.

### Case studies

#### Long-term Soviet economic decline (1945–84)

The USSR suffered structural weaknesses: too many forcibly incorporated republics, an inefficient command economy, weak leadership and the costs of empire. Together these produced an ‘invisible crisis’ behind a mask of stability.

- Industrial output growth fell from an estimated 5.25% (1967) to 2% by 1980 (Lightbody, 1999).
- The average worker earned 195 roubles/month vs. ≈750 for the average nomenklatura member (≈19m CPSU members).
- Defence spending rose from 12–14% of GNP (1965) to 15–17% (1985), with some estimates as high as 25%, vs. 6–7% in the US (Powaski, 1997).
- Third World subsidies rose from ≈1% to ≈3% of GNP (1971–80). Eastern Europe became a net cost by the 1970s (Lundestad, 2003).
- Russians made up just over 50% of the population. Coleman (1996): “the rot began at the border.”

**Why it matters:** This is the core evidence against the Reagan Victory School: the weaknesses came before Reagan, so at most he made them worse. It also shows the limits of a purely economic explanation, since decline from the 1960s did not produce a timely shift in foreign policy. Useful for the 2025 SBCS question on Soviet economic problems.

#### Collapse of détente and Soviet provocation (1975–80)

Détente was seen as a façade that froze the Cold War in place. Soviet human-rights violations after Helsinki, the covert arms build-up and the invasion of Afghanistan, together with US humiliation in Iran, produced the 2nd Cold War.

- The Helsinki Final Act/CSCE (1975) was seen as the climax of détente. The USSR violated its human-rights promises.
- SALT I and the ABM Treaty (1972) tried to cap the build-up. SALT II (1979) collapsed when the USSR invaded Afghanistan.
- Carter Doctrine (State of the Union, 23 Jan 1980): an outside attempt to control the Persian Gulf would be “repelled by any means necessary, including military force.”
- Reagan on détente: “Isn’t that what a farmer has with his turkey—until Thanksgiving Day?” (Gaddis, 2006).
- Gaddis: the Tehran embassy seizure, followed by Afghanistan, made it seem “Washington was on the defensive everywhere, and Moscow was on a roll.”

**Why it matters:** Explains why renewed containment happened. Use it for the offensive vs. defensive debate (US aggression or a response to Soviet provocation). Linked to the 2019 SBCS topic, End of Détente and Return to Confrontation.

#### Poland: Solidarity, martial law and stalemate (1980–88)

Economic collapse sparked mass strikes. These produced Solidarity, the first independent trade union in the communist world. Jaruzelski crushed it under martial law (Dec 1981), but the regime could not reform.

- Growth of national income produced fell from 9% (1970–75) to –5.4% (1980). External debt reached $23bn by 1980.
- Jul–Aug 1980: strikes involving 300,000 workers after ≈100% food-price rises. On 31 Aug the Gdańsk shipyard accepted Solidarność, led by Lech Wałęsa.
- Solidarity was formally founded on 22 Sep 1980 and grew to ≈10 million members using peaceful strikes and rallies.
- Martial law was declared on 12 Dec 1981 (Gen. Jaruzelski) and Solidarity was banned in Oct 1982. Reagan imposed sanctions on Poland and the USSR. The CIA covertly funded Solidarity.
- 1982–88: political stalemate. New strikes in 1988 demanded that Solidarity be recognised.

**Why it matters:** Shows the deep roots of Eastern European discontent: Poland had “always been the most rebellious Warsaw Pact member” (Levesque, 2010). Also an example of the Reagan Doctrine in Europe. Solidarity’s survival broke with the crushing of protests in 1970, 1956 and 1968.

#### Reagan’s military build-up and INF deployment (1979–Nov 1983)

Reagan massively raised defence spending. The NATO Double-Track Decision led to Pershing II and Cruise missiles being deployed in Europe after arms talks deadlocked over Soviet SS-20s.

- US defence budget: $171bn (1981) → $376bn (1986). The Navy grew from 454 to a planned 600 ships. The B-1 was revived and the MX and Trident were accelerated.
- Soviet SS-20s (from 1976) → NATO Double-Track Decision (Dec 1979): negotiate, or modernise INF.
- Zero Option (Nov 1981): NATO would forgo INF if the USSR dismantled its SS-4/5/20s. The USSR rejected it.
- The ‘walk in the woods’ (Nitze–Kvitsinsky, 1982) failed. Talks ended when Pershing IIs arrived in West Germany (Nov 1983).
- Renewed fear of nuclear war fuelled Western anti-nuclear peace movements.

**Why it matters:** The central evidence for the Reagan Victory thesis: the USSR had to raise defence spending from 22% to 27% of GDP (1980–85). It also shows the escalation and deadlock that critics use to argue the Soviets were not ‘bending’.

#### Economic containment and the propaganda war (1982–84)

Reagan put economic pressure on the USSR through oil prices and the pipeline. He also stepped up moral rhetoric that framed the Cold War as a spiritual struggle.

- The USA persuaded Saudi Arabia to raise oil output, depressing prices and Soviet export revenue (the USSR was a net oil exporter).
- Reagan tried, and failed, to make Western Europe abandon the gas-oil pipeline deal with the USSR.
- June 1982 speech to the British Parliament: called for fostering “the infrastructure of democracy”.
- March 1983 ‘Evil Empire’ speech: the struggle was “a spiritual one… a test of moral will and faith”.
- Aug 1984: rejected the “permanent subjugation of the people of Eastern Europe”.

**Why it matters:** Supports the claim that Reagan, through the war of rhetoric, persuaded the USSR that further confrontation was fruitless. Critics counter that Soviet resolve stiffened: Pravda warned of “catastrophe”.

#### Strategic Defense Initiative (SDI, ‘Star Wars’) (23 Mar 1983)

Reagan announced a 5-year, $26bn programme for a nationwide ballistic-missile defence system, three weeks after the ‘Evil Empire’ speech. The USSR saw it as offensive.

- It was preceded by NSDD-12 (early 1981), which called for “vigorous research and development” on BMD.
- Reagan aimed to render nuclear weapons “impotent and obsolete”. Dobrynin replied: “you will be opening a new phase in the arms race.”
- Reykjavik (1986) collapsed over SDI. The INF Treaty (1987) came only once Gorbachev agreed to negotiate SDI separately.
- Gorbachev later said: “they’re pursuing this program to wear us out.” A 1987 Soviet Energia/laser-missile launch to counter it failed.
- Its funding plummeted in the early 1990s, and under Clinton it was renamed the BMDO.

**Why it matters:** Hastened the end of the Cold War by straining Soviet resources. However, it also blocked negotiations until it was left out of the talks, so it was one of many factors, not the sole cause. Mitterrand and other allies feared it undermined MAD.

#### Able Archer 83 and the KAL shoot-down (1983)

In 1983 tension peaked. The Kremlin briefly believed a NATO nuclear attack was imminent, and a Korean airliner was shot down. These events convinced Reagan he had pushed far enough.

- Gaddis (2007): Able Archer led Andropov and his aides “to conclude—briefly—that a nuclear attack was imminent”, “probably the most dangerous moment since the Cuban Missile Crisis.”
- The 1983 Korean Airlines shoot-down (269 dead) reflected Kremlin panic, not capitulation (Gaddis).
- Reagan faced re-election in 1984 against Walter Mondale, who promised arms talks within six months.
- Autumn 1984: Reagan’s first meeting with Gromyko moderated rhetoric on both sides.

**Why it matters:** A key reason for Reagan’s shift to cooperation. It is also evidence against the Reagan Victory School: Soviet resolve stiffened rather than crumbled.

#### The Reagan Doctrine (6 Feb 1985 (Afghan aid 1979–89))

Reagan replaced containment with ‘roll-back’. The US gave overt and covert support to anti-communist guerrillas fighting Soviet-backed governments in the Third World and Europe.

- Reagan: “Support for freedom fighters is self-defense” (State of the Union, 1985). In 1977 he had said: “we win and they lose.”
- Afghanistan: the CIA sent $2bn+ to the Mujahedeen (1979–89) via Pakistan’s ISI. The programme began under Carter’s NSA Brzezinski.
- Nicaragua: the US backed the contras, partly funded through Iran-Contra. The ICJ (1984) ruled against the US, and the US blocked enforcement with its UNSC veto.
- Angola: the US backed Savimbi’s UNITA, and a 1989 agreement removed Soviet and Cuban troops. Cambodia: covert aid to the KPNLK.
- US covert Mujahedeen support (≈$1bn/yr) cost the Soviets roughly eight times as much.

**Why it matters:** Thatcher credited it with helping to end the Cold War. It drained Soviet resources (the Afghan ‘bleeding wound’ cost ≈$20bn/yr). But it also over-extended the US, its objectives resembled earlier containment, and some of the forces it backed later turned against the USA.

#### Perestroika and glasnost (1985–91)

Gorbachev tried economic restructuring (perestroika, from 1986) and openness (glasnost). Both made the decline worse: shortages, inflation, ethnic nationalism, and the erosion of CPSU authority.

- May 1985: the anti-alcoholism campaign had a damaging socio-economic impact.
- Perestroika brought self-financing enterprises and the Yeltsin-Shatalin 500-Day Plan. It divided the CPSU (conservatives: too fast; reformers: too slow).
- White (2001): up to ¼ of the population lived below the poverty line by 1990. National income fell ≈15% in 1991.
- Hosking (1993): glasnost “thrust ethnic factors to the centre of the stage… The result was to destroy the Soviet Union.”
- Gorbachev mistakenly believed Eastern Europeans would choose ‘reformed communism’.

**Why it matters:** Shows Gorbachev as an agent whose reforms had unintended consequences. Central to ‘How far was Gorbachev responsible for the collapse of the USSR he did not intend?’ and to the 2025 SBCS (a) question on Gorbachev’s views.

#### Geneva and Reykjavik summits (1985–86)

Annual Reagan–Gorbachev summits began. Geneva built personal rapport. Reykjavik collapsed over SDI but was seen as a breakthrough towards the INF Treaty.

- Geneva (1985): Reagan and Gorbachev “had come to like and respect each other” (Farnham, 2001). Gorbachev: “I have come to seek a fresh start.”
- Reykjavik (1986) collapsed at the last minute over Reagan’s refusal to exclude SDI.
- George Shultz (Secretary of State from June 1982) held a more realistic view of nuclear parity (Powaski).
- Obstacles: Reagan wanted talks linked to human rights, Soviet Jewish emigration and Afghanistan, while Gorbachev wanted arms control only.

**Why it matters:** Supports the ‘Reagan sustained the process’ argument. Its limits are also clear: Reagan took years to warm up, and Schweitzer (2002) says “Reagan happened to be there when Gorbachev ended the Cold War.”

#### Gorbachev’s New Thinking and unilateral cuts (1985–89)

Gorbachev rejected the class approach and the idea that conflict was inevitable. He sought security through political means. The USSR disengaged from the Third World, cut forces unilaterally and made peace with China.

- Principles: confrontation is counter-productive, security must not come at another state’s expense, all states are interdependent, and the USSR is a ‘normal state’.
- Dec 1988: a 500,000-troop cut within 2 years plus 10,000 tanks removed from Europe, with no reciprocity expected.
- 1988 pledge to leave Afghanistan. Aid to Nicaragua, Cambodia, Angola and Ethiopia was scaled down. Rapprochement with China (1989) was the first Soviet visit since Khrushchev.
- Lightbody: Gorbachev “had broken the traditional suspicion of Soviet intentions.”
- Conservatives said he compromised superpower status by ‘kowtowing’ to Reagan and Deng (Roberts, 1994).

**Why it matters:** The strongest evidence for the Soviet Initiative School. Petersen and Brands link it to economic stagnation, which ties it to the economic-problems debate. It started a positive cycle of action and reaction.

#### INF Treaty (Dec 1987)

Signed at the Washington summit, it was the first treaty to eliminate an entire category of nuclear weapons. It came after Gorbachev accepted Reagan’s conditions.

- Gorbachev agreed to exclude SDI and British/French missiles, and to accept asymmetrical reductions and on-site verification.
- It banned all land-based missiles with a range of 1,000–5,500km, plus SRINF of 500–1,000km.
- The US dismantled ≈859 Pershing/Tomahawk missiles and the USSR ≈1,752.
- The US Senate ratified it at the Moscow summit (May 1988). Mason called it “a milestone… possible not merely to restrain but to reverse the arms race.”
- Strains on both leaders included the 1987 crash and Iran-Contra (Reagan), and the Mathias Rust incident and Yeltsin affair (Gorbachev).

**Why it matters:** Shows top-down convergence. Use it on both sides: Reagan’s intransigent conditions vs. Gorbachev’s concessions, which suggest the initiative was Soviet.

#### The Sinatra Doctrine (end of the Brezhnev Doctrine) (Dec 1988–Dec 1989)

Gorbachev allowed, and even encouraged, Eastern European states to choose their own governments, including non-communist ones. This removed the threat of Soviet military intervention.

- Dec 1988: Gorbachev told the UN that the Brezhnev Doctrine would not be enforced.
- Apr 1989: Gorbachev told Eastern European leaders they must govern without Soviet support.
- May 1989: Shevardnadze said he “could imagine no occasion in which Soviet troops might intervene in a Warsaw Pact country.”
- Gorbachev in Perestroika: “Let everyone make his own choice, and let us all respect that choice” (Gaddis, 2005).
- The Warsaw Pact formally denounced the Brezhnev Doctrine in Dec 1989 (Lundestad, 2003).

**Why it matters:** The necessary precondition for People Power, because the ‘floodgates opened’ when the Red Army did not intervene. Powaski: it “persuaded all but the most hard-line Westerners of Gorbachev’s determination to end the Cold War.”

#### Poland 1989: Round Table and Solidarity’s victory (Feb 1989–Dec 1990)

Jaruzelski brought Solidarity into Round Table Talks and agreed to partly free elections. Solidarity swept them, and the first non-communist government in postwar Eastern Europe followed.

- Feb 1989: Round Table Talks. Apr 1989: the government agreed to legalise Solidarity and allow free elections.
- Jun 1989: Solidarity won 99/100 Senate seats and all 161 contestable Sejm seats.
- Aug 1989: Mazowiecki became the first non-communist premier since the late 1940s.
- Dec 1990: Wałęsa was elected President.

**Why it matters:** The first revolutionary breakthrough. It triggered a chain reaction of largely peaceful revolutions in 1989, which ended Moscow-imposed regimes and contributed to the USSR’s 1991 dissolution. Linked to the 2022 SBCS topic, The Collapse of Communism in Eastern Europe.

#### East Germany: Leipzig demonstrations and the fall of the Berlin Wall (Sep–9 Nov 1989 (reunified 3 Oct 1990))

Honecker refused to reform. When Hungary opened its border, people fled, and church-based protests in Leipzig grew into mass demonstrations. Schabowski’s mistaken announcement then opened the Wall.

- GDR growth fell below 1% p.a. by the late 1980s. Over a fifth of government income went on subsidies. GNP and productivity were just 60% of West Germany’s (Haftendorn, 2011).
- Pastor Christian Führer held a weekly Friedensgebet at St. Nicholas Church (from 1982). Demonstrations began on 4 Sep 1989.
- Sep 1989: 130,000 East Germans had flooded into Hungary. 9 Oct, Leipzig: >70,000 chanted “Wir sind das Volk!” and the 8,000 police and military withdrew.
- 16 Oct: 120,000 protesters, and Honecker resigned 2 days later. 23 Oct: >320,000.
- 9 Nov 1989: Schabowski’s mistake opened the Wall, and over 2 million East Berliners crossed that weekend. Germany reunited on 3 Oct 1990.

**Why it matters:** Removing the Iron Curtain resolved one of the Cold War’s original sources of disagreement and directly enabled the Malta declaration. It is the key People Power evidence: Jarausch calls it “nonviolent civil resistance”. Mason notes that the GDR fell because it refused to reform.

#### Malta Summit and the post-1989 treaties (Dec 1989–1991)

Bush and Gorbachev declared the Cold War over at Malta. Bush continued Reagan’s work through annual summits and the CFE, Chemical Weapons and START I treaties.

- Malta (Dec 1989): no agreements were signed, but it is widely seen as the official end of the Cold War. A Soviet spokesman: “We bury the Cold War at the bottom of the Mediterranean sea.”
- CFE: reduced NATO and Warsaw Pact land forces in Europe and required excess equipment to be destroyed.
- Chemical Weapons Accord: stockpiles to be cut to ≤5,000 agent tons by end-2022, with on-site inspection.
- START I (Moscow, 1991): the largest arms treaty in history, removing ≈80% of strategic nuclear weapons. Obligations passed to 12 successor states.
- Bush aimed to preserve the USSR, offering a $1.1bn loan in 1991.

**Why it matters:** Marks the end of the Cold War (1989), as distinct from the USSR’s collapse (1991). It shows the US sustaining and cooperating, not driving the process.

#### The August Coup and Yeltsin’s takeover (19–21 Aug 1991)

Conservatives tried to stop the new Union Treaty by putting Gorbachev under house arrest in Crimea. Yeltsin defeated the coup and then took Gorbachev’s power: ‘two coups in one’.

- The plotters included VP Yanayev, PM Pavlov, Defence Minister Yazov and KGB chief Kryuchkov.
- Earlier, on 12 Jun 1991, Yeltsin had been elected President of Russia with 57.3% of the vote.
- Yeltsin stood on a tank outside the Russian White House, and the coup collapsed within 2 days.
- Yeltsin banned the CPSU and forced Gorbachev to resign as CPSU chief, ending central control from Moscow.
- Sturua: “The failed coup signalled not only the end of communism but the end of perestroika as well.”

**Why it matters:** Explains the collapse of the USSR as opposed to the end of the Cold War. The coup failed partly because of glasnost’s long-term effects, and it made Gorbachev look weak.

#### Dissolution of the USSR and Gorbachev’s resignation (Dec 1991)

The Minsk Declaration dissolved the USSR and created the CIS. Republics broke away, Gorbachev resigned, and the USSR ceased to exist in international law.

- 7 Dec 1991: the Minsk Declaration (Byelorussia, Russia, Ukraine) created the CIS.
- By 20 Dec 1991, 11/15 republics had broken away. The Baltic republics chose not to join the CIS.
- 25 Dec 1991: Gorbachev resigned as Executive President, his 2nd resignation in 4 months, and Yeltsin took over.
- Yeltsin let the USSR go to avoid bloodshed and economic burdens, judging Russia better off on its own.
- Garthoff: ‘victory’ came when Soviet leaders “realised how badly their system at home and their policies abroad had failed.”

**Why it matters:** Gave the Cold War its definitive end, since the US no longer had a USSR to contain. Use it for ‘one and the same process?’ essays and for weighing the roles of Gorbachev, Yeltsin, Reagan and the republics.

### Historians

- **Lundestad (2003):** The Reagan Victory School sees Reagan abandoning status-quo containment to seek the West’s ‘victory’. The Soviet collapse, the end of dictatorship and the move to markets were concurrent, interdependent processes, and Eastern Europe had become a net cost to the USSR.
- **Spanier (1990):** Détente was “essentially an adaptation of containment” to an era of strategic parity, the Sino-Soviet schism and US pseudo-isolationism.
- **Gaddis (2005/2006/2007):** Détente froze the Cold War, so only killing it could end the Cold War. Able Archer 83 was the most dangerous moment since Cuba and convinced Reagan he had pushed far enough. The Soviet system was a ‘sandpile ready to slide’, toppled by ordinary people.
- **Powaski (1997):** The US used a technology-driven arms race to bankrupt the Soviet economy. The Soviet crisis turned critical when it could not absorb technological innovation, and the end of the Brezhnev Doctrine persuaded the West of Gorbachev’s sincerity.
- **Schulzinger (1994):** The Cold War delayed badly needed domestic reforms and damaged the US economy.
- **Vadney (1998):** The arms race was less affordable for the USSR than for the USA.
- **Walker (1993):** The West prevailed because it could supply “guns as well as butter”.
- **Farnham (2001):** At Geneva, Reagan and Gorbachev came to like and respect each other despite Reagan’s doubts.
- **Lightbody (1999):** The Soviet economy was the most significant weakness, with industrial growth falling from 5.25% to 2% (1967–80). Gorbachev’s unilateral cuts broke the traditional suspicion of Soviet intentions.
- **Bredenkamp (1991):** Western assistance could play at most a supporting role in Soviet reform.
- **Schweitzer (2002):** “Reagan happened to be there when Gorbachev ended the Cold War.”
- **Hobsbawn (1999):** Nationalist fracture was built into the system. Glasnost destroyed authority and perestroika destroyed the old economic mechanisms without providing any alternative.
- **Coleman (1996):** “The rot began at the border.”
- **White (2001):** Under perestroika, shortages and poverty worsened, and national income fell ≈15% in 1991.
- **Hosking (1993):** Glasnost thrust ethnic factors to the centre and destroyed the Soviet Union.
- **Petersen (1993) / Brands (1993):** Stagnation and the burden of defence (15–17% of GNP), together with the failure of military action, prompted Gorbachev’s conclusion that only major reform could save the USSR.
- **Roberts (1994):** The loss of Eastern Europe and the reduction of Soviet military power drew hard-line criticism, including from within Gorbachev’s own administration.
- **Sturua (1990):** The failed coup signalled the end of both communism and perestroika.
- **Garthoff (cited in Lundestad, 2003):** The West did not win through containment or the Reagan build-up. ‘Victory’ came when new Soviet leaders realised their system had failed.
- **Archie Brown / Jacques Lévesque (1997, 2010):** Gorbachev deserves virtually all the credit because of his idealistic view of universal reconciliation, and Reagan’s hard line probably slowed the end. Poland was the first breakthrough of 1989.
- **Pemberton (1998):** Despite his ‘evil empire’ rhetoric, Reagan “joined Mikhail Gorbachev to bring the cold war to an end”. The Reagan Victory School downplays domestic Soviet factors.
- **Gates:** The end of the Cold War was the triumph of containment: denied new conquests, Soviet communism’s inherent weaknesses brought it down.
- **Mason:** Communism was always ‘imposed from without’, and the GDR fell because it refused to reform.
- **Haftendorn (2011):** The GDR went from the most successful socialist economy to near collapse, and the party’s legitimacy crumbled.
- **McCauley:** Gorbachev assented to the revolutions believing Eastern Europeans had chosen socialism, and he would not reimpose force even when he realised he had allowed too much.
- **Jarausch (2009):** The fall of the Wall was a ‘magical’, peaceful triumph of people’s power over a repressive regime.
- **Bacevich (2009):** Triumphalists believed history had reached a turning point and that the US alone would decide what came next.
- **Zubok (2022):** ‘The Soviet factor’ was crucial to the peaceful revolutions, and Gorbachev was ‘the inadvertent liberator’.
- **Bartel (2022):** The end of the Cold War was the moment people’s power peaked and was overcome. Communist dictatorships had to be pushed into free elections by mass protest.

### Using this topic in the exam

- SBCS past questions: 2019 End of Détente and Return to Confrontation; 2022 The Collapse of Communism in Eastern Europe; 2025 (a) compare sources on Gorbachev’s views on the Soviet situation, (b) how far Soviet economic problems were the reason for the end of the Cold War.
- Always separate the end of the Cold War (1989, Malta) from the collapse of the USSR (Dec 1991). They are parallel processes, not one event. This distinction is a common essay question in its own right.
- Weigh the three schools: Western Triumphalist (Reagan/USA), Soviet Initiative (Gorbachev) and People Power. Know each one’s context, authors, evidence and limitations. Pitfall: claiming any factor worked alone, because each operated within the others.
- Use the players × period matrix (1981–84, 1985–89, 1989–91) for the USA, the USSR and Eastern Europe to show change over time. Reagan mattered less in starting the process, was crucial in sustaining it, and was passive during 1989.
- On Soviet economic problems: they began before Reagan and Gorbachev (from the 1960s), yet foreign policy stayed confrontational until 1985. Gorbachev’s accession, Reagan’s more conciliatory 2nd term and fear of nuclear war were the more immediate triggers.
- Practice prompts: ‘primarily a Soviet, not an American, achievement’; Gorbachev and the collapse he did not intend; top-down vs. bottom-up; ‘one and the same process’.

## P1 Theme II · Global economy

*How and why did the global economy grow from 1945 to the early 1970s, and why did it face the ‘Crisis Decades’ from the late 1960s to 2000? How far were the USA, Western Europe/Japan, MNCs and international institutions responsible — and were the crises built into Golden Age growth or triggered by external shocks?*

The topic runs in four phases: postwar reconstruction (1945–1952), liberalisation and the ‘Golden Age of Capitalism’ (1952–late 1960s), a reversal of liberalisation as the US weakened (late 1960s–1973), and the ‘Crisis Decades’ (1970s–1990s). ‘Development’ is a neutral term covering both growth and crisis, and the relative importance of each factor changes from phase to phase.

From 1945–1952 the USA dominated, using aid (Marshall Plan, Japan occupation aid) and security guarantees (NATO, US–Japan Treaty) to rebuild Western Europe and Japan, driven by Cold War containment and the goal of a liberal, multilateral Pax Americana. This fed a Golden Age: GATT cut tariffs, the EPU, EEC and EFTA formed, MNCs expanded and Keynesian mixed economies delivered unprecedented growth.

Success bred its own undoing. By the late 1960s US relative dominance declined (deficits, Vietnam War costs, competition from a recovered Europe/Japan), undermining Bretton Woods. The Nixon Shock (1971), the oil shocks (1973, 1979), New Protectionism and the Third World Debt Crisis (1982) followed — and they hit developing countries far harder and for far longer than developed ones.

The best essays link the stories: the seeds of the 1970s crises (financial liberalisation, US overstretch, dollar dependence) were sown during the Golden Age itself, while cooperation persisted in altered forms (G7, WEF, GATT rounds, Plaza Accord, ERM) even through the protectionist years.

**Four-phase periodisation**

- 1945–1952: Postwar Economic Reconstruction — rebuilding war-devastated Europe & Japan with US aid.
- 1952–late 1960s: Postwar Economic Liberalisation (‘Golden Age of Capitalism’) — free trade, mixed economy, unprecedented growth.
- Late 1960s–1973: reversal/slowing of liberalisation — US decline begins; Bretton Woods under strain.
- 1970s–1990s: ‘Crisis Decades’ — oil shocks, stagflation, protectionism, debt crises.
- Golden Age vs Crisis: advanced-economy GDP growth ~5% (1963–73) vs 2.5% (1973–79) → 2.2% (90s); world export volume growth 8% p.a. (1960–73) vs 1.5% (80s).

**Factors of growth (1945–1973)**

- USA: aid, defence underwriting, market access and shaping the institutions (Atlantic Charter, Bretton Woods, GATT).
- Active government: Keynesian mixed economies, welfare states and national planning (Monnet Plan, MITI, Taiwan/South Korea plans).
- Western Europe/Japan: regional integration (EPU, EEC, EFTA, CAP) and Japan’s Flying Geese role in Asia; later aid donors and investors themselves.
- MNCs: FDI, lower costs, technology transfer, larger markets — by 1972, 57 of the world’s 100 largest economic entities.
- International institutions: Bretton Woods fixed rates, IMF, IBRD and GATT gave stability and cut trade barriers.

**Causes of the challenges (late 1960s–2000)**

- Structural flaws built into the Golden Age: Bretton Woods’ over-reliance on US dollar stability; foreign-held dollars exceeded US gold/forex reserves by 1961.
- US relative decline: Vietnam and ‘Great Society’ spending, shrinking trade surplus, competition from recovered Europe/Japan.
- Singular external triggers: the 1973 and 1979 oil shocks.
- Policy choices: financial liberalisation, Volcker Shock interest rates, New Protectionism, Third World overborrowing from commercial banks.

**Uneven impact**

- Developed economies could borrow cheaply and adapt (alternative energy, restructuring); developing economies took on expensive loans that trapped them for much of the 1980s.
- Japan largely shrugged off the problems plaguing the West; Western manufacturing heartlands were hit by Asian Tiger competition.
- In the 1980s Singapore (8.2%), Korea (7.1%), Taiwan (6.5%) and Hong Kong (5.3%) vastly outperformed the West.
- GATT tariff cuts mainly benefited manufactured trade between developed countries; protected industries were the ones developing countries needed access to.

**Causal chains to link sections**

- US aid & security → European/Japanese recovery → Golden Age liberalisation → relative US decline → Bretton Woods collapse.
- Bretton Woods collapse → financial liberalisation → petrodollar lending → Third World overborrowing → Debt Crisis.
- Oil crises → inflation/stagflation → Volcker Shock → spike in global interest rates → Debt Crisis.
- US decline + ineffective institutions + domestic lobbies → New Protectionism → harms developing-country commodity exporters.

### Case studies

#### Atlantic Charter and the Bretton Woods system (1941–1971)

Roosevelt and Churchill’s Atlantic Charter (14 Aug 1941) set out postwar aims; at Bretton Woods (July 1944) 44 nations created the IMF and IBRD and a system of fixed exchange rates pegged to a gold-backed US dollar.

- Atlantic Charter called for reduced trade restrictions; basis of the Declaration by United Nations (1 Jan 1942, 26 governments); inspired NATO and GATT.
- Structures created at Bretton Woods were essentially those the US proposed; the US held 3/4 of the world’s gold by 1947.
- Fully functional from 1958: currencies convertible, dollars convertible to gold at $35/oz.
- IMF Suez Crisis (1956) loans: $262m to France, $1.2bn to the UK; IBRD commitments $610m (1961) → $14.244bn (1994).
- 1948–68 non-communist merchandise exports +290%; but by 1961 foreign-held dollars already exceeded US gold/forex reserves (Brett, 1985).

**Why it matters:** Shows the USA shaping a multilateral order that gave the stability (investor confidence, FDI, trade) behind Golden Age growth — but its dependence on the dollar was a built-in fragility, supporting the argument that the 1970s crisis was sown during the Golden Age.

#### US Occupation of Japan and the Reverse Course (1945–1952)

Under Gen. MacArthur the US enacted sweeping reforms; from 1947/48 the ‘Reverse Course’ prioritised Japan’s economic rehabilitation over punitive demilitarisation out of fear of communism.

- US aid to Japan: $2.3 billion during the Occupation — ~15% of imports, ~4% of GNP (59% food).
- ~40% of Japan’s industrial plants/infrastructure destroyed; new factories used the best modern machines.
- US-sponsored land reform increased competition.
- 1953–65: GDP +9%/yr; manufacturing/mining +13%.
- ‘Japanese Miracle’ (1954–73): average growth >10%/yr; 3rd largest economy by 1970.

**Why it matters:** Supports the argument that Cold War US policy was the dominant factor in reconstruction (1945–52); motivated by fear that a weak Japanese economy would strengthen communism across East Asia.

#### Mixed economy and government-led planning (1946–1980s)

Believing laissez-faire had caused the Depression, governments adopted Keynesian demand management, welfare states and national growth plans, taking 25–33% of national income in Western Europe.

- Five objectives: full employment, full-capacity production, price stability, incomes rising with productivity, balance-of-payments equilibrium.
- UK Beveridge Report (1942) → welfare state by 1948; social spending +64% in France, +50% in UK (1938–49).
- France’s Monnet Plan (1946): growth 4.6% (1950–59), 5.8% (1960–69).
- Japan’s MITI ‘Inclined Production Mode’ and Ikeda’s ‘double income in 10 years’ (1960) — targets outstripped ahead of schedule.
- Taiwan & South Korea shifted ISI → EOI; Taiwan set up ITRI (1973) after the oil crisis.

**Why it matters:** Evidence that growth was state-driven, not just US- or market-driven; Keynesianism stayed the dominant framework until the early 1970s, when stagflation discredited it.

#### GATT rounds and the WTO (1947–1995)

GATT (signed 30 Oct 1947, 23 countries) cut tariffs through successive rounds on the principles of non-discrimination (MFN), reduced barriers and reciprocity; the WTO superseded it in 1995.

- Geneva (1947): 23 countries (80% of world trade); 45,000 tariff cuts, ~35% average.
- Kennedy Round (1962–67) anti-dumping agreement; Tokyo Round (1973–79) addressed non-tariff barriers; Uruguay Round (1986–93) created the WTO, $3.7 trillion trade.
- 1950s–60s world trade grew 8%/yr (Judt, 2005); W. Germany’s share of world manufactured exports 7.3% (1950) → 19.3% (1960).
- WTO (1995) added services and intellectual property and binding dispute resolution.
- Developing countries’ share of world trade ~1/4 (early 1970s) → 1/3 by the 1990s/2000s.

**Why it matters:** Key evidence for institutions driving growth; ironically, GATT’s success in cutting manufacturing tariffs pushed countries toward non-tariff barriers — setting up New Protectionism. Continued rounds show cooperation persisted in the Crisis Decades.

#### Marshall Plan (1948–1952)

The US gave $13 billion to Western Europe for long-term reconstruction, a clean break from the piecemeal emergency loans of 1945–47.

- 1945–47 loans: UK $4.4bn, France $1.9bn, Italy $513m — only plugged emergency gaps.
- Funded food/feed/fertiliser (~1/3, mostly year 1), then capital goods, raw materials, fuel.
- Recipients’ GNP rose 15–25% (1948–52).
- Required European collaboration → ECE and OEEC (later OECD); deliberately aimed to remobilise German industry.
- Motives: Cold War containment, Pax Americana, and US economic self-interest in overseas markets.

**Why it matters:** Central evidence for the USA as the dominant actor in 1945–52; the Cold War became the ‘political glue’ of the world economy (Gilpin, 2001). Early dependency later gave way to European competition with the US.

#### Operation Bird Dog and the West German ‘miracle’ (1948–1959)

Replacing the Reichsmark with the Deutsche Mark in 1948 launched the Wirtschaftswunder (‘Miracle on the Rhine’).

- Industrial production +15% (June–July 1948), +50% (June–Dec 1948).
- Real GDP growth averaged 7.4% (1949–59); industrial production doubled 1950–57; GNP grew 9–10%/yr.
- West Germany was Europe’s largest market and prime capital-goods exporter — solving the ‘German question’ was vital.
- Reconstruction growth confirmed by Dumke (1990) and Temin (2002); Vonyó (2008/2017) shows it lasted to the end of the Golden Age.

**Why it matters:** Use for ‘was it a miracle?’ questions: Van Der Wee’s point that prosperity followed depression and war means growth partly reflects catch-up from a low base, not purely policy success.

#### US security umbrella: NATO and the US–Japan Treaty (1949–1970)

By underwriting the defence of Western Europe and Japan, the US freed their resources for economic growth.

- NATO (April 1949): first Supreme Commander US Gen. Eisenhower.
- Mutual Defence Assistance Programme (Oct 1949): $1.4bn → $5.222bn after the Korean War; Mutual Security Act (1951–61): $33.4bn.
- US–Japan Treaty (1960): Japan’s defence spending/GDP fell 20% (1958–60), <1% of GDP by 1970.
- Patrick & Rosovsky (1976): Japan’s economy would have been 30% smaller by 1976 had it borne its own defence burden.

**Why it matters:** Quantifies how much US Cold War security contributed to growth — strong evidence for the USA factor, though also a cost that fed US relative decline.

#### European regional integration: EPU, EEC, EFTA and CAP (1950–1969)

Western Europe built regional institutions — a multilateral payments union, a customs union and a rival free-trade area — that drove intra-European trade growth.

- EPU (1950, $500m US grant): surpluses offset deficits multilaterally; full convertibility restored by 1958; 1950–70 world trade grew 8% p.a.
- EEC (Treaty of Rome, 1957): six members; tariff phase-out reached in 10.5 years instead of 12; intra-EEC trade quadrupled (1958–69).
- EFTA (1960): seven states incl. UK; most industrial products tariff-free by end-1966, two years ahead of the EC.
- CAP (1962/1967): target prices and guaranteed purchase of surplus; absorbed ~2/3 of the EEC budget in the 1970s–80s and raised food costs.

**Why it matters:** Shows Western Europe’s own agency in regional growth, beyond US leadership. CAP is a good ‘to what extent’ example of integration at real economic cost.

#### MNCs: American dominance and the rise of European/Japanese rivals (1950–1984)

American MNCs drove FDI, technology transfer and market access in the 1950s–60s; from the 1960s–70s European and Japanese MNCs expanded and balanced US predominance.

- By 1969 US multinationals produced ~$140bn of goods — more than any national economy except the US and USSR (Gilpin, 2001).
- US FDI $11.8bn (1950) → $233.4bn (1984); >2/5 of world FDI in 1981.
- European labour productivity only 40% of US levels in 1950 — closed partly via technology/management transfer.
- Hoechst: 117 plants in 45 countries; Japanese FDI in the US $303m (1951–72) → $16.5bn by 1984; US share of overseas investment 70.4% (1967) → 58.8% (1976).
- MNCs: ~30% of world production (1969); 57 of the 100 largest economic entities (1972).

**Why it matters:** Use when weighing state-driven vs private-capital-driven growth — MNCs increasingly ‘dwarfed’ state power. The European/Japanese rise links to the broader US-decline narrative (Brett, 1985).

#### Japan’s regional role: Flying Geese, South Korea and Taiwan (1952–1970s)

Following Akamatsu’s Flying Geese model, Japan shed industries losing comparative advantage to the Asian Tigers, investing, lending and transferring technology to South Korea and Taiwan.

- 1965 Japan–South Korea normalisation: $800m compensation; Japan overtook the US as Korea’s largest foreign investor in the early 1970s.
- Declining Japanese industries (steel, shipbuilding) shifted to Korea, whose firms became global leaders.
- Taiwan 1952–78: $1.92bn total investment, 17% ($321m) from Japan.
- Japan’s aid rose $246m (1960) → $5.8bn (1973) — 4th largest DAC donor by 1974.
- Stan Shih (Acer) cited Sony’s Akio Morita as a model.

**Why it matters:** Evidence that Japan moved from aid recipient to regional engine and donor — Western Europe/Japan emulating the US role as US dominance declined.

#### US market access for Japanese exports (1958–1960s)

Eisenhower resisted protectionist pressure and opened the US market to Japanese and European goods.

- OEEC share of US imports: 14% (1947) → 50% (1952).
- US Trade Act 1958 let Eisenhower cut tariffs by 20%; Joint Committee on US–Japan Trade (1958).
- Stainless-steel flatware dispute (1958): Japan offered a Voluntary Export Restraint instead of US tariffs.
- US purchases from Japan +150% (1958–60) → Japan’s first-ever trade surplus; US absorbed >30% of Japan’s exports over the next decade.

**Why it matters:** Shows the US as absorber of allied exports in the liberalisation era — and an early VER, a tool that later became central to New Protectionism.

#### Soviet bloc planned economies (1945–1989)

The USSR and Eastern Europe rebuilt and industrialised rapidly from a backward base, but the planned system soured by the 1970s.

- USSR growth: 4.8%/yr (1960s) → 2.4% (1970s) → <2% after 1980.
- Concentration on capital over consumer goods caused shortages; faulty planning caused production mistakes.
- Inability to match US/Japanese technology → uncompetitive exports → mounting debt to the West.
- Economic causes contributed to the 1989 revolutions that toppled communist rule.

**Why it matters:** Explains why comparable communist growth is not counted in the ‘Golden Age of Capitalism’: structural, short-lived and not widespread. A reminder that the topic is skewed toward industrialised capitalist economies.

#### Nixon Shock and the collapse of Bretton Woods (1965–1973)

With the dollar overvalued and US gold reserves falling, Nixon suspended gold convertibility on 15 August 1971; by March 1973 fixed rates had given way to floating exchange rates.

- Military outflows to Asia: <$1bn/yr (1960–64) → $2.7bn/yr (1969–70); federal deficit $5,922m (1964) → $25,161m (1968).
- US trade surplus $6.80bn (1964) → $0.60bn (1969); current account negative in 1968.
- Nixon: 90-day price/wage freeze, gold convertibility suspended, extra 10% tariff on dutiable imports.
- Smithsonian Agreement (Dec 1971); March 1973 six EC members jointly floated against the USD.
- Developing-country BOP deficits $46bn (1975) → $108bn (1981) as financial liberalisation spread.

**Why it matters:** Core evidence that the crisis was structural and rooted in US relative decline. Gilpin (2001): losing Bretton Woods’ financial discipline ‘opened the door’ to the debt expansion of the late 1970s–80s — the bridge to the Debt Crisis.

#### India’s pharmaceutical industry (Patents Act) (1970–1990s)

India’s Patents Act (1970) waived IP protection for drugs, letting Indian firms make generics at scale behind protection.

- Domestic firms’ share of the drug market ~20% (1970) → ~80% (1990s).
- Pharmaceutical companies: 2,257 (1970) → 16,000 (1990).
- Higher tariffs raised revenue used to subsidise key sectors.

**Why it matters:** A rare case of protectionism enabling rapid, self-sufficient development — use for the short-run benefits side of a protectionism essay.

#### New Protectionism (1970s–1990s)

From the 1970s countries shielded industries with non-tariff barriers (quotas, VERs, subsidies, standards, the MFA, Super/Section 301) that were hard to quantify or negotiate down.

- Four causes: weakening US will/capacity, ineffective institutions, domestic lobby groups, and NTBs being harder to reduce.
- US deficit $2.9bn (1967) → $19.8bn (1971); 1970 first US goods trade deficit since 1893, $140bn by 1985.
- Dec 1982 US unemployment 10.6%; autos 23.2%; primary metals 29.2%.
- Reagan’s Omnibus Foreign Trade and Competitiveness Act (1988): 10% bilateral surplus-reduction requirement.
- World trade volume growth 1.2% (1980), 0.8% (1981), −2.2% (1982).

**Why it matters:** Shows the reversal of Golden Age cooperation as governments privileged national problems; it had lasting structural consequences, making future liberalisation harder.

#### Oil crises of 1973 and 1979 (Oct 1973–1980)

OPEC cut exports and embargoed the US, Netherlands and Denmark in 1973; the Iranian Revolution cut supply in 1979 — producing inflation, stagnation and stagflation.

- 1973: prices +70% in Oct (to >$5/barrel), +130% more by Dec (~$12); 1979: nearly tripled to $39.50.
- Oil 8.5% of Western Europe’s energy (1950) → 60% (1970); Saudi light crude only $1.93 (1955) → $2.18 (1971) (Judt, 2005).
- Inflation 6.4% (1969–73) → 11.9% (1973–79); OECD unemployment +7 million (1974–75).
- Non-oil LDC deficits $11.5bn (1973) → $45.9bn (1975); output per capita fell in 15 Sub-Saharan African countries in the 1970s.
- Developed economies adapted — alternative energy, restructuring; Taiwan set up ITRI (1973).

**Why it matters:** The key comparative point: long-term damage to oil-importing developing economies vs adaptation by developed ones. A singular trigger that revealed pre-existing weaknesses and fed New Protectionism and the Debt Crisis.

#### Uruguay and Colombia’s beef exports (1972–1977)

New EEC import restrictions on beef/veal from early 1974 cut Uruguay’s largest remaining market and wiped out Colombia’s EEC beef trade.

- EEC sanitary regulations had already closed Canada, Japan and US markets to Uruguayan beef.
- Uruguay chilled/frozen beef exports US$138m (1974) → US$68m (1975); by 1977 volume to the EEC ~1/5 of 1972–73 levels.
- Colombian beef exports to the EEC US$17m (1973) → zero by mid-1977.

**Why it matters:** Clear case of developed-country protectionism devastating developing-country export revenue — use for the disproportionate impact on developing countries.

#### Thatcherism and Reaganomics (1979–1990)

Governments abandoned employment/growth as the main aim in favour of fighting inflation, with tax cuts, deregulation and privatisation.

- Reagan: top marginal tax 70% → 50% (1982) → 38.5% (1987) → eventually 28%; corporate tax 48% → 34%.
- Reagan outcomes: 21+ million jobs (Dec 1982–June 1990, per Laffer); inflation cut to 4%.
- Thatcher: privatised British Telecom, British Gas, British Airways; higher income tax rate 83% → 40%; VAT 8% → 15%.
- UK unemployment 3.3 million (1986) → half by 1990, but inflation 7.5%/yr (1979–89); manufacturing recovered only by winter 1988.

**Why it matters:** Policy responses to stagflation showing the end of Keynesian consensus (Pollard, 1997); mixed outcomes are useful for evaluation.

#### Volcker Shock (1979–1982)

Fed Chair Paul Volcker raised US interest rates from ~10% to above 20% for almost 3 years to crush US inflation; world rates followed.

- US inflation fell to 4%.
- Real interest rates on LDC debt jumped to 6% (1981) and 8% (1982).
- Each 1-point rise in US rates cost Third World debtors $4–5bn/yr.
- Latin America borrowed $1bn/week in late 1981 mostly to service old debt (Frieden, 2007).

**Why it matters:** Shows global interconnectedness even in protectionist years: a policy designed to fix US inflation pushed developing-country debt to unsustainable levels.

#### Third World Debt Crisis (Mexico, 1982) (Aug 1982–1990)

In August 1982 Mexico said it could not service $86bn of debt, triggering a wave of sovereign defaults/near-defaults.

- ~$150bn of OPEC petrodollars deposited in commercial banks (1974–80) were recycled as loans.
- Latin America borrowed $50bn in 1981 (total $300bn); the Third World owed $750bn.
- By 1985: Brazil ($99bn), Mexico ($97bn), Argentina ($48bn) the largest debtors.
- Developing-country debt $87bn (1971) → $810bn (1984); capital flight >100% of new loans in Venezuela.
- Mexico’s real GDP grew just 0.1%/yr (1983–88).

**Why it matters:** The culmination of the causal chains (Bretton Woods collapse, petrodollars, oil shocks, Volcker); shows long-run divergence between developed and developing economies.

#### Managing the debt crisis: IMF conditionality, Baker and Brady Plans (1982–early 1990s)

Debtors accepted IMF austerity and structural adjustment in return for loans, while the Paris/London Clubs and the Baker and Brady Plans moved from rescheduling toward debt reduction.

- Mexico (1982) was the first SAP country; early gains stalled by 1985; Dec 1982 IMF $3.8bn loan.
- By end-1983, 17 Latin American countries had IMF adjustment agreements.
- Toronto terms (Oct 1988): partial debt cancellation for the poorest (33.33%, raised to 50% by Dec 1991).
- Baker Plan (1985) favoured growth via trade liberalisation and FDI; Brady Plan (1989) opened the way to actual debt reduction.
- Washington Consensus (Williamson, 1990): 10-point package that contradicted LDCs’ state-led models.

**Why it matters:** Shows international institutions adapting, but conditionality caused resentment; the crisis ended by the early 1990s once debt relief was agreed (Ocampo et al., 2014).

#### Continued cooperation: G7, WEF, ERM and the Plaza Accord (1971–1997)

Despite protectionism, advanced economies kept cooperating through informal summits, forums and currency agreements.

- WEF founded 1971; G6 → G7 with Canada (1976) → G8 with Russia (1997) — no legislative power.
- ERM (1979) reduced exchange-rate variability among EEC members; ERM II (1999) accompanied the euro.
- Plaza Accord (22 Sept 1985): France, West Germany, Japan, UK, US depreciated the USD vs Yen/Deutsche Mark.
- Plaza corrected the US–Germany imbalance but not US–Japan.

**Why it matters:** Prevents overstating the breakdown of cooperation in the Crisis Decades — cooperation persisted in altered forms.

#### IMF bailout of South Korea (Asian Financial Crisis) (1997–2001)

The IMF led a $60bn bailout package with conditions on exchange rates, monetary policy, structural reform and foreign participation in finance.

- Won repaid in full by Aug 2001, ahead of maturity.
- By May 1998, 80% of households suffered lower incomes.
- Unemployment more than tripled (2.05% → 6.96%, 1997–99).
- ~1/4 of the population joined a gold-collecting campaign, raising $2.2bn.

**Why it matters:** Even ‘successful’ IMF interventions imposed severe social costs — valuable for ‘assess the extent’ evaluation of international institutions.

### Historians

- **Van Der Wee:** Postwar prosperity was preceded by depression and war, so growth partly reflects catch-up from a low base rather than pure policy success.
- **Dumke (1990) and Temin (2002):** Econometrically confirmed that reconstruction growth drove Europe’s postwar ‘growth miracles’.
- **Vonyó (2008/2017):** Reconstruction’s impact lasted until the end of the Golden Age.
- **Kenwood & Lougheed (2001):** OECD-16 real GDP grew 4.8% p.a. (1950–73), developing countries 6% and the communist bloc 5%.
- **Brett (1985):** Documented US relative decline — a falling trade surplus, foreign-held dollars exceeding reserves by 1961 and a weakening competitive position vs Western Europe/Japan.
- **Hobsbawm (1996, 1999):** The Golden Age brought an unprecedented technological revolution, but after 1973 the world ‘lost its bearings’ and capitalism’s operations became uncontrollable.
- **Gilpin (2001):** The Cold War was the ‘political glue’ of the world economy, and abandoning Bretton Woods opened the door to the debt expansion of the late 1970s–80s.
- **Patrick & Rosovsky (1976):** Japan’s economy would have been 30% smaller by 1976 had it borne its own defence burden.
- **Spero & Hart (2003):** A firm engaging only in overseas trade or contracting is not truly multinational.
- **Wilkins (1974):** For years ‘multinational corporation’ was virtually a euphemism for the overseas expansion of America’s giant corporations.
- **Judt (2005):** World trade grew 8%/yr in the 1950s–60s, and decades of flat oil prices made the 1973/79 spikes all the more disruptive.
- **Pollard (1997):** Governments shifted from growth/employment to fighting inflation, with monetarist cuts in government spending.
- **Frieden (2007):** As lenders stopped lending, debtor countries ran out of money faster in a self-reinforcing panic.
- **John Williamson (1990):** Coined the Washington Consensus, the 10-point reform package promoted by the IMF, World Bank and US Treasury.
- **Ocampo et al. (2014):** The debt crisis effectively ended by the early 1990s once debt relief was agreed and investors returned.
- **Akamatsu Kaname (1930s):** The Flying Geese model: industries cascade from Japan to less-developed Asian economies in a V-formation.

### Using this topic in the exam

- ‘How far was X responsible for growth/crisis?’: explain X, weigh it against at least two other factors (USA, Western Europe/Japan, MNCs, institutions, external shocks), and judge how the answer changes across the four phases.
- ‘Assess the impact of [event]’: always separate short-term vs long-term, developed vs developing, and different regions (e.g. Japan vs Western manufacturing heartlands) — explicitly rewarded for the oil and debt crises.
- ‘Miracle’ questions: counter with catch-up growth, deliberate US policy and institutional design — but concede genuinely new features (unprecedented cooperation and technological revolution).
- Don’t treat growth and challenges as separate stories: the seeds of the 1970s crises (Bretton Woods’ dollar dependence, US overstretch, financial liberalisation) were sown in the Golden Age; the best essays argue both structural causes and singular triggers.
- Don’t overstate the breakdown of cooperation: G7, WEF, GATT rounds, the Plaza Accord and the ERM continued. Comparisons: USA vs Western Europe/Japan as donors; traditional vs New Protectionism.
- Pitfall: the discussion is skewed toward industrialised economies (75% of world production) — developing and socialist economies are understudied, and outcomes were uneven even within the West.

## P1 Theme II · Japan & China

*What led to the rise of the Japanese economy from 1947 to 1991? What led to the rise of the Chinese economy from 1978 to 2000?*

Japan’s rise is explained by four interacting factor clusters: government intervention (MITI and the developmental state), the private keiretsu system, socio-economic changes (education, savings, consensus) and international developments (the Cold War and the global economy). It ran in two phases: rapid growth (1945–1973; GNP ~10% p.a. 1950–73) and stable growth (1974–1991; ~4% p.a.) despite oil shocks, until the 1990 bubble burst.

The weighting shifts over time: US market access, technology transfer and the defence subsidy were especially critical in the 1950s–60s, government-led industrial policy dominated the high-growth era, while savings and education were constants that magnified the other factors. Beckley, Horiuchi and Miller (2018) argue Japan’s institutions worked partly because of its ‘privileged geopolitical environment’.

China’s transformation (1978–2000) was a transition from a command to a market economy, not just industrial catch-up. Maoist failure (Great Leap Forward, Cultural Revolution) created the political case for reform; Deng’s state intervention set the direction; market-oriented reforms (HRS, TVEs, SOE reform) and the open door (SEZs, FDI, WTO) delivered growth averaging 9.2% p.a.; international developments supplied capital, technology and markets.

Both economies combined strong state direction with market dynamism and favourable international conditions. But China’s reform was gradual and dual-track under one-party control, and it was not linear — Tiananmen (1989) and the Rectification paused it until the 1992 Southern Tour — whereas Japan’s postwar trajectory was comparatively steady.

**Japan: government intervention (public)**

- Developmental state (Johnson, 1982/1995): a small elite bureaucracy with a ‘pilot agency’ (MITI) prioritising growth without displacing market rationality.
- Agencies: MITI (1949) administrative guidance; JDB (1951) below-market loans; EPA five-year plans; Economic Deliberation Council (1952).
- Tools: infrastructure, subsidies and import protection, cartelised restructuring, SME support, FILP loans and tax incentives.
- ‘Picking winners’: labour-intensive (1950s) → capital-intensive (1960s) → knowledge industries (1970s) → high-quality technology (1980s).

**Japan: keiretsu, socio-economic factors and international developments**

- Keiretsu: main banks, cross-shareholdings, trading companies, R&D and mutual insurance; symbiosis with MITI’s strategic industries (Ozkan, 2011).
- Lifetime employment and enterprise unions enabled firm-specific training, Just-In-Time production and kaizen.
- Education, high savings and consensus culture were enabling/multiplier factors rather than standalone drivers.
- Cold War: Reverse Course, US security guarantee (Yoshida Doctrine), technology transfer, US market access, Korean and Vietnam War procurements.

**China: push factors and state intervention**

- Maoist economy: autarchy plus US embargo, military-skewed industry (Third Front), no competition, Party interference in factories.
- Great Leap Forward and Cultural Revolution discredited Maoism and created the political opening for reform after 1976.
- Deng’s 3rd Plenum (Dec 1978): modernisation replaced class struggle — ‘Socialism with Chinese Characteristics’ and the Four Modernisations.
- Institutions: a modern banking system, education reform, inflation control cycles and the 1992 Southern Tour relaunch.

**China: market-oriented reforms and the open door**

- HRS (from Dec 1978) built political momentum through immediate, visible gains in agriculture.
- SOE dual-track reform and TVEs: gradualism without a sudden shock to the plan, but with corruption/arbitrage risks.
- SEZs (1980) as controlled experiments, then coastal cities, open regions and the ‘Four-Along’ strategy; FDI tax incentives.
- WTO accession (Nov 2001) as the culmination of the open-door process — just outside the 1978–2000 window.

**Comparing Japan and China**

- Both had a coordinating state: MITI in Japan; Deng in China, who also had to preserve one-party control while liberalising.
- Keiretsu (large established private conglomerates) vs TVEs (small, decentralised, hybrid public/market entities).
- Japan’s infant-industry protection vs China’s SEZs: both sequenced exposure to international competition.
- International support: Japan relied heavily on the US; China diversified across the US, Japan, ASEAN and the overseas Chinese diaspora.
- Japan: postwar reconstruction/catch-up in a market economy (industrial transformation); China: transition from a command economy (structural transformation).

### Case studies

#### Japan: Allied Occupation and the Reverse Course (1945–1952)

SCAP first punished and reformed Japan, then from 1947/48 shifted to economic rehabilitation out of fear that a weak economy would boost communism, before formal peace in 1951–52.

- 1945–47: war crimes trials, military dismantled, land reform, attempted zaibatsu break-up, 1947 Constitution (Art. 9).
- Reverse Course (1947/48–50) driven by fear of domestic communism, especially after the Chinese Civil War.
- 1949 Dodge Line fiscal austerity; 1952 yen pegged at 350:$1.
- San Francisco Peace Treaty (Sept 1951): 49/52 nations signed; US kept bases in exchange for a security pact.

**Why it matters:** Sets the Cold War context in which international factors became the precondition for Japan’s growth — Japan’s timing was fortune, not policy choice (Beckley et al., 2018).

#### Japan: education reform and the high savings rate (1946–1980s)

Occupation-era reform extended compulsory education, producing the world’s most educated workforce, while household savings channelled through banks funded domestic investment.

- Compulsory education extended to 9 years (Education Reform Council, Aug 1946).
- Progression to senior high school: 43% (1950) → 94% (1980); 91% of the workforce high-school educated by 1971.
- Household savings rate <10% (early 1950s) → >20% (1970 onward); national savings peaked at 31% in the early 1970s.
- 1959–70 personal savings: Japan 18.3% vs Germany 12% vs US 7%.

**Why it matters:** Best framed as enabling/multiplier factors: savings fed capital to banks/keiretsu, education supplied skilled labour for MITI’s targeted industries — the precondition that made state and keiretsu strategies work.

#### Japan: MITI and the developmental-state agencies (1949–1952)

MITI became the architect of industrial policy through ‘administrative guidance’, supported by the JDB, EPA and Economic Deliberation Council.

- MITI (est. 1949): architect of industrial policy, arbiter and regulator via gyosei shido.
- Japan Development Bank (1951): below-market loans to coal, steel, electric power, shipping.
- EPA’s non-binding five-year plans made it an ‘economic cheerleader and sign-painter’ (Gordon, 2003).
- Economic Deliberation Council (1952): businessmen, bankers and academics advising the PM.

**Why it matters:** Core of the ‘government intervention’ argument — not a Soviet command economy, but state management was extensive (Gordon, 2003). Limitation: firms sometimes defied MITI (e.g. Honda’s entry into autos).

#### Japan: Korean War special procurements (tokuju) (1950–1953)

Japan became the supply base for UN forces, with special procurements boosting textiles, metals, chemicals, transport and electricity.

- Manufacturing production +50%, productivity +30% (June 1950–June 1951).
- Manufacturing production index: 68.9 (1949) → 189.4 (1955).
- Special procurement income $149m (1950) → $824m (1952); ~$500m p.a. through 1961.
- With US troop spending, 37% of all foreign exchange receipts (1952–53); roughly doubled raw-material import capacity.

**Why it matters:** Shows Cold War conflict as a direct economic stimulus in the recovery phase, funding expansion in bottleneck industries — strong evidence for international factors.

#### Japan: US technology transfer and the Toyota case (1950–1962)

Thousands of technical cooperation contracts, mostly with American firms, closed the technology gap; Toyota modelled itself on Ford’s methods.

- 1950–62: 1,998 technical cooperation contracts, ~2/3 with American firms.
- Technology gap virtually closed by the early 1960s; imported tech often made ~20% more efficient.
- Toyota visited Ford’s River Rouge plant; Five-Year Modernization Plan (1951–55); TWI and suggestion boxes (Fujimoto, 1998).
- Motomachi (1959): Japan’s first high-volume passenger-car plant; output 100,000 units/year by decade’s end.

**Why it matters:** International support was necessary, but Japan still had to convert it into productive capacity — domestic factors mattered for exploiting the opportunity.

#### Japan: industrial policy tools — infant-industry protection and FILP (1948–1964)

The state shielded infant industries with quotas and tariffs, and funded them through FILP loans, tax incentives and rationalisation laws.

- Near-total car import ban for a decade+ from the 1950s; tariffs up to over 60% on finished goods like automobiles.
- 1960s: ~400 product categories restricted; until 1962 ‘banned unless listed’.
- Import bans lifted only once industries matured: buses/trucks 1961, colour TVs 1964, computers 1975.
- FILP (from 1948): agencies grew from 14 (1953) to 52 (1975); effective corporate tax 19–22% vs 27–31% in the US.
- Enterprise Rationalization Promotion Law (1952); Special Measures Law (1964) consolidated excess-capacity sectors.

**Why it matters:** Shows import substitution → export promotion. Limitation: protection also entrenched inefficient ‘sunset’ industries needing costly later support.

#### Japan: the keiretsu system and lifetime employment (1950s–1980s)

Groups of 20–40 firms linked by main banks and cross-shareholdings pooled capital, shared R&D and insured each other, backed by lifetime employment.

- Big Six horizontal keiretsu (1950s): Mitsui, Mitsubishi, Sumitomo, Fuyo, Sanwa, Dai-Ichi Kangyo.
- Sumitomo Bank rescued Mazda in the 1970s with a new CEO and financial guidance.
- Vertical keiretsu: production (Toyota suppliers), distribution (Matsushita), capital (Tokyu).
- Strong correlation between MITI-designated strategic industries and keiretsu investment (Ozkan, 2011).
- Lifetime employment enabled on-the-job training, Just-In-Time production and kaizen.

**Why it matters:** The private-sector factor that kept firms competitive and insulated from short-termism; it reinforced MITI policy. Limitation: cheap intra-group capital encouraged excessive debt, and opacity reduced transparency (Kensy, 2001).

#### Japan: US security guarantee and the Yoshida Doctrine (1951–1970)

The US-Japan Mutual Security Treaty (1951, revised 1960) let Japan rely on the US for defence and concentrate resources on economic rebuilding.

- US bases in Japan in exchange for a US defence commitment.
- Defence spending/GDP fell 20% (1958–60), the sharpest shift of the postwar era.
- Below 1% of GDP by 1970.
- Patrick and Rosovsky (1976): economy 30% smaller by 1976 without the US umbrella.

**Why it matters:** Links international and government factors: low defence spending freed resources for MITI-directed investment.

#### Japan: GATT entry and access to the US market (1955–1960s)

Japan joined GATT in 1955, and Cold War logic led US administrations to resist protectionism and open the American market.

- 14/32 GATT members initially refused Japan MFN status; a balance-of-payments crisis followed the Korean War boom.
- Eisenhower, Kennedy and Johnson resisted US protectionist pressure.
- US purchases from Japan rose >150% (1958–60) → Japan’s first-ever trade surplus.
- US absorbed >30% of Japan’s exports over the following decade.

**Why it matters:** Forsberg (2000): the US desire to consolidate the Cold War alliance let Japan crack the American market, and Japanese industry seized the moment.

#### China: Great Leap Forward (1958–1962)

Mao tried to bypass gradual industrialisation through mass rural communes and labour-intensive methods, aiming to double grain and steel output.

- Private plots abolished; anti-sparrow campaign caused locust swarms; inflated harvest reports led to over-taxation.
- Backyard steel furnaces produced unusable pig iron and diverted farm labour.
- ‘Three Bitter Years’: an estimated 30 million deaths (Becker, 1996); 30–40% of housing demolished.
- Industrial investment cut 82% (1960–62); growth swung from +21.3% (1958) to −27.3% (1961).

**Why it matters:** Key evidence for the ‘push factor’ argument — Maoist failure made reform politically possible, though it explains why, not how, reform succeeded.

#### Japan: Ikeda’s National Income Doubling Plan (1960)

Ikeda aimed to double national income within 10 years, with exports expanding ~10% p.a., focused on machinery and North American/European markets.

- Five elements: social overhead capital, heavy/chemical industries, trade, labour quality/science, low-income living standards.
- First-year growth ~10% vs the 7.2% p.a. envisaged.
- Export success now rested on efficiency, not low wages.
- Shift toward capital/technology-intensive industries (petrochemicals, steel, automobiles) from the 1960s.

**Why it matters:** Shows the state signalling confidence and setting direction during the 1955–73 high-growth era, when government and keiretsu factors dominated.

#### Japan: Vietnam War stimulus (1965–1972)

Japan profited from military procurement, ship-repair contracts and exports to a US-backed South Vietnamese market.

- Japan earned ~$1bn/year (Schaller, 1997).
- Japan’s share of world GDP: 5.7% (1964) → 12.9% (1973), equal to Britain + France combined.
- 1968: Japan’s GNP ranked 2nd in the world.

**Why it matters:** A second Cold War windfall — reinforces the ‘privileged geopolitical environment’ argument.

#### China: Cultural Revolution (1966–1976)

Mao launched the Cultural Revolution to replace successors with loyalists and rectify the CCP; Red Guards persecuted ‘bourgeois’ individuals.

- An estimated 0.5–2 million deaths.
- Urban production fell, agricultural output dropped, incomes stagnated.
- Higher education shut for 6 years — the ‘Lost Generation’.
- Maoist industry was too small and inefficient (Bramall, 2009); Third Front (1964) skewed output to military production.

**Why it matters:** Lamb (2005): Mao’s revolution ironically led to China’s embrace of capitalism. Mao’s death (1976) and its discrediting created the opening for reform.

#### Japan: Nixon Shock (1971)

The end of Gold-Dollar convertibility forced the yen up and hit Japan’s balance of payments and growth.

- Yen appreciated 16% by end-1971.
- Balance of payments +$4.6bn (1971) → −$10bn (1973).
- Growth fell from ~10% (1960s average) to 3% (1971).

**Why it matters:** Shows US goodwill was not unconditional — a limit on the international-factor argument.

#### China: Sino-American rapprochement (1972–1982)

Nixon’s 1972 visit and Carter’s 1979 recognition ended China’s isolation from the US, lifting the trade embargo.

- Feb 1972 Shanghai Communiqué; drivers included the Sino-Soviet split and ‘Ping Pong Diplomacy’.
- Jan 1979 full diplomatic recognition; Taiwan Relations Act (Apr 1979); 1982 third joint communiqué.
- US lifted its 20-year trade embargo — access to US capital, technology and markets.
- Deng: ‘all of those countries that were with the United States have been rich’ (per Jian, 2019).

**Why it matters:** Enabled Deng’s broader opening (students abroad, trade, FDI, SEZs) — parallels Japan’s Cold War US backing as geopolitical timing.

#### Japan: the 1970s oil crises and adaptation (1973–1989)

After a brief recession Japan adapted faster than the West, using monetary discipline, nuclear power and trade reorientation.

- Inflation spiked to 25% (1972–75); BOJ money-supply forecasts from 1975 cut it to <3% by the mid-1980s.
- Nuclear power produced 25% of electricity by 1985.
- 1975–89: growth 4–5% p.a., roughly double major European economies, while the US suffered stagflation.
- Imports from the Middle East ~20x higher in 1980 than 1970.

**Why it matters:** Evidence for the ‘stable growth’ phase (1974–91): adaptive resilience across all factors.

#### Japan: VLSI semiconductor project (1970s–1987)

MITI compelled reluctant national champions into a cooperative R&D laboratory as it shifted toward technology-intensive industries.

- Toshiba, Hitachi, Mitsubishi; $116m in subsidies.
- Semiconductor equipment market share 10% (1970s) → 35% (1987).
- 1,000+ patents; helped Japanese firms catch up with IBM in IC production.
- Exports shifted from textiles and toys (1950s) to automobiles and semiconductors (1980s) (Ito, 1996).

**Why it matters:** Strongest example of MITI ‘picking winners’ for first-mover advantage — but revisionists argue market forces and firm dynamism did more.

#### China: Deng, the 3rd Plenum and the Four Modernisations (Dec 1978)

Deng replaced class struggle with modernisation as the Party’s objective, championing the Four Modernisations within ‘Socialism with Chinese Characteristics’.

- Challenge: 6.5bn yuan deficit, 20 million unemployed, ~100 million undernourished.
- Four Modernisations (Zhou Enlai, 1963): agriculture, industry, national defence, science & technology.
- 800,000+ researchers crash-trained; students abroad 2,124 (1980) → 38,989 (2000).
- By 1977 planners’ first instinct was to rebuild the plan, not reform (Naughton, 2010).
- ‘It does not matter if a cat is black or white…’ — Deng.

**Why it matters:** Deng as catalyst/coordinator, comparable to MITI but having to preserve one-party control; gradualism contrasts with Soviet ‘shock therapy’.

#### China: Household Responsibility System (HRS) (1978–1984)

Households contracted state-owned plots, met state quotas and kept the remainder.

- Adoption: 1% (1979) → 45% (1981) → 98% (1983) → 99% (1984).
- Agricultural output grew ~9% p.a. through the 1980s.
- 1984 grain output a record 407 million tonnes (+33.6% vs 1978); meat output +156% by 1988.
- HRS savings capitalised TVEs.

**Why it matters:** Naughton (2010): the return to household farming was one of the main causes of output acceleration and built political support for wider reform.

#### China: Japan and the ‘Flying Geese’ — Sino-Japanese trade and aid (1978–1985)

The 1978 Treaty of Peace & Friendship and Long-Term Trade Agreement made Japan a source of plants, technology and aid, in line with Akamatsu’s Flying Geese paradigm.

- Long-Term Trade Agreement target: $20bn two-way trade (1978–85).
- Japan exported over $6bn of plants/technology — 52% of China’s tech imports (1978–84).
- Trade $3.5bn (1977) → $13.2bn (1984); Japan ~26% of China’s external trade.
- 1984 aid package of ¥470bn (~$2.1bn), e.g. Baoshan steel mill.

**Why it matters:** Shows China’s diversified international support beyond the US — industrial transfer from Japan.

#### China: SOE reform and the dual-track system (1978–1998)

State enterprises moved through management reform, dual-track plan-and-market pricing and, after 1992, ownership reform, alongside wage reform.

- Sichuan pilot (1978) → 6,600 large/medium SOEs by 1980.
- Dual track (1984–92): above-quota output sold at up to 20% above the state price.
- 1995 ‘zhuada fangxiao’: by 1998, 1/4 of SOEs restructured.
- Public share of industrial output 73.4% (1983) → 11.1% (2003); private firms 440,000 (1996) → 1.32 million (2001).
- Real monthly wages rose 1.9x (1992–97).

**Why it matters:** Gradualism allowed reform without shocking the plan, but created corruption/arbitrage risks between plan and market prices.

#### China: Township and Village Enterprises (TVEs) (1978–2000)

Small, autonomous, locally owned public enterprises with hard budget constraints became a major engine of rural industry and exports.

- RMB 3,918bn (30% of gross domestic output) by 1994; 47% of industrial value-added by 2000.
- ~80,000 export-oriented TVEs, >40% of exports; TVE exports +28% p.a. (1988–99) vs 13% overall.
- Employment 28.3 million (1978) → 127 million (end-1994).
- Low tax (6% of profits in 1980); ~60% of scientific innovations commercialised via TVEs.

**Why it matters:** A distinctively Chinese innovation — contrast with Japan’s keiretsu; mutually reinforcing with HRS.

#### China: building a modern banking system (1978–1990)

The single-bank system was split into a central bank and four specialised state banks, followed by regional banks and stock exchanges.

- Pre-1978 PBOC held ~93% of financial assets.
- Bank of China, PCBC, ABC and ICBC (1984) created.
- 1984 incentive rule: Rural Credit Cooperatives’ loans outstanding doubled in a single year.
- 1990: Shanghai and Shenzhen stock exchanges.

**Why it matters:** Shows institutional reform reshaping behaviour and fuelling credit-driven growth.

#### China: inflation control cycles (1979–1995)

Successive reform waves overheated the economy, and the state repeatedly tightened credit and spending.

- 1980 inflation 6% → deficit cut by 2/3 → 1.9% average (1981–83).
- 1985: 8.8% → credit growth cut 31% → 22%.
- 1988: 27% → September 1988 tightening → 0.6% (1990).
- 1994: 24.1% → 17.1% (1995) while growth dipped only to 10.2%.

**Why it matters:** Evidence of the state managing overheating — reform was not smooth or linear.

#### China: Special Economic Zones and coastal opening (1980–1990)

SEZs created a ‘micro climate’ to attract capital, technology and management before scaling the opening up (Wu, 2005).

- Aug 1980: Shenzhen, Zhuhai, Shantou, Xiamen; May 1984: 14 coastal cities; Apr 1988: Hainan.
- SEZ tax rate 18% (vs 33%); 10% for export/tech FFEs.
- By 1990: FDI in the 14 coastal cities >$10bn; coastal provinces ~2/3 of exports.
- First 4 SEZs’ industrial output rose 8-fold (1985–90).

**Why it matters:** Controlled experiments comparable to Japan’s infant-industry protection; cost: growing coastal–inland inequality.

#### Japan: liberalisation, the Plaza Accord and the bubble (1970s–1990)

Trade friction with the US forced Japan to open markets and liberalise finance; the Plaza Accord’s endaka was followed by the bubble’s collapse.

- Exports to the US $666m → $9.6bn (1958–73).
- 1978 ‘voluntary’ export restraints on cars to the US.
- Capital controls lifted early 1980s; interest rate regulations fully lifted by ~1990.
- 1985 Plaza Accord → endaka; telecoms/railways privatised; 1990 bubble burst.

**Why it matters:** Limitations: trade friction, sunset-industry costs and the 1990 collapse show the ‘miracle’ had structural fragilities.

#### China: Tiananmen, Rectification and the Southern Tour (1989–1992)

After Tiananmen reform paused; Deng’s 1992 tour of Shenzhen, Zhuhai and Shanghai relaunched it.

- 1989–91 Rectification: suppression of dissent, spending cuts.
- Deng urged bolder liberalisation — ‘not act as women with bound feet’.
- Boosted reformists such as Zhu Rongji.
- Oct 1992: 14th Party Congress adopted the ‘socialist market economy’.

**Why it matters:** State intervention was not linear — reform needed a deliberate political relaunch, unlike Japan.

#### China: FDI, overseas Chinese capital, ASEAN and the ‘Four-Along’ strategy (1990s)

Opening spread along the coast, borders, rivers and railways; FDI surged, dominated by Hong Kong, Taiwan and Singapore.

- FDI near-nil (late 1970s) → $40–50bn/year (late 1990s), 2nd-largest recipient after the US.
- FFEs’ share of exports 1% (1985) → 45% (1999).
- 1999 sources: Hong Kong 41%, EU 11%, US 9.9%, Japan 7.2%.
- Trade with ASEAN $14.29bn (1994) → $25.04bn (1997); 77.5% of ASEAN FDI from Singapore.

**Why it matters:** Supports the argument that diaspora/regional capital, not just Western FDI, drove the opening — and diversified support, unlike Japan.

#### China: WTO accession (Applied 1986, acceded Nov 2001)

After 15 years of negotiation China joined the WTO, cutting tariffs and liberalising services.

- Industrial tariffs to 8.9% (vs India 32.4%, Brazil 27%).
- Liberalised financial, telecom, distribution and legal services.
- 12-year transitional safeguard on Chinese goods.
- Complemented by the 1998 State Council reform under Zhu Rongji.

**Why it matters:** The culmination of the open door — but falls just outside the 1978–2000 window; handle dates carefully.

### Historians

- **Johnson (1982/1995):** Japan was a developmental state led by an elite bureaucracy and a pilot agency, without displacing market rationality.
- **Gordon (2003):** Japan was not a command economy, but its postwar story was not a simple free-market miracle — state management was extensive.
- **Ito (1996):** Japanese exports shifted from textiles and toys in the 1950s to automobiles and semiconductors in the 1980s.
- **Ozkan (2011):** MITI and the keiretsu had a ‘symbiotic relationship’.
- **McGuire (2002):** Keiretsu structure gave member firms in-group loans, domestic markets and international marketing capabilities.
- **Kensy (2001):** ‘The whole of Japan is one big keiretsu’ — the networks reduced market transparency.
- **Yamamura (1976):** High Japanese savings came from rising income, bonuses, seniority wages, weak welfare, tax incentives and thrift (background only).
- **Patrick and Rosovsky (1976):** Japan’s economy would have been 30% smaller by 1976 without the US defence umbrella.
- **Fujimoto (1998):** Toyota modernised by adopting American methods after visiting Ford.
- **Forsberg (2000):** The US desire to consolidate the Cold War alliance let Japan crack the American market.
- **Schaller (1997):** Japan earned ~$1bn/year from the Vietnam War.
- **Beckley, Horiuchi and Miller (2018):** Japan’s institutions were enhanced by a privileged geopolitical environment; without it, solid growth but probably not a miracle.
- **Bramall (2009):** Late Maoist industry was too small and inefficient.
- **Becker (1996):** An estimated 30 million died in the Great Leap’s ‘Three Bitter Years’.
- **Lamb (2005):** The Cultural Revolution ironically led to China’s embrace of capitalism.
- **Naughton (2010):** Household farming drove output acceleration, and gradual reform of a command economy is feasible and resilient.
- **Wu (2005):** China could not open nationwide at once, so SEZs created a ‘micro climate’ first.
- **Jian (2019):** Reported Deng’s remark that countries with the United States became rich.
- **Akamatsu (1962):** The Flying Geese paradigm: industrial dynamism cascades from Japan to the NIEs, ASEAN and China.

### Using this topic in the exam

- Never treat factors in silos: argue interaction across time (MITI ↔ keiretsu; savings → bank lending → keiretsu investment; US umbrella → resources for industrial policy; HRS savings → TVEs; rapprochement → SEZs/FDI; Deng’s authority → protected reform).
- Japan thesis options: international factors necessary but not sufficient; government as primary coordinator; or argue by phases (Occupation/Korean War = international; 1955–73 = government + keiretsu; 1974–91 = adaptive resilience).
- China thesis options: Maoist failure as necessary precondition converted by Deng; market reforms as primary engine; or the open door + international developments as decisive for sustaining 9%+ growth.
- Comparisons: MITI vs Deng; keiretsu vs TVEs; infant-industry protection vs SEZs; Japan’s dependence on the US vs China’s diversified ties; industrial vs structural transformation.
- Show limitations: Japan’s trade friction, sunset industries and the 1990 bubble; China’s non-linear reform (Tiananmen, Rectification), coastal–inland inequality and dual-track corruption.
- Date pitfall: China’s WTO accession (Nov 2001) falls just outside the 1978–2000 window — treat it as the endpoint/legacy of 1990s policy.

## P1 Theme III · Arab–Israeli conflict

*Why did the Arab–Israeli conflict occur (1948–1979), and how effective were diplomacy and peacekeeping in managing its four wars (1948, 1956, 1967, 1973)?*

The notes treat the conflict as fundamentally one of competing nationalisms: Zionism and Arab nationalism grew almost in parallel in the 19th century, and Britain’s contradictory WWI promises (Hussein–McMahon, Sykes-Picot, Balfour) and the contradictory Palestine Mandate gave both peoples a claim to the same land. Religion (Jerusalem, the Western Wall) and economics (land, water, refugees) compounded the clash rather than causing it.

The conflict must be read at two levels: Palestinian vs Israeli, and the wider Arab states vs Israel, with the superpowers cutting across both from the 1950s. Four wars followed: 1948 (War of Independence / Al Nakba), 1956 (Suez / Tripartite Aggression), 1967 (Six-Day War / al-Naksa) and 1973 (Yom Kippur / October / Ramadan War).

The main line of argument on effectiveness: from 1948 to 1967, truces, armistices, UNTSO, UNEF I and Resolution 242 achieved only negative peace — fighting stopped but territory, refugees and recognition festered into the next war. UN mechanisms grew more robust but always depended on the parties’ consent.

Only after 1973, when Sadat’s limited war restored Arab dignity and the oil weapon added leverage, were conditions right (Yapp) for compromise. Kissinger’s shuttle diplomacy and Carter’s Camp David produced a durable peace — but only bilaterally between Egypt and Israel, leaving the Palestinian question unresolved.

**Causes: decolonisation and competing nationalisms**

- Decolonisation: overlapping British wartime promises and a Mandate tasked with both a Jewish national home and safeguarding all Palestinians’ rights.
- WWII made British withdrawal near-inevitable: imperial decline, US involvement, the Holocaust (6 million Jews murdered), the refugee crisis and violence (127 British soldiers killed by Oct 1947).
- Nationalism: Arab nationalism (anti-Turkish, anti-colonial, anti-Zionist), Pan-Arabism peaking in the 1960s, Palestinian nationalism formed in opposition to Zionism, and Herzl’s political Zionism.
- Security: Arab fears of Israeli expansionism vs Israeli existential threats, border infiltration and the drive for military superiority.
- Religion and economics are compounding factors: Jerusalem holy to all three Abrahamic faiths; post-1948 land expropriation, unequal resources and refugee poverty.

**Combatant states: the four wars**

- 1948: Israel survived the Arab invasion and controlled over 75% of the former Mandate; 750,000–940,000 Palestinian refugees.
- 1956: Egypt defeated militarily but Nasser triumphant politically; Britain and France the war’s losers.
- 1967: Israel tripled its territory (Sinai, West Bank, Gaza, Golan, East Jerusalem); Palestinian nationalism radicalised away from the Arab states.
- 1973: Arab forces not decisively defeated — restored dignity made negotiation politically possible for the first time.

**The superpowers**

- 1948: both the US and USSR rushed to recognise Israel; Czechoslovakia armed Israel despite the embargo.
- 1956: the US became the decisive arbiter, forcing Anglo-French-Israeli withdrawal; the Anglo-French exit left a vacuum filled by the US and USSR.
- 1967: Soviet false intelligence helped trigger the war; both superpowers sought to avoid direct confrontation; the US came to see Israel as a strategic counter to Soviet-aligned Arab states.
- 1973: direct US–Soviet crisis management (Kissinger–Brezhnev ceasefire), a brief possible confrontation over Egypt’s Third Army, and the oil weapon as a new economic dimension.

**The UN and conflict management**

- UNTSO (1948): unarmed observers — no enforcement power, could only report violations.
- UNEF I (1956): first armed peacekeeping force (‘Chapter Six and a Half’) — consent-dependent, withdrawn on Nasser’s request in 1967.
- Resolution 242 (1967): diplomatic ‘land for peace’ framework — no enforcement, ambiguous on withdrawal scope.
- UNEF II / UNDOF (1973/1974): larger, better-resourced, more durable — still reliant on self-reported compliance and great-power backing.
- Lasting peace came from bilateral diplomacy (Camp David), not the UN itself.

### Case studies

#### Britain’s conflicting wartime promises (1915–1917)

To win allies against the Ottomans, Britain made overlapping promises to the Arabs, France and the Zionists, none of which specified borders.

- Hussein–McMahon Correspondence (1915–16): Arab territory to return to Arab sovereignty in exchange for the Arab Revolt; Palestine was NOT excluded.
- Sykes-Picot Agreement (May 1916): secret Anglo-French division of Syria, Iraq, Lebanon and Palestine into spheres of influence (Russian assent).
- Balfour Declaration (2 Nov 1917): ‘a national home for the Jewish people’ in Palestine — not a Jewish state, no borders.
- Result: both Zionists and Arab nationalists believed Palestine had been promised to them.

**Why it matters:** The core evidence for decolonisation as the structural cause: British policy sowed the seeds of conflict, though competing nationalisms made it irreconcilable.

#### Mandate-era clashes: Wailing Wall, Arab Revolt, Peel and the 1939 White Paper (1920–1939)

Under a contradictory League of Nations Mandate (from 1922), communal violence grew as Jewish immigration rose, and British attempts at partition or restriction satisfied neither side.

- April 1920: Arab riots against growing Jewish settlement and organisation.
- 1929 Wailing Wall Incident (trigger: 1928 dispute over benches/a screen at the Western Wall); the inquiry blamed Zionist land purchases; Weizmann resigned over the Passfield White Paper.
- Nazi persecution from 1933 drove immigration → 1936 general strike → Arab Revolt (1936–1939).
- Peel Commission (1937) recommended partition — rejected by Palestinian Arabs; Britain retracted it.
- 1939 White Paper capped Jewish immigration at 15,000/year until 1944 — seen by Jews as betrayal of Balfour; Arabs also rejected it.

**Why it matters:** Shows religion (Jerusalem’s sanctity to all three faiths) and immigration compounding nationalist rivalry, and Britain’s inability to protect civilians or reconcile both sides.

#### WWII and the end of the Mandate (1945–Feb 1947)

Five converging factors made British withdrawal, and the path to a Jewish state, almost inevitable; Britain handed the problem to the UN.

- Decline of the British Empire: Britain sought an ‘honourable way out’ from 1945.
- US involvement: 1942 Biltmore Program; by 1944 most US politicians backed a Jewish state; Truman recognised Israel on 14 May 1948.
- Holocaust: 6 million Jews murdered, intensifying the Zionist push and international sympathy.
- Violence: Jewish attacks killed 127 British soldiers by Oct 1947; by Feb 1947 Britain decided to end the Mandate.
- Stalin adopted a pro-Zionist policy from late 1944, expecting a socialist state that would accelerate British decline.

**Why it matters:** Supports arguments that decolonisation plus external (superpower) support created Israel; useful for the superpower-involvement theme.

#### UN Partition Plan (Resolution 181) (29 Nov 1947)

UNSCOP found both claims valid but irreconcilable and recommended partition; the Zionists accepted, the Arabs rejected, and civil war followed until the Mandate expired.

- Arab state ~43% of Mandatory Palestine; Jewish state ~56% (much of it the arid Negev); Jerusalem a corpus separatum.
- Passed 33 in favour, 13 against, 10 abstentions; all 5 Arab member states voted against.
- 1947–48 civil war; Zionist forces launched Plan Dalet, conquering territory beyond the partition lines.
- Midnight 14 May 1948: Mandate expired; Jewish Agency declared the State of Israel (Ben-Gurion PM, Weizmann President).

**Why it matters:** Key evidence for territorial sovereignty as a cause: fragmented, indefensible borders with a large Arab population ‘trapped’ inside the Jewish state.

#### 1948 War of Independence / Al Nakba (Dec 1947–Jan 1949)

Israel’s declaration triggered invasion by Egypt, Jordan, Lebanon, Syria and Iraq; Israel won and expanded well beyond the partition lines.

- 15 May 1948: Arab states invade; atrocities on both sides (Deir Yassin — 250 Arabs killed; ambush of a Jewish medical convoy — 75 killed).
- Arab Legion (10,000) was the best-trained but the IDF grew to 100,000; Arab disunity (Abdullah’s ‘Greater Syria’ vs Egypt and Saudi Arabia).
- Israel controlled over 75% of the former Mandate; territory grew 21% beyond partition boundaries.
- Aftermath: Syria’s government overthrown (1949), King Abdullah assassinated (1951), King Farouk overthrown (1952).

**Why it matters:** Illustrates the two narratives (Independence vs Catastrophe), Arab disunity as a reason for defeat, and how Israel became a ‘garrison state’ without real security.

#### UN mediation and the 1949 Armistices (Bernadotte, Bunche) (Jun 1948–Jul 1949)

UN mediator Bernadotte secured two short truces before his assassination; Ralph Bunche’s proximity talks on Rhodes produced four armistice agreements.

- First Truce 11 Jun–9 Jul; Second Truce 18 Jul–15 Oct; IDF reorganised as Czech materiel arrived.
- Bernadotte’s plan (Galilee to Israel, Negev to Arabs, Jerusalem under UN) rejected by both; he was assassinated by the Stern Gang, 17 Sep 1948.
- Bunche’s ‘proximity talks’ on Rhodes (Jan 1949): delegations in separate hotel suites as no Arab state would negotiate directly.
- Armistices: Egypt 24 Feb, Lebanon 23 Mar, Jordan 3 Apr, Syria 20 Jul 1949; Jordan never ratified the non-belligerency pact.

**Why it matters:** The classic negative vs positive peace argument: active fighting ended, but non-recognition and territorial demands carried forward into 1956, 1967 and 1973.

#### UNTSO, Mixed Armistice Commissions, UNCCP and Lausanne (1948–1956)

The UN built observation and conciliation machinery: UNTSO and the MACs supervised the armistices, while the UNCCP’s Lausanne Conference failed to reach a final settlement.

- UNTSO established 29 May 1948 by UNSC Resolution 50; 22 officers by Aug 1949 under US Brig.-Gen. William Riley.
- MACs: equal party representatives plus a UNTSO chairman with the deciding vote — the only formal Arab–Israeli forum.
- UNCCP set up by GA Resolution 194 (11 Dec 1948); Lausanne Conference (Apr–Sep 1949) ended without agreement.
- Israel boycotted ISMC meetings from May 1951 and withdrew from the HJKIMAC in Jan 1953; by 1953 UNTSO superseded the UNCCP.

**Why it matters:** UNTSO was the most successful UN initiative of 1948–56 (Kopops et al.) but had no enforcement — evidence for consent-dependent UN machinery.

#### The Palestinian refugee problem (1948–1949)

Hundreds of thousands of Palestinians fled or were expelled; Arab states (except Jordan) would not absorb them and Israel would not readmit them.

- 200,000 had left before the Arab invasion; UN estimated 750,000 by early 1949, rising to 940,000 by June 1949, from 369 towns/villages.
- ~150,000 remained and became Israeli citizens.
- Israel blamed Arab armies for urging flight; Arabs charged deliberate expulsion / ethnic cleansing.
- UNRWA formed as a stopgap; most refugees stayed in camps for decades; UNGA Resolution 194 set principles for refugee return.

**Why it matters:** Foundational to Palestinian identity and a key contested historical debate; refugees became a destabilising force in host Arab states.

#### Rise of Nasser, fedayeen raids and the Czech Arms Deal (1952–Apr 1956)

Nasser’s Arab Socialism and defiance of the West, plus escalating fedayeen raids and Israeli reprisals, set Egypt and Israel on a collision course.

- 23 Jul 1952 Free Officers topple King Farouk; Nasser chairman of the RCC (Nov 1954), elected President (Jul 1956).
- Israeli deaths/wounded from fedayeen raids rose from 19/31 (1950) to 54/129 (1956).
- Operation Black Arrow (Gaza Raid, 28 Feb 1955) killed 38 Egyptian soldiers.
- Czech Arms Deal: 100 self-propelled guns, 200 APCs, 300 tanks, 200 MiG-15s, 50 Il-28 bombers; Israel countered with French Mystère fighters and tanks.

**Why it matters:** Supports the security cause and shows the arms race that drew in outside powers (Soviet bloc for Egypt, France for Israel).

#### Suez Crisis / Tripartite Aggression (26 Jul–7 Nov 1956)

After Nasser nationalised the canal, Britain, France and Israel colluded to invade Egypt and depose him; US pressure forced them to halt.

- 26 Jul 1956: canal nationalised to fund the Aswan Dam after the US withdrew financing.
- 29 Oct: Israel invades Sinai (Operation Kadesh); 5 Nov: Britain and France invade the Canal zone.
- Britain’s Middle East oil production share had fallen 49%→14%; France wanted Nasser gone to control the Algerian revolution.
- US motives: fear of Soviet intervention, oil, Eisenhower’s re-election on a peace platform, and not looking hypocritical after condemning Hungary.

**Why it matters:** Shows the eclipse of British/French power by the superpowers and Nasser’s political triumph despite military defeat.

#### Resolutions 997/998, Hammarskjöld and UNEF I (2 Nov 1956–17 Jun 1967)

The General Assembly demanded a ceasefire and authorised the Secretary-General to create the first UN emergency force, whose principles Hammarskjöld largely defined himself.

- UNGA Res. 997 (2 Nov, US-sponsored): ceasefire and withdrawal; Res. 998 (4 Nov): UN force authorised.
- US threatened sanctions and selling sterling reserves to collapse the pound.
- Hammarskjöld’s three principles (Summary Study): consent, impartiality, non-use of force except self-defence — ‘Chapter Six and a Half’.
- UNEF I kept a relatively quiet decade (1957–1967) but covered ~500 sq km with only 72 observation posts; U Thant had to withdraw it when Nasser asked in 1967.

**Why it matters:** The classic case that peacekeeping was born from improvisation — showing both genuine innovation and the consent-dependence that led directly to 1967.

#### Fatah, the PLO and rising tensions (Battle of Samu) (1957–Apr 1967)

Marginalised in 1948 and 1956, Palestinians revived politically; inter-Arab rivalry over the PLO and border clashes with Syria and Jordan made war seem near-inevitable.

- Fatah formed 1957 (Kuwait) under Yasser Arafat and Khalīl al-Wazīr.
- Nasser established the PLO in Jan 1964 (Cairo Arab Summit) under Ahmad al-Shukayri, largely under Egyptian control.
- 23 Feb 1966 Ba’athists take power in Syria; Nov 1966 Egypt–Syria mutual defence pact.
- 13 Nov 1966 Battle of Samu: 3 civilians and 14 Arab Legion soldiers killed; 7 Apr 1967 Israel downs six Syrian MiGs.

**Why it matters:** Links the Palestinian level of the conflict to the inter-state level and shows a slide to war that no party clearly ‘wanted’.

#### Six-Day War / al-Naksa (5–10 Jun 1967)

False Soviet intelligence, Egypt’s move into Sinai, UNEF I’s withdrawal and the closure of the Straits of Tiran led Israel to launch pre-emptive strikes and win decisively.

- 13 May: Podgorny tells Sadat Israel has mobilised against Syria (false); 18 May: UNEF I withdrawn; 22 May: Straits of Tiran closed (casus belli).
- 5 Jun: Israeli strikes destroy 304 Egyptian, 53 Syrian and 28 Jordanian aircraft.
- Israel captured East Jerusalem (7 Jun) and the Golan Heights (9 Jun); Egypt lost 12,000 soldiers, Israel 766.
- Brezhnev’s secret report (20 Jun 1967) points to Soviet miscalculation, not conspiracy; Dennis Ross: Johnson ‘missing in action’ due to Vietnam.

**Why it matters:** The pivot of the topic: air superiority and Arab disunity explain victory; useful for superpower-miscalculation and UNEF consent-dependence arguments.

#### UNSC Resolutions 234 and 242 and the 1967 aftermath (7 Jun & 22 Nov 1967)

Resolution 234 ordered a ceasefire; Resolution 242 set out the ‘land for peace’ framework that still underpins Arab–Israeli diplomacy, while the Arab states declared ‘no peace, no recognition, no negotiation’ at Khartoum.

- Res. 234 (Soviet-proposed, unanimous): ceasefire by 2000 GMT, 7 June 1967.
- Res. 242: Israeli withdrawal from occupied territories, end of belligerency, secure and recognised boundaries, navigation, just refugee settlement.
- Egypt and Jordan accepted at once; the PLO rejected it until 1988; not substantively acted on until the 1991 Madrid Conference.
- Aftermath: Israel’s territory tripled; a further 1 million Palestinians under Israeli rule, ~350,000 more refugees; Khartoum Summit (Sep 1967).

**Why it matters:** Shows how military outcomes produced the diplomatic vocabulary used afterward — but 242 was ambiguous, unenforceable and silent on Palestinian statehood and settlements.

#### War of Attrition and Palestinian extremism (1967–8 Aug 1970)

Egypt waged a low-intensity war along the Suez Canal while Palestinians, disillusioned with the Arab states, organised independently and some turned to terrorism.

- From Mar 1969: Egyptian bombardment and commando raids to dislodge Israel from Sinai and destroy the Bar Lev Line.
- Ceasefire 8 Aug 1970, after heavy losses and US agreement that Israel would respect Resolution 242.
- 350,000–400,000 more Palestinian refugees after 1967, with 665,000 under Israeli control; Arafat emerged as the decisive voice.
- The PFLP advocated terrorism in Europe/America, provoking Israeli bombing of refugee camps in Syria/Jordan.

**Why it matters:** Evidence that 1967 settled nothing — the ‘no war, no peace’ deadlock and radicalised Palestinian nationalism fed directly into 1973.

#### Sadat’s peace offer and decision for war (Feb 1971–Jan 1973)

Nasser’s successor Sadat offered peace for Sinai; when Israel refused, he planned a limited war with Syria to force negotiations on Arab terms.

- Nasser died 28 Sep 1970; Feb 1971 Sadat offered permanent peace for Israeli withdrawal from Sinai — Israel (Dayan) was not ready to compromise.
- 18 Jul 1972: Sadat expelled 15,000 Soviet advisors.
- 30 Nov 1972: Sadat and Ahmad Ismail Ali decided on war; 31 Jan 1973 Syrian and Egyptian forces under joint command.
- Aims included breaking defeatism, regaining Sinai, easing the economic burden and attracting US aid; Asad sought to regain the Golan.

**Why it matters:** Shows war as an instrument of diplomacy and Israeli over-confidence (Sharon, Sep 1973) — key to why 1973 led to peace.

#### Yom Kippur / October / Ramadan War and the oil weapon (6–24 Oct 1973)

Egypt and Syria’s surprise attack initially overran Israeli positions; a US airlift helped Israel recover, and the superpowers jointly imposed a ceasefire.

- 6 Oct 1973: IDF outnumbered 12-to-1 at the outset; Israeli intelligence failed to predict the war.
- Syrian forces repelled by 11 Oct; Israel crossed the Suez Canal by 18 Oct.
- 20 Oct Kissinger in Moscow with Brezhnev; 22 Oct joint US–Soviet ceasefire; Soviet alert over Egypt’s trapped Third Army before fighting ended 24 Oct.
- Arab OPEC members halted oil exports to the US and Netherlands and cut overall exports by 25% until 1974.

**Why it matters:** Arab forces were not decisively defeated and gained political leverage — creating conditions more conducive to negotiation than at any point since 1948.

#### Resolutions 338/339, UNEF II and UNDOF (22 Oct 1973–1979)

The Security Council ordered a ceasefire and negotiations; UNEF II separated Egypt and Israel and UNDOF supervised the Israeli–Syrian disengagement on the Golan.

- Res. 338 (22 Oct): ceasefire within 12 hours, implement 242, begin negotiations; Res. 339 (23 Oct) confirmed it.
- UNEF II (25 Oct 1973): max ~7,000 troops by 20 Feb 1974; renewed 8 times, last to 24 Jul 1979.
- UNEF II: only 5 low-level militarised disputes; 51 peacekeeper deaths over ~6 years.
- UNDOF (31 May 1974): ~1,300 peacekeepers; relied on self-reported troop figures and biweekly inspections.

**Why it matters:** Strongest evidence that peacekeeping became more effective over time — yet still dependent on the parties’ cooperation and great-power diplomacy.

#### Kissinger’s shuttle diplomacy and the 1973–75 agreements (Nov 1973–Sep 1975)

Kissinger used incremental, step-by-step disengagement deals to stabilise the fronts while reducing Soviet influence.

- 11 Nov 1973 Six-Point Agreement (Kilometre 101) — first Israel–Egypt agreement since 1949.
- Geneva Conference (21–29 Dec 1973) failed to reconvene after opening speeches.
- Sinai I (18 Jan 1974) negotiated in eight days; Israel–Syria Separation of Forces Agreement (31 May 1974).
- Sinai II (1–4 Sep 1975): Egypt regained more of Sinai incl. oilfields; Suez Canal reopened for the first time since 1967.

**Why it matters:** Laid the groundwork for Camp David, but step-by-step manipulation favouring Israel damaged Arab trust in piecemeal diplomacy.

#### Camp David Accords and the Egypt–Israel Peace Treaty (17 Sep 1978; 26 Mar 1979)

After a 12-day summit, Carter, Sadat and Begin agreed two frameworks; the 1979 treaty made Egypt the first Arab state to recognise Israel.

- Summit 5–17 Sep 1978; Carter and Vance met the delegations separately.
- Framework 1: Sinai withdrawal for full diplomatic relations and shipping rights; Framework 2: Palestinian autonomy in the West Bank (excluding Jerusalem) and Gaza.
- Treaty signed on the White House lawn, 26 Mar 1979; Sinai withdrawal completed 1982.
- Arab League boycott and HQ moved to Tunis; PLO rejected it; Sadat assassinated 6 Oct 1981; Mubarak upheld the peace.

**Why it matters:** The only durable, positive peace in the topic — but bilateral only: Palestinian statehood, refugees, Jerusalem and settlements were left unresolved (Edward Said).

### Historians

- **Fraser (2015):** Arabs and Jews had different names for the land, reflecting totally different views of its past, present and future — an Arab land entitled to independence vs the fulfilment of Jewish statehood.
- **L. Carl Brown:** The Middle East is “the most penetrated international relations subsystem in today’s world.”
- **Schulze (2017):** Zionism and Arab nationalism developed almost in parallel in the 19th century as secular ideologies of emancipation and self-determination built around identity, history, religion and culture.
- **Goldschmidt Jr. and Al-Marashi (2019):** Muslims feared Jewish actions at the Western Wall could lead to a claim on the Temple Mount; in 1948 poor Arab morale and smaller-than-expected Arab armies were major reasons for defeat.
- **Kopops et al. (2015):** From 1948 to 1956 UNTSO was the most successful UN initiative in the Middle East; its peace-observation function survived the collapse of broader peace conferences.
- **Dennis Ross (2017):** Vietnam’s demand on US policymaking attention left Johnson ‘missing in action’ between 14 May and 5 June 1967, enabling the slide to war.
- **Yapp (1991):** After 1967 the conditions for conflict resolution were not right: the Arabs could not make peace from utter defeat, Israel would not concede after victory, and the superpowers lacked the will and ability to impose peace.
- **Edward Said:** Millions of Palestinians could not be expected to go away or to acquiesce to an Israeli, Egyptian or American idea of their ‘autonomy’ — a critique of Camp David.

### Using this topic in the exam

- For ‘causes’ questions, weigh the six factors: decolonisation created the structural conditions, competing nationalisms made the conflict irreconcilable; use religion and economics as compounding factors.
- Read the conflict at two levels (Palestinian–Israeli and Arab states–Israel), with the superpowers cutting across both from the 1950s.
- For effectiveness questions, use explicit criteria: stopped fighting (negative peace)? addressed root causes (positive peace)? enforceable or consent-dependent? set a durable precedent? who bore the cost (e.g. Palestinians excluded from Camp David)?
- Trace the UN thread: UNTSO (1948) → UNEF I (1956) → Resolution 242 (1967) → UNEF II/UNDOF (1973) — more robust over time but always consent-dependent; lasting peace came from bilateral diplomacy.
- Comparative judgement: 1948–67 produced only negative peace; 1973–79 produced a lasting outcome because both sides had incentives — but only between Egypt and Israel. Pair with Yapp’s ‘conditions must be right’.
- Band 1 technique: organise thematically by actor (combatant states / superpowers / UN) and use the four wars as evidence across each theme, rather than narrating war by war.

## P1 Theme III · Indo–Pakistani conflict

*Why did conflicts occur and how did they affect the international order? How effective were the attempts to manage these conflicts?*

The Indo-Pakistani conflict arose out of the 1947 partition of British India into a Muslim-majority Pakistan and a Hindu-majority India. Between 1947 and 1972 the two states fought three wars (1947, 1965, 1971). Six interlocking causes explain it: decolonisation, security, territorial sovereignty, nationalism, religion and economic interests — all converging on Kashmir, a ¾ Muslim princely state ruled by a Hindu Maharaja.

The causes compound rather than stand alone: Britain’s rushed partition left the princely states’ status ambiguous, Hari Singh’s contested accession to India became the legal flashpoint, and Nehru’s secular ‘One Nation’ vision clashed with Jinnah’s ‘Two Nation’ theory, in which Pakistan was ‘incomplete’ without Kashmir. Kashmir’s strategic location made it a zero-sum security prize.

Each war ended with a negotiated agreement — Karachi (1949), Tashkent (1966), Simla (1972) — and UN machinery (UNCIP, UNMOGIP, UNIPOM) supervised ceasefire lines. But the superpowers moved from minimal involvement (1947) to self-interested mediation (1965) to committed bloc alignment (1971), while the UN declined from meaningful if limited machinery to paralysis by veto.

Overall verdict: conflict management was reasonably effective at the tactical level — ending hostilities, fixing ceasefire lines, returning troops and POWs — but ineffective at the strategic level. No agreement resolved Kashmir’s sovereignty or held the promised plebiscite, so each ceasefire was a pause, leading to the 1989 Kashmir insurgency.

**Causes: six interlocking factors**

- Decolonisation: Britain’s rushed, inadequate framework for integrating princely states (esp. Kashmir) into the new dominions.
- Security: Kashmir as a strategic buffer bordering Afghanistan, China and the USSR — whoever held it held military advantage.
- Territorial sovereignty: the contested legality of the Instrument of Accession and Pakistan’s irredentist claim.
- Nationalism and religion: secular ‘One Nation’ (Nehru) vs religiously-defined ‘Two Nations’ (Jinnah); Hindu–Muslim mutual distrust.
- Economic interests: Indus water dependency created an upstream–downstream power asymmetry, though the 1960 treaty contained it.

**Combatant states across the three wars**

- 1947: Pakistan backed the tribal invasion via ‘Azad Kashmir’ forces; India airlifted troops after a conditional accession and referred the dispute to the UNSC.
- 1965: Pakistan’s ‘now or never’ Operation Gibraltar and Grand Slam; India counter-attacked across the LoC and opened a Punjab front.
- 1971: India backed the Mukti Bahini and declared war after Operation Chengiz Khan; Pakistan surrendered in Dhaka after 13 days.
- Kashmiris: the Maharaja acceded under duress; in 1965 the local populace refused to join the planned uprising.

**The superpowers and China**

- 1950s: US–Pakistan alliance (1954 MDAA, SEATO, CENTO) pushed non-aligned India toward the USSR (1955 Khrushchev recognition, 1958 Soviet veto).
- 1965: US arms embargo on both; USSR mediated at Tashkent; both superpowers chiefly sought to keep China out.
- 1971: US (and China) backed Pakistan — USS Enterprise to the Bay of Bengal; USSR backed India — 1971 Friendship Treaty, arms, UNSC vetoes.
- Double-edged: superpower pressure helped end wars faster, but patronage and arms emboldened both states to escalate.

**The UN and conflict management**

- 1947–49: UNSC Res. 38/39/47/51, UNCIP and UNMOGIP — observation only, no enforcement power.
- 1965: escalating Res. 209 → 210 → 211, U Thant’s personal mediation and the temporary, undermanned UNIPOM.
- 1971: UNSC paralysed by the Soviet veto; after Simla, UNMOGIP’s mandate disputed.
- Structural limits: unarmed observers, no Art. 42-style mandate, the sovereign equality principle (Art. 2(1)) and chronic under-resourcing.

### Case studies

#### Partition of British India and the Radcliffe Line (Jul–Aug 1947)

Mountbatten concluded Congress–Muslim League differences were irreconcilable; the Indian Independence Act set a midnight 14–15 Aug 1947 deadline and Radcliffe hastily drew the borders.

- Indian Independence Act (18 Jul 1947); Radcliffe Boundary Commission of 4 Congress + 4 Muslim League members, chaired by Cyril Radcliffe, who had never visited India.
- Power transferred 14 Aug (Pakistan) and 15 Aug (India); borders not published until 17 Aug.
- ~15 million Hindus, Sikhs and Muslims crossed the borders; 14–16 million eventually displaced; deaths estimated 200,000 to 2 million.
- Kashmir was one of 565 princely states pressured by Mountbatten to accede by 15 Aug 1947.

**Why it matters:** Core evidence for decolonisation as a cause: Britain’s ‘cut and run’ departure and failure to devise a method for integrating princely states.

#### Poonch rebellion and the tribal invasion of Kashmir (Aug–22 Oct 1947)

A revolt in Poonch, backed by Pakistan with arms and transport, grew into an invasion of Pathan tribals and Pakistani regulars in mufti advancing on Srinagar.

- Kashmir signed a Standstill Agreement with Pakistan; none was concluded with India.
- Aug 1947: open revolt in Poonch; Azad Kashmir government organisation begins.
- Early Sep 1947: Pathan raiders attack from Pakistani territory with Pakistani Army arms, communications and transport.
- 22 Oct: the invading column captures Muzaffarabad; Muslim troops of the J&K State Forces defect and massacre Dogra counterparts.

**Why it matters:** Shows Pakistan’s irredentism and role in the outbreak; Pakistan framed it as a spontaneous uprising, India as Pakistani instigation.

#### Kashmir’s accession to India (25–27 Oct 1947)

Facing the invasion, Maharaja Hari Singh appealed to India, which made aid conditional on accession; his signature triggered the First War and a lasting legal dispute.

- Nehru’s two conditions: support from Sheikh Abdullah’s National Conference, and provisional signing of the Instrument of Accession.
- Instrument signed 26 Oct; accepted the next day with the proviso of a ‘reference to the people’ — a provisional accession.
- 27 Oct 1947: Indian 161st Infantry Brigade airlifted into Srinagar.
- Jinnah called the accession ‘fraudulent’ (signed under duress, Standstill Agreement breached); India held the tribal invasion voided the Standstill Agreement.

**Why it matters:** The contested legal act at the heart of the territorial-sovereignty cause; Kashmir was contiguous to both dominions (Ganguly, 2019).

#### First Indo-Pakistani War (First Kashmir War) (22 Oct 1947–1 Jan 1949)

The Indian Army fought ‘Azad Kashmir’ forces increasingly reinforced by Pakistani regulars until a stalemate split Jammu & Kashmir roughly in two.

- Pakistan’s Gilgit-based offensive failed to take Kargil or hold Ladakh/Baltistan against Indian tank counter-offensives.
- By end-Dec 1947 stalemate: Pakistan held Gilgit, Baltistan and a western strip; India held Ladakh, most of Kashmir Province, and Jammu.
- India lacked the military resources and political will to force a complete Pakistani withdrawal.
- Ceasefire 1 Jan 1949; ~2,000 casualties on each side; India held 54% of Kashmir, Pakistan 46%.

**Why it matters:** Ganguly traces its origins to competing ideologies, Pakistani irredentism, Kashmir’s strategic location and Britain’s inadequate transfer of power.

#### UNSC referral, Resolutions 38/39/47/51 and UNCIP (1 Jan 1948–Jan 1949)

India referred the dispute to the UNSC, which set up a commission (UNCIP) and proposed a withdrawal-then-plebiscite process that neither side completed.

- 1 Jan 1948 referral under Articles 34 and 35; Res. 38 (17 Jan) and Res. 39 (20 Jan) — a 3-member commission.
- Res. 47 (21 Apr 1948): UNCIP expanded to 5 members; 3 steps — Pakistan withdraws, India reduces to law-and-order minimum, UN-run plebiscite.
- UNCIP Resolution (13 Aug 1948): two-part withdrawal — accepted by India, effectively rejected by Pakistan.
- Res. 51 (3 Jun 1948) directed the Commission to the disputed areas; the ceasefire was helped by British officers commanding both armies.

**Why it matters:** Gave an international forum and a proposed solution, but the UNSC could persuade not compel (Art. 2(1)); UNCIP could only ‘investigate and mediate’.

#### UNMOGIP and the Karachi Agreement (Jan–31 Jul 1949)

UN military observers arrived to supervise the ceasefire, and the Karachi Agreement fixed the ceasefire line that held until 1965.

- First UNMOGIP observers arrived Jan 1949; formalised by the 27 Jul 1949 Karachi Agreement; continued under UNSC Res. 91 (30 Mar 1951).
- An 830 km ceasefire line from Manawar through Keran to the glacier area; troops kept ≥500 yards from the line.
- Disputes referred to the UNCIP Military Adviser, whose decision was final.
- Never more than ~45 observers for the entire ceasefire line; unarmed, observation and reporting only.

**Why it matters:** Prevented full-scale war for 17 years (1948–1965) but left Kashmir’s status unresolved — the classic ‘ceasefire not settlement’ evidence.

#### The plebiscite stalemate and Kashmir’s integration into India (1949–1957)

Both sides agreed to a plebiscite in principle but never held one, while India progressively integrated its part of Kashmir.

- India insisted on full withdrawal of tribal/Pakistani forces first; Pakistan refused unless India withdrew too.
- 1951: Sheikh Abdullah’s National Conference won J&K Constituent Assembly elections; 1951 constitution limited India’s jurisdiction to foreign affairs, defence and communications (ratified in the 1952 Delhi Accords).
- 1954: accession ratified by the state’s constituent assembly; 1957: Indian-held area became the state of Jammu and Kashmir.
- Growing superpower patronage later emboldened both states — setting the stage for 1965.

**Why it matters:** The unheld plebiscite is the constant thread connecting all three wars.

#### Superpower alignments: US–Pakistan and Soviet–India (1947–1961)

Pakistan joined US-led alliances as part of the ring around the USSR; non-aligned India drifted toward Moscow.

- 14 May 1954 Mutual Defense Assistance Agreement: US aid of ~US$80 million/year; Pakistan joined SEATO (1954) and the Baghdad Pact/CENTO (1955).
- India a founding member of the Non-Aligned Movement (1961 Belgrade Conference).
- 1951: USSR sent 50,000 tonnes of wheat; 1955: Khrushchev publicly recognised Kashmir’s accession to India.
- Late 1958: USSR vetoed a UNSC resolution on the crisis.

**Why it matters:** Shows how the Cold War internationalised the dispute and how patronage later emboldened both sides.

#### Indus waters dispute and the Indus Waters Treaty (Apr 1948; 1960)

Partition gave India control of upstream barrages on rivers Pakistan depended on; a 1960 treaty divided the rivers.

- The Indus floodplain supports ~90% of Pakistan’s food and 65% of its employment.
- Apr 1948: East Punjab engineers cut water supplies to a key Pakistani canal.
- 1960 Indus Waters Treaty: western rivers (Indus, Jhelum, Chenab) to Pakistan; eastern rivers (Ravi, Beas, Sutlej) to India; funded Tarbela and Mangla Dams.
- No ‘water wars’ since, despite multiple military conflicts.

**Why it matters:** Economic-interest cause (Wirsing & Jasparro, 2006) — and a counterpoint showing resource cooperation persisted amid hostility.

#### Sino-Indian War (context only — not examinable) (20 Oct–20 Nov 1962)

China defeated India over the Himalayan border (Aksai Chin and the Northeast Frontier Agency), with consequences for the Indo-Pakistani balance.

- Ended with a unilateral Chinese ceasefire after capturing both disputed areas; China retained Aksai Chin.
- India’s military modernisation made it the world’s 3rd-largest military — crucial in 1965 and 1971.
- 1963 China–Pakistan Border Treaty improved Pakistan–China relations.
- Pakistan wrongly believed India was still weakened, helping trigger 1965.

**Why it matters:** Context for Pakistan’s 1965 miscalculation and for the China factor in superpower calculations.

#### Rann of Kutch and Pakistan’s ‘now or never’ mentality (Apr 1965)

Pakistan seized posts in the disputed Rann of Kutch and concluded Indian morale was low, while other avenues to win Kashmir seemed exhausted.

- India retreated in the Rann of Kutch due to poor logistics, reinforcing Pakistan’s mistaken belief in low Indian morale.
- Negotiations had yielded little; great powers were tiring of the dispute; 1963 UK/US-brokered talks failed and Pakistan went to the UNSC in 1964.
- Kashmir was being progressively integrated into India.
- India’s post-1962 build-up threatened to close Pakistan’s window for military action.

**Why it matters:** Explains the outbreak of 1965 as a Pakistani strategic gamble based on miscalculation (Schofield, 2010).

#### Operation Gibraltar, Operation Grand Slam and the Second War (5 Aug–22 Sep 1965)

Pakistan infiltrated J&K to spark a Kashmiri uprising; when locals did not rise, India counter-attacked, Pakistan launched Grand Slam, and India opened a Punjab front before a stalemate.

- Infiltrators: Indian estimate 30,000–40,000; Pakistani estimate 5,000–7,000.
- India took the Haji Pir Pass; Operation Grand Slam (1 Sep) targeted the Akhnoor Bridge; 6 Sep India attacked toward Lahore and Sialkot.
- India lost 65–70 aircraft, 175–190 tanks, ~3,000 casualties; Pakistan ~20 aircraft, 200 tanks, ~3,800 men.
- Largest tank battle since WWII; ceasefire accepted by India 21 Sep and Pakistan 22 Sep.

**Why it matters:** Clear evidence of Pakistan triggering the outbreak and of the war ending in stalemate without resolving Kashmir.

#### Superpowers, China and other players in 1965 (Aug–Sep 1965)

The US embargoed arms to both sides and backed UN action; the USSR shifted from obstruction to offering ‘good offices’; China threatened India.

- US military embargo on both nations — a major blow to Pakistan, whose hardware was mostly US-supplied.
- Kosygin offered Soviet ‘good offices’ from 4 Sep; his third offer (17 Sep) led to Tashkent.
- China’s ultimatum (18 Sep 1965) demanded India dismantle military works on the China–Sikkim boundary.
- Turkey and Iran offered troops for a UN force (10 Sep); Dean Rusk (8 Sep) welcomed Soviet support for UN efforts.

**Why it matters:** Superpower motives were self-interested — chiefly excluding China; military exhaustion after the embargo mattered more than mediation.

#### U Thant, Resolutions 209–211 and UNIPOM (31 Aug 1965–Mar 1966)

The Secretary-General’s personal mediation failed, but escalating UNSC resolutions culminating in Res. 211 secured a ceasefire, supervised by the new UNIPOM.

- U Thant visited Rawalpindi and New Delhi 9–15 Sep; his ceasefire appeals of 12 and 14 Sep failed.
- Res. 209 (4 Sep) ignored — ‘a scrap of paper’; Res. 210 (6 Sep) widened the SG’s mandate; Res. 211 (20 Sep) demanded a ceasefire effective 22 Sep 0700 GMT.
- UNIPOM’s first observers arrived 23 Sep; ~90 observers for over 1,000 miles of border.
- Early Soviet obstruction delayed UNSC intervention in late August.

**Why it matters:** Shows firmer UN action backed by rare major-power unity, yet still unable to compel compliance (Art. 2(1)) and badly under-resourced.

#### Tashkent Declaration (10 Jan 1966)

Shastri and Ayub Khan, mediated by Kosygin, agreed to withdraw to pre-war positions and renounce force.

- Withdraw all armed personnel to pre-5 Aug 1965 positions by 25 Feb 1966; withdrawal supervision finished by end-March 1966.
- Pledges: peaceful settlement, non-interference, restore high commissioners, repatriate POWs.
- Pakistan achieved none of its war aims; student riots after Ayub Khan’s return.
- Aftermath: India deepened alignment with the USSR; Pakistan turned increasingly to China.

**Why it matters:** Enshrined non-use of force but had limited substantive significance — Kashmir’s status remained unresolved.

#### East Pakistani dissatisfaction and the Bangladesh Liberation War (1966–1971)

West Pakistan’s failure to accommodate the East culminated in the blocked 1970 election result, brutal repression and India’s backing of Bengali independence.

- East Pakistanis saw Kashmir as a West Pakistani cause that left them needlessly vulnerable in 1965 (Ganguly, 1998).
- 1970 elections: Awami League won 167 of 169 East Pakistani seats; blocked by the Army and Bhutto; Mujibur Rahman jailed.
- 27 Mar 1971: Ziaur Rahman declares independence; ~10 million refugees flee to India; Mukti Bahini trained in Indian camps.
- A US-sponsored UNSC ceasefire resolution (introduced by George H.W. Bush) was vetoed by the USSR.

**Why it matters:** Shows the 1971 war arising from Pakistan’s internal crisis and India’s intervention — the unravelling of Pakistan’s raison d’être.

#### Third Indo-Pakistani War and Operation Chengiz Khan (3–17 Dec 1971)

Pakistan’s pre-emptive air strikes brought India formally into the war; within 13 days Pakistan’s Eastern Command surrendered and Bangladesh was formed.

- Operation Chengiz Khan (3 Dec): strikes on eight Indian air stations; India held air superiority from 4 Dec.
- 16 Dec 1971: surrender in Dhaka; 17 Dec Indira Gandhi’s unilateral ceasefire, reciprocated by Yahya Khan.
- ~93,000 Pakistani servicemen taken prisoner.
- 300,000 to 3,000,000 civilians estimated killed in Bangladesh; a further 8–10 million fled to India.

**Why it matters:** Comprehensive Pakistani defeat created a power asymmetry and undermined the idea of Pakistan as a single Muslim homeland.

#### The superpowers and China in 1971 (Aug–Dec 1971)

Rival blocs formed — the US and China behind Pakistan, the USSR behind India — and both superpowers made naval shows of force.

- Indo-Soviet Friendship Treaty (9 Aug 1971); Soviet arms worth ~$730 million.
- Nixon routed supplies to Pakistan via Jordan and Iran, violating Congressional sanctions, while pursuing rapprochement with China.
- 11 Dec 1971: USS Enterprise sent to the Bay of Bengal; the Soviets sent nuclear-missile-armed ships and a nuclear submarine in response.
- China gave aid of lesser quality than Soviet aid to India and did not intervene militarily.

**Why it matters:** Superpowers were not neutral mediators: Cold War rivalry emboldened allies to escalate and paralysed the UNSC (Mahmood’s three Soviet motives).

#### Simla Agreement and UNMOGIP after Simla (2 Jul 1972 onward)

India and Pakistan agreed to settle differences bilaterally and recognised the Line of Control, sidelining third-party and UN mediation.

- Pakistan recognised Bangladesh; India returned captured territory and the 93,000 POWs without war-crimes trials.
- Differences to be settled ‘by peaceful means through bilateral negotiations’ (at India’s insistence).
- 1948 and 1971 ceasefire lines recognised as the Line of Control (LoC).
- India held UNMOGIP’s mandate had lapsed and has lodged no complaints since Jan 1972; Pakistan disagreed and still lodges complaints.

**Why it matters:** Brought a longer period of relative peace but no mechanism or timeline for Kashmir — which re-emerged as the 1989 insurgency (Ganguly, 2001).

### Historians

- **Ganguly (2019):** Kashmir was contiguous to both dominions, so the partition’s own accession logic created a genuine dilemma.
- **Ganguly (2001):** Congress and the Muslim League had diametrically opposed bases, so the two states were ‘already locked into a potential collision course’; uneven Indian policy after 1984 culminated in the 1989 insurgency.
- **Ganguly (1998):** For Nehru, Kashmir’s integration proved secularism; for Jinnah, Pakistan was ‘incomplete’ without it; after 1965 East Pakistanis saw Kashmir as a West Pakistani cause.
- **Ganguly:** The First War had four sources: competing ideologies, Pakistani irredentism, Kashmir’s strategic location, and Britain’s insufficient arrangements for the transfer of power.
- **Hewitt (1997):** India and Pakistan shared a deep ‘mutual distrust’ from partition onward.
- **Burke (1974):** The distrust stemmed from the antithetical nature of Hinduism and Islam and Britain’s ‘cut and run’ departure.
- **Wirsing & Jasparro (2006):** Control of the Indus River system has been a persistent source of tension.
- **Schofield (2010):** Pakistan hoped reactivating Kashmir through rebellion would oblige Western intervention, and believed Indian morale was low after the Rann of Kutch.
- **Mahmood:** The USSR backed Bangladesh’s independence to strengthen India against China, pursue Indian Ocean naval bases, and gain a South Asian foothold.

### Using this topic in the exam

- Show how the six causes compound — religion and nationalism reinforced each other, while decolonisation created the legal/territorial ambiguity that security and sovereignty concerns exploited — rather than listing them.
- Overall verdict: management was effective tactically (ceasefires, withdrawals, POW exchanges) but not strategically — none of Karachi, Tashkent or Simla resolved Kashmir’s sovereignty; the plebiscite question runs through all three wars.
- Agreements depended on military exhaustion at signing rather than reconciliation — ceasefires held only until the next perceived window (Pakistan in 1965, India’s advantage in 1971).
- UN peacekeeping was structurally toothless: unarmed observers, no Art. 42-style mandate, sovereign equality (Art. 2(1)), and too few observers (UNMOGIP ~45, UNIPOM ~90–100) for a 1,000+ mile border.
- Use the frameworks: phase of conflict (outbreak / escalation / de-escalation), management lens (Karachi → Tashkent → Simla; UNMOGIP → UNIPOM → paralysis), or actor-centred (combatants, superpowers, UN).
- Pitfall: the Sino-Indian War (1962) is context only, not examinable — use it only to explain Pakistan’s 1965 miscalculation and the China factor.

## P1 Theme III · Congo Crisis

*Why did conflicts occur and how did they affect the international order? How effective were the attempts to manage these conflicts?*

The Congo Crisis (1960–1965) was a period of political upheaval and civil war in the newly independent Republic of the Congo that doubled as a Cold War proxy conflict between the US and USSR. In Kent’s words, “the demands of the Cold War had to confront the requirements of decolonisation in an explosive socio-economic and political mix.” Around 100,000 people are believed to have died in a country of ~14 million divided into 200+ ethnic groups.

The crisis is periodised into two phases. Phase 1 (1960–1963) was fragmentation: the Force Publique mutiny (5 Jul 1960), the secessions of Katanga and South Kasai, and the Kasavubu–Lumumba constitutional deadlock that let Mobutu seize power. Phase 2 (1963–1965) was insurrection against the central government: the Kwilu and Simba rebellions, fuelled by socio-economic discontent and the Lumumbist legacy.

The causes interlock: hasty Belgian decolonisation left no prepared elite; regional/ethnic parties split over federalism vs centralism; foreign mining interests (UMHK, Forminière) bankrolled secession; and colonial divide-and-rule had politicised ethnic identities. External actors then turned a domestic breakdown into an international one.

Conflict management was mixed. ONUC — then the largest UN operation — and U Thant’s forceful turn ended the secessions militarily (Operation Grandslam), but durable political settlement failed: the Luluabourg Constitution and the 1965 elections fed into, rather than prevented, Mobutu’s second coup, and Phase 2 was dominated by unilateral US/Belgian action and mercenaries.

**Causes — Decolonisation**

- Belgian rule rested on the “colonial trinity” of state, missionary and private-company interests, with a paternalist attitude that gave Africans no legislative role.
- Belgium failed to prepare the Congo: Western moral principles were placed above political education; Van Bilsen’s 1955 Thirty-Year Plan was dismissed (critics suggested he be tried for treason).
- Jan 1959 Leopoldville riots made Belgium capitulate; De Schrijver’s Oct 1959 four-year limit still caused the Nov 1959 disturbances.
- Round Table Conference (Jan 1960) set independence for 30 Jun 1960 — “an experiment in instant decolonisation.”
- Namikas (2013): colonialism left the Congo exploited, underdeveloped and poorly governed.

**Causes — Domestic Politics**

- 1950s parties were mostly regional/tribal/cultural, divided over centralism vs federalism, though all sought independence.
- MNC (est. 10 Oct 1958, Lumumba) was the only genuinely national party; its Jul 1959 split produced MNC-L (centralist) and MNC-K (Kalonji, Baluba) — the seed of the South Kasai secession.
- ABAKO (est. 1950, Kasavubu) was Bakongo-based, tied to the Kimbanguist Church, and federalist.
- CONAKAT (Tshombe from 1959) wanted a loose confederation and Katangan self-government, financed by Belgian interests via UMHK.

**Causes — Economic Interests**

- Copper, diamond, gold, uranium reserves; by 1960 ~60% of the Western world’s cobalt supply.
- Katanga accounted for 75% of Congo’s mineral output; taxes there were 47% of Congo’s tax income; UMHK controlled ~70% of Katangan mineral production.
- The colonial economy was export-led and served Belgian profit: forced labour, a 1917 decree requiring 60 days a year of agricultural work, 4–7 year indentured contracts (1922 law).
- Kent (2010): “in economic terms the fundamental problem was not essentially of African making.”

**Causes — Ethnic & Religious Nationalisms**

- Belgian chiefdoms entrenched local identities that nationalists later politicised.
- Bakongo vs Upper River People in Leopoldville: ABAKO framed anticolonialism along ethno-linguistic lines (Kikongo vs Lingala).
- Lulua vs Baluba: colonial rule arbitrarily split a Tshiluba-speaking people, first favouring Baluba (1930s) then Lulua (1950s, e.g. Lulua Frères, 1952).
- “Native Katangese” vs “Strangers”: Lulua/Baluba migrants were 38% of Katanga’s population by 1960; BALUBAKAT (1957) aligned with Lumumba against secession.

**Conflict management — the UN’s three objectives**

- Five UNSC resolutions set ONUC’s objectives: (1) Belgian withdrawal (143, 145, 146, 161); (2) withdrawal of foreign personnel/mercenaries (161, 169); (3) internal stability (143, 145, 161, 169).
- Mandate intensified over time: calling for withdrawal → force as a last resort (Res 161) → “requisite measure of force” against mercenaries (Res 169).
- Hammarskjöld’s strict neutrality contrasted with U Thant’s readiness to use force.
- Impartiality was contested: once the government collapsed, ONUC had no clear authority to give or remove consent.

### Case studies

#### Belgian rule and hasty decolonisation (1908–Jun 1960)

Belgium ruled the Congo paternalistically without political preparation, then, after the Jan 1959 Leopoldville riots, rushed to grant independence within 18 months.

- Belgian colony 1908–1960, replacing the Congo Free State after international outrage over abuses.
- Dec 1955: Van Bilsen’s Thirty-Year Plan called for gradual transfer of power — most Belgians rejected it.
- 4–7 Jan 1959 riots; 13 Jan 1959 Van Hemelrijek proclaimed independence the goal — “without fatal procrastination, yet without fatal haste.”
- Round Table Conference, Brussels (Jan 1960): 16 resolutions; independence 30 Jun 1960.

**Why it matters:** Core evidence for decolonisation as a cause: an unprepared state with no trained elite descended into chaos within days of independence (“instant decolonisation”).

#### Party politics and ethnic rivalries before independence (1950–Jun 1960)

Ethnic/regional parties formed and split over centralism vs federalism, while colonially-politicised ethnic tensions (Lulua–Baluba, Bakongo–Upper River, Katangese–“strangers”) sharpened.

- MNC est. 10 Oct 1958; split Jul 1959 into MNC-L and MNC-K.
- MNC-L won 23% of the vote (Lecture 10) / 36 of 137 Chamber seats (Lecture 9) in the May 1960 elections.
- Dec 1957 Baluba won Luluabourg municipal elections; Aug 1959 Baluba demonstrations violently repressed.
- Lulua leaders proposed evicting 100,000 Baluba farmers back to South Kasai.

**Why it matters:** Shows domestic politics and ethnic nationalism as causes — the federalist–centralist divide underlay both secessions, and the Lulua–Baluba conflict led directly to South Kasai.

#### Force Publique mutiny, Belgian intervention and ONUC’s creation (5–16 Jul 1960)

Days after independence the army mutinied against its Belgian officers; Belgian troops intervened without permission and the Congo appealed to the UN, which created ONUC.

- 5 Jul 1960: Force Publique mutiny at Thysville spreads nationwide.
- 9–10 Jul 1960: Belgian troops intervene unilaterally.
- Hammarskjöld invoked Article 99 for the first time in UN history; UNSC Res 143 authorised ONUC.
- US Military Air Transport Service moved ~9,000 UN troops in about two weeks using ~50 C-124s.

**Why it matters:** Achievement of UN conflict management: near-immediate deployment on the government’s request — but Res 143 gave no clear guidance, so all parties later criticised the Secretariat.

#### Secession of Katanga (11 Jul 1960–Jan 1963)

Tshombe declared the richest province independent, backed by UMHK and Belgian settlers, accusing the central government of communism.

- Katanga: 40–50% of Congo’s revenue/foreign trade; 32,000 Belgian settlers by the 1950s.
- UMHK advanced Tshombe 1,250 million Belgian francs (~US$25 million in 1960) and supplied 80% of Katanga’s revenues.
- UMHK engaged René Clemens to draft Katanga’s constitution, set up its bank and currency.
- Belgium, France and Britain supported Katanga in practice though not in name; European mercenaries served in its army.

**Why it matters:** Key case for economic interests and external actors as causes; Brussels justified support in Cold War terms. Ended only by UN force (Grandslam).

#### Hammarskjöld’s Katanga diplomacy and strict neutrality (Jul–Sep 1960)

Hammarskjöld negotiated peaceful UN entry into Katanga but refused Lumumba’s demands that ONUC subdue the secession, citing non-interference in internal politics.

- Res 146 (9 Aug 1960) reaffirmed Belgian withdrawal from Katanga but said ONUC would not influence internal conflict.
- Brought two companies of the Swedish battalion to talks with Tshombe; Belgian troops fully withdrew from Katanga by early September.
- 17 Jul 1960 Lumumba ultimatum: Congo “may be obligated to call upon the Soviet Union to intervene.”
- Lumumba–Hammarskjöld relationship “degenerated thereafter.”

**Why it matters:** Shows the limits of impartial mediation: success on Belgian withdrawal, but neutrality pushed Lumumba towards the USSR and drew criticism from non-aligned/communist states.

#### Secession of South Kasai and the Bakwanga massacres (9 Aug 1960–Oct 1962)

Kalonji declared the “Autonomous State of South Kasai” as a Baluba homeland; Lumumba’s ANC offensive led to massacres that triggered the wider political crisis.

- Declared 9 Aug 1960; backed by Forminière (diamond mining), which received mining concessions.
- “Autonomous State” framing signalled a federally-governed region, not rejection of Congolese sovereignty.
- 23–26 Aug 1960: ANC seized Bakwanga; massacres killed ~3,000 civilians — Hammarskjöld called it “a case of incipient genocide.”
- Ended Oct 1962 after an internal coup led by Albert Kankolongo.

**Why it matters:** Causal link between the secessions and the political crisis (Lumumba’s dismissal, Mobutu’s coup) — shows causes and phases are interconnected.

#### Constitutional deadlock and Mobutu’s first coup (5 Sep 1960–1961)

Kasavubu dismissed Lumumba, who in turn declared Kasavubu deposed; Mobutu’s coup “neutralised” both while siding with Kasavubu, splitting the country four ways.

- 5 Sep 1960: Kasavubu said Lumumba had “plunged the nation into fratricidal war.”
- 14 Sep 1960: Mobutu’s coup expelled Soviet/Czech/Communist bloc personnel.
- 18–24 Sep 1960: ceasefire with UN; ANC withdrew from South Kasai.
- Four sections: Leopoldville government, Katanga, Kasai, and Gizenga’s Stanleyville government (UN recognised Mobutu’s).

**Why it matters:** Domestic politics as a driver of fragmentation; shows how Cold War alignment (Mobutu backed by Belgium and the CIA) shaped who held power.

#### US covert action and the murder of Lumumba (Aug 1960–Jan 1961)

After being rebuffed by the US, Lumumba requested Soviet aid; the Eisenhower administration authorised plans to eliminate him, and he was handed to Katangan enemies and murdered.

- 28 Jul 1960: US Secretary Herter insisted all aid go through the UN.
- 15 Aug 1960: Lumumba formally requested Soviet military aid.
- CIA Station Chief Devlin described a “classic Communist takeover”; Aug 1960 covert programme lasting ~7 years.
- Lumumba captured 1 Dec 1960; murdered in Katanga 17 Jan 1961.
- Khrushchev (23 Dec 1960) blamed the UN and proposed a “troika” to replace the Secretary-General.

**Why it matters:** Shows the Cold War internationalising the crisis; Soviet aid arguably followed US rejection rather than Soviet ambition. Lumumba’s death damaged the West and the UN’s neutrality.

#### Gizenga’s Stanleyville regime, Lovanium and the Adoula government (1961–1962)

Gizenga’s Soviet-backed “Free Republic of the Congo” dissolved to join UN-sponsored Lovanium talks; Kennedy backed Adoula, who briefly united moderates and radicals.

- Adoula named PM 2 Aug 1961, with Gizenga as deputy PM; CIA financially supported his candidacy.
- Lovanium talks did not deliver a Lumumbist government; Gizenga was arrested and imprisoned.
- Gizenga expelled from government by 1962; many Lumumbists went into exile.
- Kennedy sought to distance the US from Belgian “recolonisation.”

**Why it matters:** A partial political settlement whose exclusion of Lumumbists sowed the Phase 2 rebellions.

#### Resolution 161 and Operations Rum Punch and Morthor (21 Feb–Sep 1961)

After Lumumba’s death, Res 161 allowed force as a last resort; ONUC moved against Katanga’s mercenaries, with mixed results and Cold War controversy.

- Rum Punch (28 Aug 1961), under Brig.-Gen. K.A.S. Raja: peaceful surrender of 81 foreign mercenaries.
- Morthor (13 Sep 1961): Indian brigade in an 8-day engagement; mercenaries counterattacked.
- Morthor executed without full approval of the UK, France and US; USSR saw ONUC as a US proxy.
- 17 Sep 1961: Hammarskjöld died in a plane crash near Ndola en route to ceasefire talks with Tshombe.

**Why it matters:** Shows the mandate’s evolution and the limits of force under contested authority; evidence for the critique of lost UN neutrality.

#### Resolution 169, Operation Unokat and the Kitona Declaration (Nov–Dec 1961)

Under U Thant, Res 169 authorised the “requisite measure of force” against mercenaries; Unokat pressured Tshombe into the Kitona Declaration, which he did not implement.

- Res 169 (24 Nov 1961) fully condemned the secession.
- The US broke with the European powers to support Res 169, providing planes, trucks and weapons.
- Unokat (5 Dec 1961) captured Camp Massart (16 Dec).
- 21 Dec 1961: Kitona Declaration recognising central authority — not carried out.

**Why it matters:** Illustrates the shift to a more robust UN stance and US–UN convergence, but also Tshombe’s stalling — military pressure without political compliance.

#### U Thant’s reconciliation plan and Operation Grandslam (Aug 1962–Jan 1963)

U Thant’s Plan for National Reconciliation failed; after a Katangese attack on peacekeepers he ordered Grandslam, which decisively ended the Katanga secession.

- Aug 1962: Plan for National Reconciliation for Katanga to join a federalised Congo — in reality a set of US proposals.
- 24 Dec 1962: Tshombe’s mercenaries attack UN peacekeepers; Grandslam launched 28 Dec 1962.
- Aim: “complete freedom of movement for ONUC all over Katanga.”
- 17 Jan 1963: Tshombe/Munongo signed a formal instrument of surrender in Élisabethville.

**Why it matters:** The strongest evidence of effective UN management at the military/territorial level; contrast with Hammarskjöld’s neutrality. But U Thant’s acquiescence to US pressure (Greene Plan) undercuts impartiality.

#### ONUC phase-out and withdrawal (Feb 1963–30 Jun 1964)

After Katanga, ONUC was wound down, shrinking sharply just as new rebellions began, and fully left in mid-1964.

- Phase-out began Feb 1963; reduced presence extended 6 months at the government’s request.
- By Jan 1964, ONUC had shrunk from ~20,000 to ~5,500 personnel.
- Last peacekeepers (85 Nigerian, 58 Canadian) left N’djili Airport 30 Jun 1964.
- ~2,000 civilian experts at the peak (1963–64) — the largest UN assistance programme to that point.

**Why it matters:** The UN “strengthened the hand of the central government and kept the country together,” but its departure left Phase 2 to unilateral Great Power action.

#### Kwilu rebellion (“Second Independence”) (Aug 1963–Dec 1965)

Mulele, a Maoist-trained former Lumumba minister, led a revolutionary rising of disenfranchised youth in Kwilu, suppressed brutally by the ANC, mercenaries and Belgian paratroopers.

- By late 1963 the Congolese franc had plummeted, schools were overcrowded and unemployment high.
- Escalated 16 Jan 1964 in Idiofa/Gungu; rebels believed bullets would “turn to water.”
- ONUC rescued over 100 missionaries (Mar–Apr 1964) but played no other role.
- 350 Belgian paratroopers and mercenaries; the “National Butchery of Kikwit” killed over 3,000 civilians in a day (per Col. Joseph Monzimba).

**Why it matters:** Shows socio-economic causes and the failure of the post-1960 settlement; UN limited to rescue, so management fell to force and foreign intervention.

#### Simba rebellion and external involvement (Oct 1963–Dec 1964)

Lumumbist successors of the CNL seized almost half the country, but disunity and lack of strategy, plus US, Belgian and mercenary support for Tshombe’s government, defeated them.

- CNL founded 3 Oct 1963 in Brazzaville by Gbenye and Soumialot; Uvira taken 15 May 1964, Kindu 22 Jul 1964, Stanleyville 4 Aug 1964.
- Peak (Sep 1964): almost half the country and 7 of 21 local capitals.
- 24 Apr 1964: Johnson gave the Congolese Air Force 6 T-28s, 10 C-47s, 6 H-21s.
- China (Zhou Enlai, Liu Shaoqi) gave stronger support than the USSR; East Germany sent aid worth 3 million deutsche marks; Che Guevara’s Cubans trained Simbas.
- Tshombe recalled as PM 30 Jul 1964 with mercenaries and his Katangese Gendarmerie.

**Why it matters:** Shows Phase 2 Cold War dynamics (Sino-Soviet split, converged US–European interests, little US hesitation over force) and that unilateral action, not the UN, dominated.

#### Luluabourg Constitution (1 Aug 1964–Nov 1965)

Kasavubu pushed through a new constitution with a strong presidency and 21 provinces, sidelining PM Adoula.

- Replaced the provisional Loi Fondamentale; commission’s draft released Jun 1964.
- Provinces increased from 6 to 21; name formalised as “Democratic Republic of the Congo.”
- Automatic parliamentary dissolution; elections within 6–9 months.
- 9 Jul 1964: Kasavubu asked Tshombe to form a transitional government.

**Why it matters:** Addressed a root cause (federalist vs centralist tension) but concentrated unchecked power in the President — a limited domestic settlement.

#### Stanleyville hostages and Operation Dragon Rouge (Aug–Nov 1964)

Rebels held Westerners hostage in Stanleyville; a joint US–Belgian rescue succeeded but damaged Tshombe’s standing.

- 28 Oct 1964: rebel commander placed Westerners (including Americans) under house arrest.
- Late Nov 1964: joint US–Belgian rescue operation.
- Belgian military assistance became “the backbone of the ANC” after Tshombe’s return.
- Simba/eastern rebellion put down Dec 1964; Soumialot exiled.

**Why it matters:** Clearest example of unilateral Western military intervention in Phase 2; politically it weakened Tshombe and set up his dismissal.

#### 1965 elections and Mobutu’s second coup (18 Mar–24 Nov 1965)

Tshombe’s CONACO won the 1965 elections, but the Kasavubu–Tshombe power struggle prompted Mobutu’s bloodless coup, ending the crisis under his rule.

- 223 parties contested 167 seats; elections ran eight weeks instead of six.
- CONACO won 122 of 167 seats.
- 14 Oct 1965: Kasavubu dismissed Tshombe, named Kimba PM — parliament rejected Kimba.
- 24 Nov 1965: Mobutu’s coup (25 Nov in one account), cancelling elections and ruling under a “regime of exception” for five years.

**Why it matters:** Evidence that durable political settlement failed: elections had limited impact on unity and provided the avenue for Mobutu’s takeover.

### Historians

- **Kent (2011):** The demands of the Cold War had to confront the requirements of decolonisation in an explosive socio-economic and political mix.
- **Kent (2010):** In economic terms the fundamental problem was not essentially of African making.
- **Parker (2024):** The Congo Crisis was not the first war of decolonisation in Africa, but it was the worst up to that point in time.
- **Namikas (2013):** Colonialism left the Congo exploited, underdeveloped, and poorly governed.
- **Britannica (2024):** Private European and American corporations invested heavily in the Belgian Congo after World War I.

### Using this topic in the exam

- Overall judgement: distinguish achievements at the military/territorial level (secessions ended by force, notably Grandslam) from failures of durable political settlement (Luluabourg Constitution and 1965 elections fed into Mobutu’s coup).
- Contrast Hammarskjöld’s strict neutrality (refusing to help end the secessions, citing sovereignty) with U Thant’s forceful stance from 1961–62 — shows UN management evolving and becoming more decisive.
- Weigh the UN against unilateral Great Power action (US covert operations, Belgian military aid, mercenaries), which dominated Phase 2 once ONUC had withdrawn.
- Use the Bakwanga massacres to show causes and phases are interconnected (South Kasai → Lumumba’s dismissal → Mobutu’s coup) rather than separate.
- Pitfall: don’t present ONUC as purely impartial — both Secretaries-General faced the critique of lost neutrality (USSR saw pro-Western bias; U Thant acquiesced to US pressure in the Greene Plan).
- Causes essays: organise by the syllabus categories — decolonisation, domestic politics, economic interests, ethnic and religious nationalisms — and show how they interacted with the Cold War.

## P1 Theme III · Bosnian War

*Why did conflicts occur and how did they affect the international order? How effective were the attempts to manage these conflicts? (Guiding: What was the Bosnian War? What contributed to it? What measures were taken to manage it, and how effective were they?)*

Bosnia-Herzegovina was the most multi-ethnic republic of Yugoslavia — Bosniak Muslims 44%, Orthodox Serbs 31%, Catholic Croats 17% (1991 census) — and its position between Serbia and Croatia made it vulnerable to nationalist territorial aspirations. When Yugoslavia collapsed with the fall of communism, Bosnia’s independence (declared 3 Mar 1992, recognised 6 Apr 1992) triggered war between the Republic, the Bosnian Serb Republika Srpska and the Croat Herzeg-Bosnia.

The causes fall into three categories: domestic politics (post-Tito devolution, the 1990 League of Communists collapse, nationalist election victories), economic interests (US$21 billion debt, IMF austerity, failed Marković reforms, regional inequality), and ethnic and religious nationalisms (Ottoman, Habsburg and WWII legacies suppressed, not resolved, under communism).

The war was fought largely through ethnic cleansing: over two million people were displaced and the Dayton Accords ended four years that claimed more than 250,000 lives. Key episodes include the Siege of Sarajevo, the attacks on the UN “safe areas” and the Srebrenica Genocide (July 1995), as well as the Croat–Bosniak “war within a war.”

Conflict management moved from failed EC/UN mediation (Carrington-Cutiliero, Vance-Owen, Owen-Stoltenberg), sanctions and a humanitarian-only UNPROFOR, to US-led diplomacy and NATO force. Operation Storm and Operation Deliberate Force pushed the parties to Dayton. As Burg and Shoup argue, the international community attempted, but failed, to reconcile mutually exclusive principles of state formation.

**Causes — Domestic Politics**

- After Tito’s death (1980), the 1974 constitution devolved real power to the republics, with a collective presidency and a weak federal government.
- Collapse of communism in Eastern Europe (1989), German unification (1990) and imminent Soviet collapse removed Western attention and the Soviet threat that had encouraged unity.
- Jan 1990 14th Congress: Milošević’s “one person, one vote” vs Slovene/Croat devolution — the walkout dissolved the all-Yugoslav Communist party.
- 1990 multi-party elections brought ethnic separatist/nationalist parties to power (in Bosnia: SDA, HDZ, SDS); four republics declared independence Jun 1991–Apr 1992.
- Competition for influence among Bosnian Serbs, Croats and Muslims: Serb referendum (Nov 1991), Serb republic proclaimed (9 Jan 1992).

**Causes — Economic Interests**

- 1960–1980 growth averaged 6.1%, but 1970s debt — estimated at US$6 billion — turned out to be US$21 billion.
- 1980s IMF austerity bred resentment of Communist elites; the 1987 Agrokomerc affair (promissory notes worth almost US$1 billion) discredited the system; strikes in 1987–88.
- After Gorbachev (1985), the West was no longer generous in restructuring Yugoslav debts, as a non-Soviet communist example was no longer needed.
- Marković’s 1989–90 reforms brought short-term gains, but inflation restarted (120% annually); Serbia took $1.5 billion from the central bank and imposed customs duties on Croatia and Slovenia.
- Regional inequality — Slovenia’s GDP per capita $12,383 vs Kosovo’s $1,592 (1989); Gini 0.32–0.35 — fuelled separatism.

**Causes — Ethnic & Religious Nationalisms**

- Nearly 400 years of Ottoman rule made Islam prominent but fostered religious tolerance.
- Austro-Hungarian rule (from 1878) promoted Catholicism, increasing Catholic–Orthodox tensions.
- Centralised Serbian monarchy after WWI and WWII Axis occupation/collaboration left long-standing grievances.
- Communist rule merely suppressed ethnic tensions, which resurfaced after the late 1980s.
- November 1990 elections put nationalist parties into power: SDA (Izetbegović), HDZ (Kljuić), SDS (Karadžić).

**Conflict management — tools and actors**

- Mediation: EC (Carrington-Cutiliero), UN–EC ICFY (Vance-Owen, Owen-Stoltenberg), the Contact Group, and US-led Dayton — “four attempts” per Editore (2014).
- Sanctions and arms embargo: over a hundred UNSC resolutions, but embargoes failed to deter aggressors and arguably gave Serbs an initial advantage.
- UN peace operations: UNPROFOR protected humanitarian aid and was mandated to protect six safe areas, but never enforced protection.
- NATO escalated from symbolic statements and monitoring (1992) to enforcement (1992–93) to direct combat (1994–95).
- US policy shifted (Watson, 2018) from reluctance, to diplomacy, to armed intervention — driven partly by the ‘CNN effect.’

### Case studies

#### Collapse of Yugoslavia: economic crisis and political breakdown (1980–Jan 1992)

Economic crisis, austerity and the end of the Cold War eroded the federation; the 1990 Communist Congress collapse and nationalist election victories led to secession and war in Croatia.

- Debt estimated at US$6 billion turned out to be US$21 billion; IMF austerity in the 1980s.
- 1987 Agrokomerc affair: promissory notes worth almost US$1 billion without collateral.
- Jan 1990: Slovene and Croatian delegations walked out of the 14th Congress, dissolving the all-Yugoslav party.
- 1990: Kučan (Slovenia) and Tuđman (Croatia) elected; Serbia/Montenegro favoured Yugoslav unity.
- By January 1992 the SFRY had ceased to exist.

**Why it matters:** Supports essays on domestic politics and economic causes: the end of the Cold War removed both Western financial support and the external incentive for unity.

#### Rise of the nationalist parties and the SDS takeover plan (May 1990–Dec 1991)

The first free elections put ethno-nationalist parties into power in Bosnia; the SDS prepared a coordinated takeover of municipalities.

- SDA founded 26 May 1990 in Sarajevo as a “party of Muslim cultural-historic circle.”
- HDZ founded 18 Aug 1990; SDS founded 1990 under Karadžić to keep Bosnian Serbs in Yugoslavia.
- From Sep 1991 the SDS established “Serb Autonomous Regions.”
- Dec 1991 top-secret SDS document planned takeover via shadow governments, “crisis headquarters” and coordination with the JNA.

**Why it matters:** Shows ethnic and religious nationalism politicised directly; the secret plan shows premeditated ethnic mobilisation ahead of the war.

#### Formation of Republika Srpska (24 Oct 1991–12 Aug 1992)

Bosnian Serbs created their own assembly, referendum and republic to remain in Yugoslavia, then declared independence and set war aims.

- 24 Oct 1991: Assembly of the Serb People of Bosnia and Herzegovina founded in Banja Luka.
- 9–10 Nov 1991 Serb referendum; 9 Jan 1992 Republic proclaimed; 28 Feb 1992 constitution adopted.
- Declared independence 7 Apr 1992; renamed Republika Srpska 12 Aug 1992.
- 12 May 1992: Karadžić’s Six Strategic Objectives; VRS created under Ratko Mladić.
- By 1993 RS controlled about 70% of Bosnia.

**Why it matters:** Key non-state actor; the Six Objectives (separate borders, corridors, divided Sarajevo, access to the sea) explain the logic of ethnic cleansing.

#### Herzeg-Bosnia and the HVO (Oct 1991–Oct 1992)

Bosnian Croats created the Croatian Community of Herzeg-Bosnia with close ties to Croatia; its HVO first allied with the ARBiH, but the alliance broke down.

- Oct 1991: JNA destroyed the Croat village of Ravno; Izetbegović did not react.
- 12 Nov 1991 Kordić–Boban meeting: “an age-old dream, a common Croatian State.”
- 18 Nov 1991: HZ-HB established (Boban president); declared unlawful 23 Nov 1991.
- 8 Apr 1992: HVO formed (Bosniaks 20–30% of it); 21 Jul 1992 Izetbegović–Tuđman agreement placed it under ARBiH command, collapsing Oct 1992.

**Why it matters:** Shows competing nationalisms and Croatia’s role as an external actor; sets up the Croat–Bosniak War.

#### Carrington-Cutiliero (Lisbon) Plan (Feb–Mar 1992)

The EC’s first mediation proposed ethnic power-sharing and a state of three constituent units; all sides signed, but Izetbegović reneged within a week.

- Proposed by Jose Cutiliero and Lord Carrington in February 1992, before full-scale war.
- Every district would be classified as Bosniak, Serb or Croat, even without an ethnic majority.
- Signed by all three sides 18 Mar 1992.
- Izetbegović’s reasons remain unclear — possibly partition, promised US support, or Middle Eastern support.

**Why it matters:** Failure of early regional mediation (lacked a uniform approach), but the idea of a Bosnia divided between three peoples became a feature of every later plan.

#### Independence referendum and international recognition (29 Feb–22 May 1992)

Bosniaks and Croats voted for independence in a referendum boycotted by Serbs; recognition by the US and EC coincided with the outbreak of war.

- Turnout 64%, of whom 99.7% voted for independence — short of the required two-thirds majority.
- SDS said independence would make Serbs “a national minority in an Islamic state.”
- 3 Mar 1992: Izetbegović declared independence; recognised 6 Apr 1992; UN admission 22 May.
- Ethnic cleansing 1992–late 1995 displaced more than two million people.

**Why it matters:** The trigger of the war; shows how recognition of contested statehood collided with Serb self-determination claims (Burg and Shoup).

#### Siege of Sarajevo (5 Apr 1992–29 Feb 1996)

The JNA and then the VRS besieged the capital from the surrounding hills for nearly four years.

- Siege force of 13,000; at least 500,000 bombs dropped from 2 May 1992.
- ARBiH defenders averaged ~34,931 troops without heavy weapons/armour.
- 13,952 killed, including 5,434 civilians.
- Lifted following the Dayton Agreement (14 Dec 1995).

**Why it matters:** Central example of VRS aims (Objective 5: divide Sarajevo) and of the arms embargo’s effect on the Bosnian government; market shellings later triggered international action.

#### Serb ethnic cleansing campaign (Prijedor, Banja Luka, Višegrad) (Apr–Aug 1992)

Bosnian Serb militia and SDS activists systematically expelled non-Serbs from eastern Bosnia, the Sava valley, Bosanska Krajina and Sarajevo’s suburbs.

- Between 700,000 and 1,000,000 Bosniaks expelled from Serb-held territory.
- 70% of all expulsions in Apr–Aug 1992; 37 municipalities attacked; 850 villages razed.
- Prijedor: non-Serbs forced to wear white armbands, bused to Omarska, Trnopolje and Keraterm camps.
- Non-Serb population fell from 726,960 (54%) in 1991 to 235,015 (36%) in 1997.

**Why it matters:** Evidence for ethnic nationalism as the driver of the war’s development; use the UN Commission of Experts’ definitions (S/25274, S/1994/674).

#### Sanctions, arms embargo and NATO enforcement at sea and in the air (May 1992–Nov 1995)

The UNSC sanctioned the FRY and NATO moved from monitoring to enforcement, yet Belgrade kept supplying the VRS.

- Res 757 (30 May 1992) passed 13–0; Bush seized ~US$200 million of Yugoslav assets.
- Res 781 (9 Oct 1992) no-fly zone; Res 787 (16 Nov 1992) shipment ban.
- 22 Nov 1992: Operation Maritime Guard — “the first authorised use of force to back a UN Security Council resolution”; Deny Flight from 12 Apr 1993.
- Despite sanctions, Belgrade remained the main source of soldiers, ammunition and finance for RS until 1995.
- Res 1022 suspended sanctions 22 Nov 1995.

**Why it matters:** Shows limits of sanctions: embargoes did not prevent or deter the conflict and arguably gave Serbs an initial advantage and a three-year stalemate.

#### UNPROFOR and the Sarajevo airlift (Jun 1992–Dec 1995)

UNPROFOR’s mandate was primarily to protect humanitarian aid, from Sarajevo airport to escorting convoys across Bosnia.

- Res 758 (8 Jun 1992): Sarajevo airport; Res 770 (13 Aug 1992) and 776 (14 Sep 1992) extended aid protection.
- 3 Jul 1992–31 Jan 1993: 2,476 aircraft carried 27,460 tons of relief.
- UNHCR delivered ~950,000 tonnes in Bosnia (Jul 1992–end 1995), including 160,000 tonnes to Sarajevo in over 12,000 flights.
- The “longest-running humanitarian airlift in history, surpassing the 1948–49 Berlin airlift.”

**Why it matters:** Best evidence that the UN reduced human suffering; but it resisted enforcing aid delivery and did not deter ethnic cleansing.

#### ICFY and the Vance-Owen Peace Plan (Aug 1992–18 Jun 1993)

The UN and EC created the ICFY, whose Vance-Owen Plan proposed ten semi-autonomous regions with power-sharing; the Bosnian Serbs rejected it.

- London Conference (Aug 1992) set principles incl. non-recognition of gains won by force; on 30 Aug the Alipasino Polje market was shelled, killing 15.
- Plan: nine-member presidency divided equally, rotating President, veto rights.
- Clinton administration failed to support it or provide an implementation force.
- RS president signed 30 Apr 1993; RS Assembly rejected it 6 May; 96% rejected it in a referendum; Owen declared it “dead” 18 Jun.

**Why it matters:** The last plan for a mixed, united Bosnia — obsolete given ethnic cleansing. Its decentralisation became the backbone of later agreements.

#### Croat–Bosniak War and Croat/Bosniak ethnic cleansing (18 Oct 1992–23 Feb 1994)

A “war within a war” between the Republic and Herzeg-Bosnia (backed by Croatia), with ethnic cleansing by Croat and also Bosniak forces.

- First armed incidents in central Bosnia, October 1992; the alliance mostly fell apart by early 1993.
- 1993: Croat forces evicted Bosniaks from western Mostar; Heliodrom and Dretelj camps; Ahmići and Stupni Do massacres.
- 5,000 expelled from Vitez and 20,000–25,000 from Croat-controlled Mostar.
- Bosniak forces expelled Croats from Konjic (Mar 1993) and Bugojno (1993–94); Topalović’s killings in Sarajevo.

**Why it matters:** Shows violence by all three factions and that the warring parties had limited intention to settle — responsibility for peace passed to international actors.

#### Srebrenica 1993 and the UN “safe areas” (Jan–Jun 1993)

After VRS attacks shrank and shelled the Srebrenica enclave, the UNSC declared it and five other towns “safe areas” and authorised force in self-defence and NATO air power.

- Jan 1993 Bosniak attack on Kravica; VRS counter-offensive reduced the enclave to 150 km², with 50–60,000 refugees.
- 12 Apr 1993: VRS artillery killed 56, including 14 children in a school playground.
- Res 819 (16 Apr 1993) and 824 (6 May 1993): Srebrenica, Bihać, Goražde, Sarajevo, Tuzla, Žepa.
- Res 836 (4 Jun 1993) authorised force in self-defence and “all necessary measures, through the use of air power.”

**Why it matters:** Shows the UN expanding its mandate but without the means to protect — the setup for the 1995 failure.

#### Owen-Stoltenberg Plan (Jul–Aug 1993)

UN/EC mediators proposed a union of three ethnic republics; Izetbegović rejected it as partition.

- Vance resigned 1 Apr 1993, replaced by Thorvald Stoltenberg on 1 May.
- 20 Aug 1993 map: Serbs 53%, Muslims 30%, Croats 17%.
- Croats and Serbs accepted; Bosniaks demanded territory in eastern/western Bosnia and Adriatic access.
- 28 Aug 1993: Croatian Republic of Herzeg-Bosnia proclaimed in Grude.

**Why it matters:** Shows mediation drifting towards partition; the intensifying Croat–Bosniak war overwhelmed it.

#### Washington Agreement and the Federation (23 Feb–18 Mar 1994)

Under strong US pressure, Bosniaks and Croats agreed a ceasefire and created the Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina.

- 23 Feb 1994 ceasefire; HVO-controlled territory had dropped from 20% to 10%.
- Provisional agreement 1 Mar 1994; signed 18 Mar 1994 at a ceremony hosted by Clinton.
- Territory divided into ten autonomous cantons.
- Feb 1994: first Markale market attack killed 68 and injured 144, renewing calls for multilateralism.

**Why it matters:** First effective US-led settlement; resumed joint operations against Serbs, helping alter the military balance and end the war.

#### NATO’s first combat; Goražde and Bihać (Feb–Dec 1994)

NATO moved to combat over Bosnia while the VRS attacked the safe areas of Goražde and Bihać.

- 28 Feb 1994 Banja Luka Incident: NATO shot down four Serb jets — its first combat action.
- 30 Mar–23 Apr 1994: Serb offensive on Goražde; 10 Apr NATO’s first close air support mission.
- Nov–Dec 1994 Bihać Crisis: heaviest fighting of 1994, six different military formations.
- 28 May 1995: Bosnian Serbs captured 33 British UN servicemen near Goražde.

**Why it matters:** Marks the US shift to Phase 3 (armed intervention) and NATO’s escalation, but limited strikes did not stop attacks on safe areas.

#### The Contact Group (Apr 1994–1995)

France, Germany, Russia, the UK and the US formed an informal mediation framework and proposed a 51–49 territorial map.

- Established April 1994 after the Sarajevo marketplace shelling.
- July 1994 map: 51% to the Muslim-Croat federation, 49% to the Bosnian Serbs — rejected as an “American diktat.”
- USA and Germany wanted to lift the arms embargo; France and the UK opposed it; Russia sought changes favouring the Serbs.
- An estimated 500 Russian volunteers fought alongside Bosnian Serb paramilitaries.

**Why it matters:** Achieved consensus among the major powers for the first time and set the basis for later talks, but internal splits probably prolonged the conflict.

#### Srebrenica Genocide (9–11 Jul 1995)

Karadžić ordered the conquest of Srebrenica; the VRS under Mladić captured the UN safe area and systematically executed Bosniak men and boys.

- About 8,000 Muslim men and boys killed (Watson’s phase account: “upwards of 7,000”).
- Srebrenica and Žepa fell in July 1995 without UN reaction.
- 21 Jul 1995 London Conference: Boutros-Ghali let General Janvier request NATO airstrikes without consulting civilian UN officials.
- NATO and the UN agreed to use air strikes in response to attacks on any safe area.

**Why it matters:** The gravest failure of UN peacekeeping — safe areas were never provided with security; it pushed the West towards decisive force.

#### Operation Storm and Operation Deliberate Force (Summer–20 Sep 1995)

Croat and Bosniak offensives turned the tide, and NATO’s bombing campaign after the second Sarajevo market attack forced the Serbs to negotiate.

- Operation Storm: the largest European land offensive since WWII; the first Serb defeat in four years.
- Clinton authorised a private company to use retired US military personnel to train the Croatian army.
- 28 Aug 1995: Serb mortar shell killed 37 in the Sarajevo marketplace.
- 30 Aug–20 Sep 1995: Deliberate Force hit 338 targets; 14 Sep Holbrooke secured Karadžić and Mladić’s agreement to end the siege.

**Why it matters:** Shows force — battlefield change plus NATO airpower — as decisive in conflict management, marginalising UNPROFOR.

#### Dayton Accords (1 Nov–20 Dec 1995)

US-hosted talks produced the General Framework Agreement for Peace, preserving Bosnia as one state of two entities, implemented by NATO’s IFOR.

- Talks at Wright-Patterson Air Force Base, Ohio — chosen to stop negotiation via the media.
- Initialled 21 Nov 1995 by Izetbegović, Tuđman and Milošević; signed in Paris 14 Dec 1995.
- Bosniak-Croat Federation and Bosnian Serb Republic; Sarajevo the undivided capital.
- IFOR (60,000–66,000 troops) deployed 20 Dec 1995; OSCE organised elections in 1996; Annex 4 remains the constitution.

**Why it matters:** The successful peace process — ended a war that claimed more than 250,000 lives; peace has endured, but it entrenched ethnic divisions in the state structure.

### Historians

- **Burg and Shoup (2000):** The essence of ethnic conflict is the struggle between mobilised identity groups for power; much of Bosnia’s story is how the international community attempted, but failed, to reconcile mutually exclusive principles of state formation.
- **Burg and Shoup (2000) on civilians:** The civilian population bore the brunt of the war: displacement amounted to more than half the pre-war population.
- **Editore (2014):** From February 1992 until Dayton, four attempts to end the conflict were made — by the EC, the UN with the EC, the USA, and the Contact Group.
- **Watson (2018):** US policy shifted in three phases: reluctance to intervene, attempts at diplomacy, then armed military intervention.
- **Boyadjeva (2002):** Resources spent on the Gulf Wars led the US to cede a decisive role in what was seen as an intrinsically European problem.
- **Recchia (2015):** The Clinton administration, favouring intervention, saw Deliberate Force as imminent — “we have to hit ‘em hard.”

### Using this topic in the exam

- Causes essays: organise by the three syllabus categories (domestic politics, economic interests, ethnic and religious nationalisms) and show how the end of the Cold War linked them.
- Compare the peace plans (Carrington-Cutiliero, Vance-Owen, Owen-Stoltenberg, Contact Group) and explain why each failed — and how Dayton succeeded where they did not (battlefield change, NATO force, US leadership).
- Evaluate UNPROFOR with both sides: humanitarian success (Sarajevo airlift, ~950,000 tonnes) vs failure to protect safe areas (Srebrenica, Žepa) and marginalisation by NATO and the US.
- Trace NATO’s evolution from monitoring (1992) to enforcement (1993) to direct combat (1994–95), and the US shift from reluctance to intervention (Watson’s three phases, ‘CNN effect’).
- Sanctions/embargo: evaluate critically — they did not prevent or deter the war and arguably created an initial Serb advantage and three-year stalemate.
- Pitfall: don’t present ethnic cleansing as one-sided — the notes give examples from Serb, Croat and Bosniak forces; and note the warring parties’ limited intention to settle.

## P2 Theme I · Forming nation-states

*How did Southeast Asian countries form states and build nations after independence? — What were the democratic and authoritarian features of SEA governments? Why were different forms of government established over time? How did SEA nation-states consolidate power, and how successful were they?*

Independent Southeast Asia produced four forms of government: parliamentary democracies, constitutional monarchies, military governments and communist states. Their establishment is explained by three factors studied before, at and after independence: the decolonisation experience (colonial attitudes, tutelage, peaceful or violent transfer), the role of local political elites and masses, and Cold War developments such as US intervention under the Domino Theory.

Decolonisation fell into three clusters: peaceful transfer with tutelage (Philippines, Malaya, Singapore), peaceful transfer without tutelage (Burma, Cambodia, Laos) and violent re-imposition of control (Vietnam, Indonesia). Except Malaysia and Singapore, every early democracy shifted to military or authoritarian rule by 1970 — Thailand (1947), Indonesia (1959), Burma (1962), the Philippines (1972) — because elites could not compromise and the masses’ demands were suppressed, raising the military’s influence.

Consolidation of power is the government’s effort to strengthen its capacity to exercise power, chiefly by building legitimacy and controlling opposition, analysed through four factors: leaders, sources of power and legitimacy, political challenges and opposition, and the Cold War. Three outcomes emerge: unstable regimes that reverted to democracy (Indonesia 1998, Philippines 1986, Thailand 1973–76 and 1992), stable authoritarian rule (Burma’s Tatmadaw, Vietnam’s VCP) and semi-democracies (Singapore, Malaysia).

The main line of argument: decolonisation set initial conditions but did not determine long-term stability; the Cold War was an amplifier rather than a root cause; and successful consolidation correlates less with regime type than with a regime’s capacity to adapt — Doi Moi and Singapore/Malaysia’s electoral adjustments versus Suharto’s and Marcos’s failure to reform in time.

**Four forms of government**

- Parliamentary democracies: early Philippines, Indonesia, Burma; Malaysia and Singapore throughout
- Constitutional monarchies: Cambodia, Laos, Malaysia, Thailand from 1932
- Military governments: Thailand 1947–73, Burma from 1962, Indonesia’s New Order, Philippines under martial law
- Communist states: Vietnam, Cambodia, Laos

**Factors for the establishment of different forms of government**

- Decolonisation experience: US and Britain willing to cede control if democracy resulted; French/Dutch refusal legitimised the military and revolutionary (often communist) struggle
- Political beliefs of elites: Marcos, Sukarno, Phibun prone to authoritarianism amid disunity
- Distribution of power: groups bound only by anti-colonialism clashed once in power, making the military seem a better alternative authority
- Familiarity with constitutional processes: hasty or violent decolonisation left little experience of compromise, so a ‘strongman’ seemed necessary
- Masses’ desire for influence: popular/communist movements; suppressing unrest increased the military’s political influence
- Cold War: US intervention (Domino Theory), North Vietnamese support for regional communists, the Sino-Soviet Split and the end of the Cold War

**Four factors for the consolidation of power**

- Role of government leaders: personal style, ideology, political skill
- Sources of power and legitimacy: constitutions/elections, state ideology, the military, economic performance, patronage networks
- Political challenges and popular opposition: responses to rebellions, insurgencies, protests and elite/mass dissent
- Cold War developments: superpower aid, ideology and alliances strengthening or weakening domestic power
- Success is measured by regime stability/durability; failure leads to new governments being formed

**Consolidation clusters compared**

- Indonesia/Philippines/Thailand: highly personalistic leaders; manipulated constitutions/elections (Golkar, KBL); patronage and growth, but cronyism corrosive; mass uprisings (1973, 1986, 1992, 1998) toppled regimes once legitimacy collapsed
- Burma/Vietnam: collective leadership or military hierarchy; single-party/military ideology gave deep control; far less dependent on US aid or democratic appearances; highly resilient
- Singapore/Malaysia: ‘semi-democracies’ (William Case) — procedurally genuine but tilted elections, coercive laws (ISA, Sedition Act) against explicit challengers, military strictly professional and apolitical
- Role of the military: independent political force (Burma, Indonesia, Thailand) vs subordinate to the party (Vietnam, ‘the party commands the gun’) or professional and apolitical (Singapore, Malaysia)

### Case studies

#### Thailand: 1932 revolution to military rule (1932–1973)

A bloodless 1932 coup by Western-educated ‘Promoters’ ended Chakri absolute monarchy, but post-war democracy collapsed in Phibun’s 1947 coup, beginning military rule under Phibun, Sarit and Thanom until 1973.

- 24 Jun 1932: King Prajadhipok forced to accept constitutional monarchy; he abdicated in 1935
- 1945–48: 9 administrations amid Pridi–Seni–Phibun rivalries; 6 Jun 1946 King Ananda Mahidol found dead of a gunshot wound, used by royalists to undermine Pridi
- 8 Nov 1947 coup claimed to defend ‘Nation, Religion and King’; 1951 ‘Silent Coup’ gave a military-dominated cabinet (19/25 seats)
- Sarit’s ‘benevolent paternalism’ revived royal ceremony; US military aid quadrupled in the 1960s (budget ~US$20m to ~US$250m/year)
- Growth of 8.2% p.a. (1961–70) created a business class and students who later challenged the regime (businessmen won 46% of seats in 1969)

**Why it matters:** Thailand was never colonised, so it shows elite rivalry and the masses rather than decolonisation driving the shift to authoritarianism; US aid ‘consolidated Thailand’s militarised state’ (Baker & Phongpaichit), and the most stable period (Sarit) had no constitution or elections at all.

#### Indonesian Revolution and the Madiun Affair (17 Aug 1945 – 2 Nov 1949)

Sukarno declared independence; the returning Dutch tried to re-impose control through two Police Actions until US pressure forced the 1949 Hague Agreement.

- Japan armed PETA (1943, 65,000 strong), later the backbone of the Republican Army
- Battle of Surabaya (Oct–Nov 1945): up to 15,000 Indonesian and 600 Allied deaths, incl. Brigadier Mallaby
- Linggadjati (Nov 1946) and Renville (Jan 1948) agreements broke down; Police Actions of Aug 1947 and Dec 1948 drew UN and US condemnation (Marshall Aid threat, Jan 1949)
- Sep 1948 Madiun: PKI/PSI declared an Indonesian Soviet Republic; the Republican Army suppressed it (c.8,000 dead, 35,000 arrested)

**Why it matters:** Key example of violent re-imposition: the struggle legitimised and politicised the military (setting up Suharto’s later rise), and Madiun’s anti-communist credentials won US support — a Cold War factor in decolonisation.

#### Vietnam: the DRV and the First Indochina War (1930–1954)

Ho Chi Minh’s communist-led Vietminh declared the DRV in 1945, fought France from 1946 and won at Dien Bien Phu; Geneva divided Vietnam at the 17th parallel.

- ICP founded 1930, Vietminh 1941; Aug 1945 DRV declared with 10/15 cabinet members from the ICP
- Mar 1946 Ho–Sainteny Agreement recognised the DRV only as a ‘free state’ in an Indochinese Federation
- 23 Nov 1946 French bombardment of Haiphong (2,000–6,000 civilian deaths) began the war; Vietminh redistributed 630,000 hectares to peasants
- 7 May 1954 French surrender at Dien Bien Phu; promised nationwide elections never held as the US and Diem feared a Vietminh win

**Why it matters:** Shows how violent re-imposition legitimised communism: Ho’s moderate, nationalist strategy (dissolving the ICP in Nov 1945) won broad support, and the military stayed inseparable from the Party rather than becoming an independent force.

#### Philippines: independence with tutelage and the Bell Act (1907–1954)

Long US tutelage led to independence on 4 Jul 1946, but the Bell Act tied rehabilitation aid to free trade and US ‘parity’ rights.

- 1907 Philippine Assembly; 1934 Tydings–McDuffie Act set a 10-year transition; 1935 Constitution
- 1946 Bell Act tied US$500m+ of rehabilitation aid to free trade and a ‘parity clause’ — critics saw a surrender of sovereignty
- Pro-US ilustrado elite (Roxas, Liberal Party) dominated politics
- Huk insurgency (1946–54) defeated after Magsaysay’s land reforms; AFP (founded 1935) aligned to the US by the 1947 Military Bases Agreement

**Why it matters:** Model of peaceful transfer with tutelage, communism suppressed with colonial support and an apolitical military — yet it still turned authoritarian, proving decolonisation alone did not determine stability.

#### Malaya: Malayan Union, the Alliance and the Emergency (1946–1957)

The Malayan Union provoked a Malay backlash and was replaced by a Federation; the UMNO–MCA–MIC Alliance won British trust while the British defeated the MCP in the Emergency, leading to independence on 31 Aug 1957.

- 1946 Malayan Union (centralised, jus soli citizenship) replaced by a Federation safeguarding the Malay special position; UMNO formed in response
- The Emergency shortened the independence timetable from 25 to 8 years; MCP outlawed 1948 after 3 European planters were killed
- Templer’s ‘New Villages’ (Briggs Plan) plus hearts and minds; by 1954, 5 states declared ‘white areas’
- Alliance won 51/52 seats in 1955; Tunku’s firmness at the 1955 Baling Talks won British trust

**Why it matters:** Peaceful transfer with tutelage: communism was suppressed with colonial support and the military stayed under British command, giving Malaysia a shared anti-communist baseline and no strong post-independence communist insurgency.

#### Burma: independence without tutelage (1945 – 4 Jan 1948)

Aung San’s AFPFL used mass pressure to force a swift British withdrawal; Panglong brought in some minorities, but Aung San’s assassination left U Nu with a fragile union.

- 1945 White Paper proposed 3 years of direct rule; rallies and People’s Voluntary Organisations forced London to commit to independence
- Jan 1947 Aung San–Attlee Agreement; Feb 1947 Panglong Agreement (Shan, Kachin, Chin; Karens largely boycotted)
- Apr 1947 election: AFPFL won 173/210 seats; Aung San assassinated 19 Jul 1947, aged 32
- BIA/BNA (30,000-strong) politicised the military; communists expelled from the AFPFL in 1946

**Why it matters:** Peaceful transfer without tutelage: the under-3-year withdrawal left elites unprepared for democracy and no power-sharing design, while the wartime army became an independent power source — roots of the 1962 coup.

#### Philippines: patron-client democracy to martial law (1946 – 21 Sep 1972)

Democracy built on patron-client networks and an ‘old oligarchy’ produced corruption and gridlock until Marcos declared martial law, promising a ‘New Society’.

- Near-identical Liberal and Nationalist parties; c.200 wealthy families dominated; Quirino’s ‘ten percenters’ demanded bribes (Ricklefs)
- Magsaysay used the military to protect ballot boxes in 1953; Congress blocked his land reform; he died in a 1957 air crash
- Marcos elected 1965, re-elected 1969; CPP formed 1968 with the NPA; First Quarter Storm Jan 1970 (4 students killed)
- Plaza Miranda bombing (Aug 1971) cited for Proclamation No. 1081, 21 Sep 1972

**Why it matters:** Shows the failure of early democracy despite tutelage: US-style checks were undermined by patron-client politics, and Marcos viewed democracy as ‘wasteful and paralyzing’ — elite beliefs and mass unrest as factors.

#### Burma: U Nu’s democracy to Ne Win’s coup (1948 – 2 Mar 1962)

AFPFL factionalism, Buddhist Socialism and ethnic insurgencies undermined parliamentary rule; after an 18-month caretaker government, Ne Win seized power permanently in 1962.

- Karen and White Flag communist insurgencies from 1948 contained but not defeated
- Jan 1958 AFPFL split into ‘Stable’ (Ba Swe, Kyaw Nyein) and U Nu’s ‘Clean’ factions; U Nu passed the budget without debate and asked Ne Win to form a caretaker government
- 1961 State Religion Bill made Buddhism the state religion, enraging minorities and the military
- By end-1961 insurgents controlled one-tenth of the country; 2 Mar 1962 bloodless coup set up the Union Revolutionary Council

**Why it matters:** Supports arguments on elites’ inability to share power and unfamiliarity with constitutional processes; the military’s suppression of unrest raised its political role.

#### Indonesia: parliamentary democracy, Guided Democracy and Gestapu (1949 – Mar 1967)

Fragmented parties and unstable cabinets discredited democracy; Sukarno imposed Guided Democracy (Nasakom), balancing the army against the PKI until the 1965 Gestapu Coup led to the PKI’s destruction and Suharto’s rise.

- 5 cabinets rose and fell 1949–55; 1955 election: PNI 22.3%, Masjumi 20.9%, NU 18.4%, PKI 16.4% (91.5% turnout of 39m voters)
- PKI tripled to 1 million (1954–55), c.3m by the early 1960s; 1958 PRRI rebellion suppressed by the army
- Sukarno restored the 1945 Constitution (1959), dismissed parliament (1960), cancelled 1960 elections; inflation reached 500% by 1965
- 30 Sep 1965 Gestapu: 6 generals murdered; up to half a million killed in the purge; Suharto president Mar 1967, with US/Western support

**Why it matters:** Illustrates every factor for democracy’s failure (elite disunity, Java/Outer Islands split, mass PKI growth) and shows the Cold War amplifying a domestic power struggle.

#### Cambodia and Laos: independence to communist rule (1953–1992)

Both gained independence from France as constitutional monarchies with little tutelage; Cold War pressures and North Vietnamese support brought communist governments in 1975.

- Sihanouk’s 1953 ‘royal crusade for independence’; full independence 9 Nov 1953; his Sangkum won 83% and all seats in 1955 by intimidation/fraud
- Sihanouk allowed North Vietnamese bases (1966) and secret US bombing (1969); overthrown by US-backed Lon Nol, Mar 1970
- Khmer Rouge took Phnom Penh Apr 1975; genocide killed an estimated 1–2 million of 8 million (1975–78); Vietnam invaded Dec 1978, withdrew by 1992
- Laos: 1957 coalition collapsed 1959 under US pressure; Kong Le’s 1960 coup created a three-way civil war; Pathet Lao founded the Lao PDR in Dec 1975

**Why it matters:** Shows Cold War developments (US intervention, North Vietnamese networks, the Sino-Soviet Split driving the Third Indochina War) as decisive in establishing communist states.

#### Singapore: SLF, PAP, Merger and Operation Cold Store (1954–1965)

British tutelage via Westminster-style elections brought the SLF and then the PAP to power; Lee Kuan Yew used Merger and Operation Cold Store to defeat the pro-communist left.

- 1953 Rendel Commission; SLF won 1955; Marshall resigned after the failed 1956 London talks
- PAP won the 1959 landslide (43/51 seats), partly through its pro-communist faction’s control of unions
- PAP split: confidence vote 27–8 with 16 abstentions; 13 assemblymen expelled; Barisan Sosialis formed Aug 1961
- Merger Referendum won Sep 1962; Operation Cold Store (1963); joined Malaysia 16 Sep 1963, separated 9 Aug 1965

**Why it matters:** Peaceful transfer with tutelage where communism was suppressed with colonial support, laying foundations for the PAP’s later semi-democratic dominance.

#### Vietnam: DRV, RVN and the Second Indochina War (1954–1976)

The legitimate, cohesive Lao Dong North outlasted the despotic, US-backed South; after US withdrawal, Saigon fell and Vietnam reunified as the SRV.

- 1960 DRV constitution gave Ho absolute presidential powers, though he practised collective leadership
- Diem placed brothers in key posts and persecuted Buddhists; NLF controlled about two-thirds of southern villages by 1962
- 1963 Buddhist Crisis; US-sanctioned coup killed Diem and Nhu (Nov 1963); 6 short-lived governments before Thieu (1965)
- Gulf of Tonkin (Aug 1964), Tet Offensive (30 Jan 1968); Paris Peace Accords 1973; SRV proclaimed 12 Jul 1976; c.600,000 North Vietnamese and 58,000 US soldiers dead

**Why it matters:** Central case for Cold War intervention under the Domino Theory; the contrast between northern revolutionary legitimacy and southern despotism shows legitimacy mattered more than US aid (Clark Clifford: ‘a civil war in Vietnam’).

#### Malaysia: 13 May 1969 and semi-democracy (1957–2000)

The Alliance’s ethnic bargain broke down in the 13 May 1969 riots; under Razak and Mahathir the BN consolidated power through constitutional limits on debate, the NEP, co-optation and coercive laws.

- Social Contract: Malay special position (Section 153) in exchange for Chinese economic power and citizenship
- 13 May 1969: official toll 196 dead (unofficial 600–800); NOC/MAGERAN ruled 1969–71 under Tun Razak
- 1971 Constitution (Amendment) Act banned discussion of sensitive issues; NEP raised bumiputera equity from 1.5% to 20.3% (1969–90); BN formed 1 Jun 1974
- Operation Lalang (1987, 100+ detained under the ISA); 1998 Anwar sacked and jailed, sparking Reformasi; BN kept two-thirds in 1999 only via Chinese/Indian votes

**Why it matters:** Key semi-democracy case: constitutional and electoral processes stabilised Malaysia by narrowing debate while offering legitimate opposition channels — unlike Indonesia/Philippines, where crises meant regime replacement.

#### Burma: Ne Win, the BSPP and the Burmese Way to Socialism (1962–1988)

Ne Win’s military regime ruled through a single party and a socialist state ideology, crushing ethnic insurgents and protests, until economic failure triggered collapse in 1988.

- Ne Win’s legitimacy came from being one of the ‘Thirty Comrades’ and the 1958–60 caretaker government; style ‘gradual and cautious’ (Taylor), 1980 amnesty
- BSPP sole legal party from 1964; 1974 Constitution entrenched single-party rule and a unitary state
- Burmese Way to Socialism (1962) nationalised almost the entire economy; foreign trade fell from 40% to 13% of GDP by 1977
- An estimated 500,000 dead in four decades of civil strife; 1985/1987 demonetisations wiped out savings

**Why it matters:** Stable authoritarian cluster: deep ideological and military control with little dependence on US aid or democratic appearances — but economic failure eroded legitimacy.

#### Indonesia: Suharto’s New Order, its fall and democratic transition (1965–2001)

Suharto consolidated power through Golkar, the military’s dwifungsi role and economic patronage, backed by the West; the 1997 Asian Financial Crisis exposed cronyism and forced his resignation, opening a turbulent democratic transition.

- Golkar won every election from 1971 (65% rising to 74.5% in 1997); opposition merged into PPP and PDI (1972–73); 1985 Ormas legislation imposed Pancasila
- Dwifungsi (1958 Nasution speech, law 1982): by the late 1970s half the cabinet and two-thirds of governorships were military appointees
- Oil revenues quadrupled after 1973–74; state share of GDP rose from 13% to 41% by 1983; cukong businessmen (Liem Sioe Liong) bound to the regime
- Malari riots 1974 (11 dead), Tanjung Priok 1984 (28+ dead); 4 Trisakti students killed 12 May 1998, riots c.2,250 dead; resigned 21 May 1998
- Golkar lost the April 1999 election to PDIP (33.7% vs 22.4%); Wahid dismissed 2001

**Why it matters:** Unstable cluster: manipulated elections gave only a thin veneer of legitimacy, cronyism proved corrosive, and US support faded after the Cold War — a case of failure to reform in time.

#### Singapore: the PAP’s semi-democracy (1965–2000)

After Separation, the PAP consolidated one-party dominance through the ‘politics of survival’, coercive laws, grassroots and labour controls, and constitutional innovations allowing controlled diversity.

- PAP held all seats in 1968, 1972, 1976 and 1980; 1968 British withdrawal announcement heightened vulnerability
- ISA used in Operation Coldstore (1963, 100+) and Operation Spectrum (1987, 22 alleged Marxist conspirators)
- NCMP (1984), GRC (1988), NMP (1990), elected presidency (1991/93); Newspapers and Printing Presses Act (1974)
- Jeyaretnam’s 1981 Anson by-election win ended the monopoly; opposition peaked at 4 seats in 1991; defamation suits (Tang Liang Hong, 1997)

**Why it matters:** Supports the semi-democracy argument (Case): tilted but impeccably conducted elections and periodic adjustments extended PAP rule, while the apolitical SAF removed the military as a rival power source.

#### Philippines: Marcos’s New Society, EDSA and restored democracy (1972–2001)

Marcos ruled through martial law, the military and cronies, but Aquino’s assassination, a fraudulent snap election and military defections led to the 1986 People Power Revolution; restored democracy remained elite-dominated.

- 1973 Constitution let Marcos hold presidential and prime-ministerial power; KBL (1978) paralleled Golkar
- AFP tripled (45,000→150,000, 1967–86); cronies Cojuangco, Benedicto, Disini given monopolies; c.70,000 political prisoners by 1977
- Benigno Aquino Jr assassinated 1983; NPA regulars grew 1,900→24,430 (1973–86)
- Feb 1986 snap election: Marcos claimed victory against tallies showing Aquino ahead (by ~800,000); Enrile and Ramos mutinied 22 Feb; Marcos fled 26 Feb
- US bases not renewed after 1991; Estrada forced out by ‘EDSA II’ in Jan 2001

**Why it matters:** Unstable cluster: US Cold War support propped up an anti-communist strongman but its withdrawal undermined him; mass uprising toppled the regime once legitimacy collapsed.

#### Thailand: 1973 uprising, Thammasat, Prem and Black May (Oct 1973 – 2000)

Student-led protests ended military rule in 1973; democracy collapsed in the 1976 Thammasat massacre; Prem’s managed democracy followed; after the 1991 coup, Black May 1992 restored civilian rule — with the King decisive each time.

- Oct 1973: 500,000+ demonstrators; King Bhumibol ordered Thanom and Praphas to resign
- 1975 election: 22 parties, no majority; 1,333 strikes and 322 demonstrations (1973–75); Thammasat massacre 6 Oct 1976 (46+ officially dead)
- Prem (1980–88): longest period of undisputed parliamentary function; business held one-third of seats in 1979, two-thirds by 1988
- Feb 1991 NPKC coup; Black May 1992 (500,000+ protesters, hundreds killed); 1997 ‘People’s Constitution’; military budget share 22% (1985) to 13% (1996)

**Why it matters:** Shows constitutions as tools of whoever held power and popular opposition repeatedly forcing change, with the monarchy as the decisive moderating force (1973, 1976, 1992).

#### Vietnam: the SRV and Doi Moi (1976–2000)

An orthodox old-guard VCP struggled to reintegrate the South and was isolated after invading Cambodia; Nguyen Van Linh’s Doi Moi opened the economy while preserving Party supremacy.

- VCP held 92% of the 395-seat National Assembly; 30/34 cabinet members also on the Central Committee
- Only 0.6% of Mekong Delta households in cooperatives by 1982; rice production fell 11.83m tons (1976) to 9.79m (1979); boat people exodus
- Invasion of Cambodia (1978): US trade embargo till 1995, total dependence on Soviet aid and COMECON
- Doi Moi (Dec 1986): Resolution 10 (1988) land tenure; SOEs halved 12,000→6,000 (1986–94); 1992 Constitution allowed 8% non-party candidates; ASEAN and normalised US relations 1995

**Why it matters:** Stable authoritarian cluster and the best example of adaptation: economic opening without losing political control (like China, unlike the USSR), rooted in the VCP’s historical legitimacy.

#### Burma: 8888 Uprising, SLORC and SPDC (Mar 1988 – 2021)

Nationwide protests forced Ne Win out, but Saw Maung’s coup created SLORC, which ignored the NLD’s 1990 landslide and ruled by force, ceasefires and mass organisation.

- Sein Lwin’s crackdown killed up to 3,000; Ne Win resigned 23 Jul 1988; SLORC coup 18 Sep 1988; NLD formed 28 Sep 1988
- May 1990 election: NLD 392/485 seats (70% turnout) vs NUP’s 10; Aung San Suu Kyi under house arrest 1989–95 and 2000–02
- Ceasefires with 17 of 21 armed groups in the early 1990s; USDA (1993) had 11m members by 1999
- 1991 Nobel Peace Prize; ASEAN admission 1997; SPDC 1997; 2008 constitution, 2010 elections, fresh coup 2021

**Why it matters:** Shows legitimacy and coercive capacity are not the same: coercion papered over Burma’s loss of legitimacy only until 2010/2021, and the end of the Cold War brought greater international scrutiny.

### Historians

- **Jan Pluvier:** The expulsion of the communists helped make the AFPFL ‘acceptable in British eyes’.
- **Ricklefs:** Philippine politics was dominated by patronage — e.g. Macapagal overspent despite pledging frugality.
- **Baker & Phongpaichit:** US Cold War aid ‘consolidated Thailand’s militarised state’.
- **Taylor:** Ne Win’s style was ‘gradual and cautious’, e.g. releasing political prisoners after 2 years.
- **William Case:** Singapore and Malaysia are ‘semi-democracies’ that ‘[borrow] cunningly some features of democracy in order substantively to avoid it’.
- **Clark Clifford:** Vietnam was ‘a civil war in Vietnam’, not simply Communist aggression — the Domino Theory did not materialise beyond Indochina.

### Using this topic in the exam

- Use the three establishment factors chronologically (before, at and after independence); weigh decolonisation as setting initial conditions only — the Philippines and Burma had peaceful transfers yet became authoritarian, while Malaysia and Singapore sustained semi-democracy.
- For ‘why did early democracies fail’, apply the four factors (elite beliefs, distribution of power, constitutional familiarity, the masses) to the Philippines, Indonesia, Burma and Thailand; the critical variable was elites’ (in)ability to compromise and whether mass energies were accommodated or suppressed.
- Treat the Cold War as an amplifier rather than a root cause: it entrenched communism in Vietnam/Cambodia/Laos and propped up (then helped undermine) Marcos, Suharto and Sarit/Thanom.
- For consolidation questions, compare the three clusters on leaders, sources of legitimacy, opposition and Cold War; argue that success correlates with capacity to adapt (Doi Moi, NCMP/GRC, NEP) more than regime type.
- Know the triggering events that shook or ended regimes (1973/1976/1992 Thailand, 1986 EDSA, 1988 Burma, 1998 Suharto) and compare the military’s role across all eight case studies.
- Thailand is excluded from decolonisation questions as it was never colonised.

## P2 Theme I · National unity

*Why was there a need for national unity efforts post-independence? What are the different ways to build national unity? How successful were Southeast Asian nation-states in building national unity over time?*

National unity has a tangible side (territorial integrity, social stability) and an intangible side (social cohesion and a national identity built from disparate ethnic, cultural and religious groups). It requires a single government commanding the loyalty of all citizens. The need arose from indigenous minorities with separate histories (Karens, Moros, Pattani Malays, Acehnese) and non-indigenous Chinese and Indian minorities brought in under colonial rule, whom governments both feared and depended on economically.

Governments chose between two approaches: dominant culture (assimilation), which is coercive and exclusionary (Burma, Indonesia, Thailand, Philippines), and multiculturalism (integration), which is less coercive and more inclusive (Singapore, Malaysia). They used four tools: ideology, religion, language and education. Success is judged by social stability — the absence of separatist insurgencies — and the formation of a widely accepted national identity.

Outcomes varied. Burma failed outright, its force breeding the world’s longest-running ethnic insurgencies. Indonesia’s New Order contained rather than resolved tensions, which unravelled in 1997–98. The Philippines’ deeper cleavage was class, not ethnicity. Thailand was comparatively successful thanks to continuous independence and the monarchy. Vietnam’s policy shifted with geopolitics. Malaysia achieved managed co-existence, and Singapore was the most stable, though its identity remains contested.

Cross-cutting arguments: force was consistently counter-productive while negotiated settlements proved more durable; Chinese integration succeeded where there were no religious or cultural barriers; external geopolitics shaped policy; and economic development was double-edged, creating new inequities by the 1990s. The unifying anchor also differed: the military in Burma and Indonesia, the VCP in Vietnam, the monarchy in Thailand, and dominant parties in Malaysia and Singapore.

**Need for national unity: the minorities**

- Indigenous minorities originated within SEA: Karens/Kachins/Shans/Chins, Papuans, Cordillerans and Moros, hill tribes and Pattani Malays, highland minorities, Orang Asli
- Non-indigenous minorities descend from colonial-era immigrants, chiefly Chinese and Indians
- Colonial immigration caused indigenous ethnic-economic nationalism that persisted after independence
- Governments were simultaneously fearful/suspicious of, and economically dependent on, these minorities

**Approaches and tools**

- Dominant culture (assimilation): minorities must adopt the dominant language/religion — Burma, Indonesia, Thailand, Philippines
- Multiculturalism (integration): a new culture drawn from all groups — Singapore, Malaysia (with exceptions)
- Inverse pair: Indonesia is officially accommodative but assimilative in practice; Malaysia is officially assimilative but accommodative in practice
- Tools: ideology (Pancasila, Burmese Way to Socialism, Nation-Religion-King, Rukunegara, Shared Values), religion, language, education

**Assessing effectiveness**

- Are minorities integral to the populace, or victimised/rejected?
- Is there equity of political representation?
- Are there efforts at equitable wealth distribution?
- Can all groups practise their own cultural identities?
- Do all groups see themselves as part of one nation-state?

**Cross-cutting patterns**

- Unifying anchor: Tatmadaw (Burma) and ABRI (Indonesia); the VCP (Vietnam); the monarchy (Thailand, whose military was factionalised); dominant parties UMNO/BN and PAP
- Force is consistently counter-productive (Burma, Aceh/East Timor, Mindanao/Cordillera, Meo Revolt); negotiated settlements (Aceh 2005, ARMM/CAR, Thai accommodation) more durable
- Chinese integration correlates with absence of religious/cultural barriers — Thailand and the Philippines vs Indonesia, Burma, Vietnam
- External geopolitics drove policy: Vietnam’s minority policy, Singapore in a ‘Malay sea’, East Timor/Aceh and international pressure
- Economic development is double-edged: used by Suharto, Sarit and the NEP, but created new inequities by the 1990s–2000s

### Case studies

#### Burma: Panglong, the union state and U Nu’s Burmanization (Feb 1947 – 1962)

Aung San’s Panglong agreement created a pluralist federal union, but it collapsed into civil war as U Nu pushed Burmanization through Buddhism and the Burmese language.

- Panglong, Feb 1947: ‘full autonomy in internal administration for the frontier areas’; 12 Feb still Union Day
- 1947 Constitution: Shan, Kachin, Karenni States, seats in the Chamber of Nationalities; Shan and Karen states could secede after a decade
- By end-1949 ‘almost the whole of the country was in the hands of one rebel group or another’ (Kingsbury)
- 1961 State Religion Bill made Buddhism the state religion; Burmese sole medium of instruction from Standard Three
- 1958 Shan State chose to secede; 1959 Karen/Karenni/Mon/Chin/communist alliance; 1960 Shan uprising; 1961 Kachin uprising

**Why it matters:** Shows colonial divide-and-rule (Ministerial Burma vs Frontier Areas) and Aung San’s death undermining unity; U Nu’s religious Burmanization alienated minorities and even politicised the Sangha.

#### Burma: Ne Win’s Tatmadaw and coercive assimilation (1962–1988)

Ne Win cast the Tatmadaw as ‘defender of national unity’, imposed the Burmese Way to Socialism and official xenophobia, and met insurgencies with force.

- BSPP (formed 4 July 1962) sole legal party; Ne Win portrayed with Aung San as a ‘Great Unifier’
- ~300,000 Indian/Pakistani nationals forced to leave; 1967 military-instigated anti-Chinese riot killed hundreds
- Insurgencies (KNU, Kachin Independence Army 1961, Shan State Army 1964) met with force; KNU ceasefire only in 2012
- 1974 Constitution created 7 non-autonomous minority states and 7 Burman divisions; Citizenship Act excluded Indian/Chinese immigrants from the ruling party

**Why it matters:** Clearest example of a dominant-culture approach enforced by the military; force created a self-perpetuating cycle of insurgency and resentment — ‘generating rather than healing’ divisions (Tucker).

#### Burma: SLORC, USDA and the ceasefires (1988–2001)

SLORC continued coercion through the USDA, loyalty tests and a stage-managed National Convention, while ceasefires with insurgents were military rather than political deals.

- USDA (1993, modelled on Golkar): 17.7 million members by July 2001; three national causes incl. ‘non-disintegration of the Union’
- 1991: politicians tested on 300+ loyalty questions; ~15,000 civil servants dismissed
- 1993 National Convention: only 99/702 delegates elected; NLD walked out 1995
- Ceasefires from 1989 with 17/21 groups; Khun Sa’s MTA bargained for personal benefit; 5 groups formed a new anti-government alliance by 1999; at least 24 separatist factions since the 1980s (Thomson)

**Why it matters:** Evaluates Burma as a failure — the state’s penetrations ‘succeeded in provoking rebellion’ (David Brown); Karen revolt the ‘oldest continuous ethnic revolt in the world’.

#### Indonesia: Youth Oath, Pancasila and Guided Democracy (1928–1965)

Sukarno built unity through the Youth Oath, Pancasila and gotong royong, presenting himself as the ‘social cement’, but the Jakarta Charter controversy and the 1958 regional revolts exposed deep divisions.

- Sumpah Pemuda (1928): one fatherland, one nation, one language (Bahasa Indonesia)
- Pancasila, 1 June 1945; Jakarta Charter’s 7 words on Islamic law removed 18 Aug 1945, feeding the Darul Islam rebellion (West Java, 1948–62)
- Unitary republic 17 Aug 1950; 5 cabinet changes 1949–55; 1958 Sumatra/Sulawesi revolts defeated by 1961
- Gotong Royong Cabinet 1957 and Parliament 1960; Sukarno declared ‘I am NASAKOM’ (1963)

**Why it matters:** Shows ideology as the main unity tool — Pancasila as a ‘vague philosophical rationalization of a plural society’ (Vatikiotis) — and the persistent risk of Islamic and regional disunity.

#### Indonesia: Suharto’s New Order uniformity, PKAT and transmigration (1965–1998)

Suharto turned Pancasila into ‘rigid ideological uniformity’, spread Bahasa Indonesia and development, and pursued assimilation of indigenous minorities through PKAT, land seizures and transmigration.

- P4 Pancasila courses for civil-service promotion (from 1978); 1985 Ormas decree; 1973 Bill merged opposition into PDI and PPP
- Per-capita income rose from US$260 to over US$500 in the 1970s (‘Father of Development’)
- Indonesian speakers: 40.8% (1971), 61.4% (1980), over 80% (1990); six recognised religions
- 1980s: 75%+ of land classified ‘State Forest’ (‘one of the largest land grabs in history’ — Duncan)
- Transmigration 1969–1989: ~730,000 families moved; fuelled Dayak–Madurese clashes in Kalimantan (2001)

**Why it matters:** Supports the argument that the New Order gave stability but only ‘contained latent forces’; officially accommodative but Javanese-centred and assimilative in practice.

#### Indonesia: East Timor and Aceh separatism (1953–2006)

Both regions had distinct origins; military repression deepened separatism until post-Suharto governments resolved the conflicts — East Timor by independence, Aceh by a peace deal.

- East Timor: Portuguese, Catholic; invaded/annexed 1976; Nov 1991 Dili massacre (200+ killed); UN referendum after Suharto’s fall; independent 2002
- Aceh: 1953 rebellion (part of Darul Islam); 1959 ‘special territory’ status ended the revolt
- Aceh’s oil/gas ~11% of national wealth (~US$2.6bn/yr from LNG) while Aceh remained poor
- GAM formed 1976 by Hasan di Tiro; 1989 Military Operations Area; Aug 2005 peace agreement after the 2004 tsunami, 2006 local elections

**Why it matters:** Evidence that force is counter-productive and that international pressure (UN, end of the Cold War) and negotiated settlements proved decisive; Hasan di Tiro called ‘Indonesia’ a cloak for ‘Javanese colonialism’.

#### Indonesia: the Indonesian Chinese (1965–2017)

A small, wealthy and politically vulnerable minority faced forced assimilation and pogroms while being co-opted economically, with restrictions eased only after 1998.

- Under 4% of the population; violent pogroms in 1965, 1967 and 1998
- 1967 ‘Basic Policy for the Solution of the Chinese Problem’: Chinese schools phased out (last closed 1974), script banned publicly, Indonesian names forced
- Patron-client ties (Liem Sioe Liong); 1990 tycoons told to hand 25% equity to co-operatives
- Post-1998: Habibie lifted the Mandarin ban; Lunar New Year a public holiday 2003; Ahok Jakarta governor 2014–17

**Why it matters:** The 1998 pogrom revealed forced assimilation as a total failure — contrast with integrated Thai Chinese; also shows development’s double edge (4% controlled ~75% of the economy by the 1980s).

#### Philippines: class, land reform, language and the Chinese (1946–1998)

With no clear anti-colonial adversary and politics dominated by personality and patronage, class divisions were the main source of disunity; land reform, a national language and Marcos’s Tadhana history had limited success.

- Huk Rebellion (1946–54); Magsaysay’s 1955 Land Reform Act blocked by Congress — Central Luzon tenancy stayed at 70–80%
- Only 4.6m of 8.1m targeted hectares redistributed by 1998 (Aquino’s CARP 1988, Ramos’s Social Reform Agenda 1994)
- Pilipino promoted from 1959; 1974 Bilingual Education Policy judged a failure; Tadhana: only 5 of 19 volumes completed
- Garcia’s ‘Filipino First’ (Retail Trade Nationalization Act 1954); Chinese 1.3% of population control ~60% of the economy; 1975 Naturalization Law; assimilation via intermarriage

**Why it matters:** Supports the argument that patronage and personality politics undermined unity more than ethnicity; the two moments of greatest unity were anti-regime (1983 assassination, 1986 EDSA).

#### Philippines: Mindanao Moros and Cordillerans (1898–2014)

Moro grievances over land and Christian dominance became armed separatism after the 1968 Jabidah Massacre; Cordillerans protested Marcos’s crony projects. An accommodative turn came under Aquino and Ramos.

- Moros 5–6% of population; US land laws gave 24 ha to Christians vs 10 for non-Christians
- 1968 Jabidah Massacre → MIM, then Nur Misuari’s MNLF seeking a Bangsa Moro nation
- Tripoli Agreement 23 Dec 1976 (OIC-mediated) broke down within a year; MILF split 1981; ARMM 1989; Ramos’s 1996 Peace Agreement; MILF deal only 2012/2014
- Cordillerans (~2%): Chico River dam and Cellophil logging triggered the CPDF; not secessionist; Executive Order 220 (1997) autonomous region not implemented by 2005

**Why it matters:** Comparison of a secessionist vs a non-secessionist minority; autonomy solved the problem of violence, not of a broad national identity; international pressure (OIC, Malaysian mediation) drove accommodation, paralleling East Timor.

#### Thailand: Nation, Religion, King to Phibun’s Thai-ness (1851–1944)

Chakri kings built a territorial Thai state and Vajiravudh’s triad of Nation, Religion, King; Phibun pushed extreme, forced Thai-ness including anti-Chinese measures, though the Chinese later integrated fully.

- 1902 Sangha Administration Act; 1911 Nationality Act made anyone born in the boundaries ‘Thai’
- Vajiravudh (1910–25) coined Chat, Satsana, Kasat and called the Chinese ‘Jews of the East’
- Phibun renamed Siam ‘Thailand’ (1939); Cultural Assimilation Policy (1939); ratthaniyom directives
- 1939 anti-Chinese laws: Thai names and language, Chinese teaching limited to 2 hrs/week; by the 1980s the ~10%-population Chinese controlled ~81% of the economy as integrated Thai nationals

**Why it matters:** Shows a long-standing dominant-culture ideology; Chinese integration via intermarriage and no religious/cultural barrier is the region’s success story.

#### Thailand: Sarit, the monarchy, Buddhism and the NIB (1957–post-1976)

Sarit revived the monarchy as a unifying force, centralised the Sangha and used ‘development’ as a rallying mantra; the National Identity Board later fused the triad with democracy and development.

- King Bhumibol toured provinces extensively, reaching minorities; revered as semi-divine
- Sangha Administration Act 1962 centralised the Sangha under a Bangkok Supreme Patriarch
- Thammathut (1965) and Thammacarik (1964–65) spread Buddhism to rural and tribal areas
- 1976 monk Kitthiwuttho: ‘Killing Communists is not Demeritorious’

**Why it matters:** Shows adaptability as the key to the triad’s longevity, and the monarchy replacing a factionalised military as the unifying anchor — ‘allegiance to the monarchy’ holding Thai-ness together (Streckfuss).

#### Thailand: Northern hill tribes and Pattani Muslims (1896–2004)

Forced resettlement provoked the Hmong Meo Revolt, while assimilation of the Malay-Muslim south produced separatism (PULO); later accommodation helped in the north but Pattani violence escalated after 2000.

- Northeast had 34% of population but 18% of the budget (1976); 1968 Meo Revolt met with bombing/napalm until 1973, ~15,000 refugees
- Pattani restored to Thailand by the 1909 Bangkok Treaty; 1923 Patani riots; 1948 Dusun Nyiur Incident (~400 Malay Muslims, 30 police killed)
- 1961 Sarit allowed pondoks to register; but Buddhist transmigration (100,000 families from 1961) deepened mistrust
- PULO founded 1967; of 2,593 incidents 1993–2004 only 18% occurred by 2000

**Why it matters:** Contrasts the limits of Thai assimilation where a religious barrier existed; supports the argument that force entrenches grievances while accommodation is more durable.

#### Vietnam: North Vietnam’s land reform and unity tools (1945–1975)

The VCP’s anti-colonial legitimacy, the cult of Uncle Ho, mass organisations and patriotic education unified the North, while a brutal land reform eliminated landlords and minority autonomy secured support for the war.

- 54 recognised ethnic groups; Kinh ~87% of population
- Land reform 1953–56: party admitted 30% of ‘landlord’ convictions erroneous; Nov 1956 Nghe An rebellion; ~1.6m households (~8m peasants) gained land
- Fatherland Front (1955); 9-year elementary education; Trung Sisters and Tran Hung Dao narratives
- 1960 Constitution (Article 3) guaranteed minority cultural autonomy; Tay Bac / Viet Bac Autonomous Regions

**Why it matters:** Shows a unifying decolonisation narrative unmatched elsewhere, plus a Confucianist culture (Duiker) suited to collective discipline; accommodation of minorities was driven by wartime need.

#### South Vietnam: Diem’s Personalism and the Buddhist Crisis (1955 – 2 Nov 1963)

Diem’s Personalism, nepotism and Catholic bias alienated Buddhists and Chinese; the 1963 Buddhist Crisis led to a US-approved coup and his execution.

- National Revolutionary Movement sole legal party; Strategic Hamlets (1961+) failed
- Forced 300,000 Saigon Chinese to register as citizens or lose business rights
- May 1963 Vesak flag ban in Hue → 9 killed; Thich Quang Duc’s self-immolation (June 1963); August pagoda raids
- Diem and Nhu executed 2 Nov 1963

**Why it matters:** Example of a fragmented, personalistic South with no basis of unity — religious divisiveness destroying a regime.

#### Vietnam: the SRV, the Chinese and minorities after 1975 (1975–2002)

Post-reunification assimilation — forced collectivisation, dismantled autonomous regions, anti-Chinese campaigns — gave way to accommodation under Doi Moi as geopolitics shifted.

- National income fell 2%/year 1976–80; ~75,000/month fled in 1978–79; ~two-thirds of 545,000 boat people (1978–80) ethnic Chinese
- Autonomous Regions dismantled 1975; Central Highlands population 420,000 (1926) → 2.8m (1991) → 4m+ (2001), ~30,000 conflicts (1988–92)
- Doi Moi: GNP growth averaged 8.2% in the 1990s; richest decile 10.6× poorest (1996) rising to 12.5× (2001–02)
- Minority Assembly representation 17% by 2000 (13% of population); 2001 Central Highlands protests

**Why it matters:** The clearest case of national unity policy driven by external Cold War/Sino-Vietnamese dynamics; comparatively stable, but fault-lines emerged late in the period.

#### Malaysia: Malayan Union to the Social Contract (1945–1965)

A colonial racial division of labour left deep ethnic-economic divides; Malay opposition defeated the Malayan Union, and the Alliance’s Social Contract enshrined Malay special position, but the 1963–65 Malaysia experiment ended in race riots and Separation.

- By 1957, 97.5% of rice farmers Malay, 66% of commerce Chinese
- Malayan Union (22 Jan 1946) jus soli citizenship; UMNO formed May 1946; Federation (1 Feb 1948) restored Sultans’ sovereignty
- Alliance won 51/52 seats in 1955; Section 153 enshrined the Malay special position
- Sabah/Sarawak brought in to dilute Singapore’s Chinese; PAP’s ‘Malaysian Malaysia’ challenge; 21 July 1964 race riots; Separation Aug 1965

**Why it matters:** Shows the colonial roots of ethnic division and how Malay dominance remained ‘more symbolic than real’ before 1969, building Malay discontent.

#### Malaysia: 13 May 1969, the NEP and Malay pre-eminence (1969–2000)

After the 1969 riots, UMNO built a Malay-centred framework — the BN, constitutional bans on sensitive issues, Rukunegara and the NEP — while Mahathir’s Bangsa Malaysia sat uneasily with Islamisation.

- Alliance lost its 2/3 majority in 1969; BN formed 1973; 1971 Constitution (Amendment) Act; Rukunegara proclaimed 31 Aug 1970
- NEP: Malay share ~20% by 1990 (target 30%); poverty in Peninsular Malaysia fell from 49% (1970) to 15%
- Malay medium in all state schools/universities by 1982; bumiputera degree enrolment 35.6%→66.7% (1969–80)
- 1971 National Cultural Policy rejected by non-Malays; Bangsa Malaysia from 1991; 2000 survey found ‘very little inter-ethnic mixing’

**Why it matters:** Shows stability since 1969 via managed co-existence, not integration — ‘the politics rather than the economics of the NEP’ stabilised Malaysia (Mohamad).

#### Malaysia: the Orang Asli (1970s–1999)

Recognised as citizens but not bumiputera, the Orang Asli faced assimilation into Malay culture through regroupment and covert Islamisation, which largely failed.

- Less than 0.5% of population (1999)
- Regroupment Schemes (late 1970s) largely failed; covert Islamisation with material incentives
- By 1993, 80.8% lived in poverty
- Orang Asli Association membership rose from 277 (1989) to ~15,000 (1997)

**Why it matters:** Evidence that assimilation strengthened a separate Orang Asli identity rather than producing unity — an exception to Malaysia’s accommodative practice.

#### Singapore: survival, multiculturalism and Shared Values (1961–1993)

Forced independence created a ‘struggle for survival’ narrative; the PAP built CMIO multiculturalism and meritocracy, self-help groups and the 1991 Shared Values, while depoliticising society and keeping democratic processes.

- Lee Kuan Yew called independence a ‘moment of anguish’; EDB established 1961
- NTUC displaced the pro-communist STUC (de-registered 1961); NCMP, NMP, GRC and Elected President (1993)
- Four official languages; MENDAKI (1981), SINDA (1991), CDAC (1992)
- Shared Values White Paper 5 Jan 1991, adopted 15 Jan 1991

**Why it matters:** Multiculturalism was driven by external geopolitics (a Chinese-majority state in a Malay region) and an ‘identity deficit’ (Ang & Stratton); the synthetic identity resonates with few, critics note.

#### Singapore: language, education, religion, National Service and housing (1956–2001)

Bilingualism with English as the neutral medium, religious harmony laws, National Service and HDB ethnic quotas engineered integration, though Malay-Muslim under-representation in sensitive military roles drew criticism.

- 1956 All-Party Report; Nanyang University merged into NUS (1980); bilingual policy from 1969; Nanyang Siang Pau executives arrested 1971
- National Education (1997), compulsory Social Studies (2001)
- Maintenance of Religious Harmony Act (1990); National Service compulsory from 1967
- HDB quotas (max ~87% Chinese, 25% Malay, 13% Indian/Others); Malay household income fell from 74% to 70% of Chinese (1980–1990)

**Why it matters:** Most socially stable case study (no racial violence since independence), but ethnic income gaps and contested identity show co-existence is not the same as a common Singaporean identity.

## P2 Theme II · SEA economic change

*How did Southeast Asian states pursue growth, equity and economic nationalism after independence (to 2000), why did some succeed where others failed, and how necessary was government intervention?*

Every SEA government pursued three aims that often conflicted: growth, equity and economic nationalism. Colonial powers left no indigenous business class or planning institutions, so in every state – capitalist or socialist – the government had to lead. The real discriminator is not WHETHER the state intervened but HOW (direct SOE control vs indirect incentive-setting) and HOW WELL (efficient vs corrupt/cronyist).

Owen groups the states as capitalist (Philippines, Thailand, Malaysia, Singapore, New Order Indonesia, South Vietnam), socialist (Burma post-1962, North Vietnam, SRV post-1975) and ‘Third Way’ (U Nu’s Burma, Guided Democracy Indonesia). The capitalist economies broadly followed ISI → EOI → diversification; Salvatore argues the key was transitioning from ISI to EOI at the right time. Singapore moved fastest; Thailand and Malaysia succeeded by the mid-1980s; Indonesia only partially; the Philippines failed.

The socialist economies had to make a more dramatic pivot away from central planning altogether. Burma – best endowed at independence – became the region’s worst performer through extreme nationalisation and autarky, then SLORC’s contradictory opening. Vietnam failed equally badly in 1976–85 but pragmatically reformed through Doi Moi (1986), achieving 8–9.5% growth in the 1990s.

Growth rarely brought equity: Gini, urban–rural and regional gaps widened in Thailand, Indonesia, Singapore and post-Doi Moi Vietnam, while Burma’s ‘equity’ came amid poverty. The 1997 Asian Financial Crisis exposed the trade-off between liberalisation-driven growth and financial stability – Thailand and Indonesia worst hit, Singapore and the Philippines comparatively sheltered by sound regulation.

**The three economic aims**

- Growth: increase in GDP/productive capacity, usually via industrialisation (ISI → EOI, agricultural modernisation, FDI); can be achieved while equity is sacrificed
- Equity: fair distribution across population/ethnic groups (land reform, redistribution, affirmative action e.g. NEP); pursuing it over efficiency often reduces productivity
- Economic nationalism: indigenising wealth, reducing foreign/minority control (nationalisation, restrictions, protectionism); excluding foreign capital (e.g. Burma) starves the economy of capital and competition
- Ne Win model answer: equity (top 25% earned only 5× the bottom 25%) and nationalism achieved but growth failed – a hollow victory, so not a success overall

**ISI vs EOI and the miracle economies**

- ISI: tariffs protect ‘infant’ industries to replace imports – diversifies and builds a base, but breeds complacency, inefficiency and cronyism (licences/quotas as favours)
- EOI: produce goods of comparative advantage for export – overcomes small domestic markets and forces efficiency, but can undermine nationalism via foreign dependence
- Salvatore: ISI and EOI are sequential, not alternatives – success depends on transitioning at the right time; Philippines and Indonesia stuck in ISI too long
- Four ‘miracle’ factors: strong government commitment, sound policies, controlled integration into the global market, close security/economic ties to the US
- World Bank (The East Asian Miracle, 1993): high savings/investment, pro-investment taxes, well-regulated bank-based finance, pro-export incentives, openness to foreign tech/investment

**Role of the government (Tarling)**

- Government had to lead: colonial economies were extractive, not developmental – only the state had the capital and oversight to fill the vacuum
- Planning: national development plans (Malaysia’s First Malaysia Plan; Indonesia’s Repelita) vs centralised Five-/Four-Year Plans (Vietnam, Burma)
- Participation: indirect in capitalist states (stability, fiscal policy, technocrats, FDI + some SOEs like JTC, FELDA, Pertamina) vs direct and total in socialist states
- Spectrum: Singapore’s state stimulated private capital; Thailand’s state genuinely scaled back; Indonesia’s ‘bureaucratic capitalism’ and the Philippines’ ‘patrimonialist plunder’ captured it

**Other factors: ethnic Chinese, foreign aid, technology**

- Chinese share of listed-firm capitalisation (1980s): Singapore 70% of population/81%; Thailand 10%/81%; Indonesia 3.8%/73%; Malaysia 28%/69%; Philippines 1.3%/50–60%
- Political response varied: accommodated (Thailand), restricted via NEP (Malaysia), violent tension and cukong system (Indonesia), co-opted then backlash (Philippines), expelled (Burma, Vietnam’s Hoa)
- Cold War aid: US to Thailand; USSR/China to Vietnam; Japanese aid as Japan rose; World Bank, IMF, ADB
- Green Revolution: IR8 (IRRI, Philippines, mid-1960s) averted famine and created exportable surplus
- Electronics take-off (late 1970s–80s): Singapore’s Second Industrial Revolution most successful; Burma, Vietnam, Indonesia struggled

### Case studies

#### Thailand: 1932 Revolution and Phibun’s state-led nationalism (1932–57)

Never colonised, Thailand already had an active Chinese business class; after 1932 Phibun pushed ‘a Thai economy for the Thai people’ and built state enterprises, but most were joint ventures with Chinese businessmen.

- 1929 survey: 20,259 rice traders, 3,709 rice millers, 12,738 timber traders
- By 1945, 30 state enterprises; Thai Rice Company handled 70–80% of export rice by 1939
- Hewison: ‘haphazard state-led development’; government share of domestic investment 32% (1952) → 38% (1953–55)
- 1952 directive forcing Chinese into centralised associations; Shell Oil threatened with closure
- Sophonphanich family (Bangkok Bank) expanded despite nationalist policy

**Why it matters:** Shows economic nationalism did NOT replace private business – the autonomous Chinese bourgeoisie checked the state, the root of Thailand’s later successful EOI transition.

#### Burma: U Nu’s Pyidawtha Plan (Third Way) (1948–62)

A Buddhist-principled welfare plan (Aug 1952) aimed at an agro-based economy with land redistribution, but state monopolies and poor implementation made it fail by 1956.

- Burma was arguably BETTER endowed than its neighbours at independence (resources, literacy, land-man ratio)
- Saya San Rebellion (1930–31) against Chettiar moneylenders shaped later policy toward Indians
- Target: reallocate 10 million acres by 1955 – only 1.4 million redistributed by 1958
- SAMB bought rice below global price; rice price slump of -25% (1955–56)
- GDP only reached pre-war levels by 1958–59; DSI (1951) foreshadowed military-economic dominance

**Why it matters:** The ‘Third Way’ example: ideology over sound management; proves resource endowment is not destiny – policy choices determined Burma’s outcome.

#### Indonesia: weak foundation and Sukarno’s Guided Economy (1949–65)

With no domestic bourgeoisie, the state led out of necessity; from 1957 Sukarno nationalised Dutch property, discriminated against the Chinese and poured money into heavy-industry ISI, causing economic collapse.

- ~300 Dutch plantations + 300 firms nationalised (1957)
- 1959 8-year plan: 12-fold increase in project expenditure without a clear financing source
- Konfrontasi (1963–66) and West Irian seizure (1961–62) drained finances; 1964 ‘go to hell with your aid’
- Hyper-inflation over 2000 per cent a year by the mid-1960s; GNP per capita declined 1957–65 (Tarling)
- Exports US$931m (1959) → US$685m (1966); forex reserves US$313m → US$17m (1960–67)

**Why it matters:** Classic failed ISI driven by nationalism and inexperience; supports the argument that intervention was necessary but its quality determined results.

#### Philippines: early ISI, Filipino First and land reform (1950–65)

Import and forex controls produced rapid early ISI – ‘Asia’s second NIC after Japan’ – alongside anti-Chinese economic nationalism and land reforms blocked by the ilustrado Congress.

- Manufacturing 8% → 20% of GDP and agriculture 42% → 26% (1950–60)
- Retail Trade Nationalization Act (1954); 1958 bill required 60%+ Filipino ownership
- Filipino share of import trade 23% → 70% (1948–65)
- Magsaysay defeated the Huks by 1954; Land Reform Act (1955) and Macapagal’s Code (1963) watered down by Congress
- US$620m Rehabilitation Act aid in exchange for the Bell Act

**Why it matters:** High protection discouraged efficiency, so the Philippines failed to transition to EOI; the land-reform arc is the key synthesis point for equity essays.

#### Vietnam: divided North and South economies (1954–75)

Geneva (1954) split Vietnam into a US-aid-dependent capitalist South and a Soviet/Chinese-backed socialist North that collectivised agriculture and prioritised heavy industry.

- South: rice exports ceased after 1965; imports 12× exports; collapse when US withdrew
- North: >90% of sectors nationalised by 1965; industrial output +15%/year (1959–64), 80% of aid from Moscow
- Work points awarded by TIME spent killed incentives
- Five percent plots produced 2–3× the yield and 60–75% of farmer income – ignored by Le Duan in 1974
- US bombing (1966–75): 9 of 30 provincial capitals completely destroyed

**Why it matters:** Shows the inherent flaws of collectivisation and aid-dependence that underlay the post-1975 crisis and the later case for Doi Moi.

#### Singapore: foreign reports, EDB and ISI under Malaysia (1955–65)

An entrepot with no resources or hinterland, Singapore followed foreign reports into government-led industrialisation and planned ISI inside a Malaysian common market that never materialised.

- 1950s growth rate <2%; 1947 workforce: services 30.9%, commerce 23.2%, agriculture 8.1%
- IBRD Report (1955), Lyle Report (1959), Winsemius Report (1961) → first Development Plan (1961–64)
- EDB (Aug 1961): ‘one-stop investment agency’; spawned DBS, National Productivity Board, JTC by 1968
- Exports 126.7% of GDP in 1950–60 – inherently open economy

**Why it matters:** Explains why Singapore’s path differed: no fallback primary sector, so it could skip most of ISI.

#### Thailand: Sarit and kanphatthana (1957–77)

Sarit shifted the state to an indirect role – infrastructure, planning and promotion of private and foreign investment – backed by heavy US aid and agricultural expansion.

- BoI, IFCT, NESDB (1959); Promotion of Investment Act (1960)
- Irrigated land 600,000 ha (1947) → 2.2 million ha (1969)
- 1951–69: over $900m US aid/loans; foreign capital only 12% of capital formation (1964–72)
- Manufactured exports 1.2% (1960) → 32.3% (1980) of total exports
- 15–20 Sino-Thai families main beneficiaries; Thanom/Praphas/Narong in 137 firms by 1973; protection more than doubled in the 1970s

**Why it matters:** Domestic capital stayed dominant (unlike Indonesia/Philippines) – but also shows crony over-protection, useful for balanced evaluation.

#### Malaysia: laissez-faire ISI, merger and the 1969 riots (1957–71)

Malaysia inherited an ethnic division of labour and tin/rubber dependence; non-discretionary ISI under the Pioneer Industries Programme stalled, and racial-economic inequity fuelled the 13 May 1969 riots.

- Tin & rubber = 85% of export earnings at independence (1957)
- Pioneer-status firms 18 → 246 (1959–71); consumption goods share 21.9% → 31.2%
- Non-discretionary incentives; manufacturing stagnated at 9% of GDP in the 1960s
- Merger (1963) and Singapore’s Separation (1965) exposed racial/economic inequity
- ‘Race became the leitmotiv’ of Malaysian politics (Drabble)

**Why it matters:** Sets up the NEP: shows the cost of ISI without a targeted strategy and why equity became urgent.

#### Burma: Ne Win’s Burmese Way to Socialism (1962–88)

From 30 April 1962 Ne Win nationalised virtually everything, expelled minorities and sealed the economy; agriculture and exports collapsed, and the 1970s Four-Year Plans brought only shallow recovery.

- By end-1965 state controlled 60% of manufacturing and 90% of legal trade
- Rice exports 1,521,000 tons (1962/63) → 204,000 tons (1972/73)
- ~1,000 private firms closed, ~2 million workers unemployed; visas restricted to 24 hours (1964)
- Hmaung-kho black market: 70–80% of goods in Yangon/Mandalay by early 1980s
- 2nd Four-Year Plan growth 5.9% (1976–77), 6.4% (1977–78), but Burma became a net importer of oil and rice in the 1980s

**Why it matters:** The ultimate cautionary tale on extreme economic nationalism; achieved equity and nationalism but catastrophic growth failure.

#### Singapore: forced EOI after Separation and British withdrawal (1965–79)

Losing its hinterland (1965) and British bases (1971), Singapore pivoted decisively to EOI under Goh Keng Swee through export incentives, manpower training and aggressive courting of FDI.

- British withdrawal: loss of 40,000 jobs = 10% of national income
- Technical/vocational enrolment rose 8× (1966–76)
- 1978: 100% foreign ownership allowed (‘major turning point’)
- Lost man-days 129,893 (1960) → 1,001 (1977); NTUC + National Wages Council (1971)
- Foreign-affiliated firms = 71% of exports by 1976; manufacturing 13.2% (1960) → 29.1% (1980) of GDP

**Why it matters:** Fastest ISI→EOI transition in the region; crisis-driven pragmatism over nationalism.

#### Philippines: Marcos, dependent state capitalism and crony capitalism (1965–86)

Marcos expanded state corporations and foreign borrowing under martial law; growth was loan-financed, cronies were bailed out, land reform became a political weapon and the 1983 Aquino assassination triggered crisis.

- Foreign debt US$2.2bn (1972) → US$25bn (1983); US$27bn before Marcos’ 1986 fall
- Sugar 67 cents/lb (1974) → 7 cents/lb (1978)
- PD 27 (Oct 1972): holdings >7 hectares liable for sale – Aquino family land among the first seized
- Benedicto’s sugar losses (14bn pesos) and Lucio Tan’s Fortune Tobacco favours
- Rice 3.9m tons (1961) → 7.9m tons (1981); tariffs cut to max 50% (1981–85) under structural adjustment

**Why it matters:** Key example of ‘patrimonialist plunder’ – political criteria replacing economic ones – and of failure to transition to EOI.

#### Indonesia: New Order recovery and the oil boom (1966–81)

Suharto staked legitimacy on development: the Berkeley Mafia courted Western aid and FDI, and the 1970s oil windfall funded infrastructure, the Green Revolution and state ISI corporations.

- 1967 Foreign Investment Law; IGGI (14 nations) formed 1967; ASEAN formed 8 Aug 1967
- Oil export revenue grew 45.5% a year (1970–81) (Vatikiotis); oil <US$3 (1972) → >US$30/barrel (1980)
- Rice 12m tons (1968) → 29m tons (1989); self-sufficiency by mid-1980s
- 1975: state firms produced 75% of cement
- Repelita I (1969); Article 33 constitutional statism

**Why it matters:** Growth driven by a commodity windfall rather than industrial transformation – less structurally robust than it first appears.

#### Malaysia: the New Economic Policy (1971–90)

After 1969 the NEP sought national unity by reducing poverty and ending the identification of race with economic function, using state agencies to restructure ownership while still courting FDI.

- 1970 ownership: 63% foreign, 35% other Malaysians, 2% Malay; target Malay share 30%
- FELDA, RISDA, PERNAS, PNB (bought Guthrie 1981); Petronas nationalised oil (1974)
- Free Trade Zone Act (1971) and Investment Incentives Act (1968) courted FDI
- Bumiputera equity 2.4% → 20.3%; poverty 40.3% → 15% (1971–90)
- Fell short of 30%; rewards to a connected bumiputera ‘oligopoly’

**Why it matters:** Best balance of the three aims among the case studies; the core equity-vs-growth and nationalism-vs-FDI case.

#### Indonesia: the Pertamina affair (1974–76)

Pertamina’s director Ibnu Sutowo funded lavish projects on foreign borrowing, assuming high oil prices; in Nov 1974 the company was found to owe US$10bn.

- Debt of US$10bn ≈ 2/3 of Indonesia’s GNP
- Oil prices stabilised in March 1974, contrary to Sutowo’s assumption
- Sutowo forced to resign (1976); credibility with creditors severely damaged
- Robison: growth of ‘state-sponsored private business groups’ – bureaucratic capitalism

**Why it matters:** Illustrates how state participation became a vehicle for unaccountable borrowing and patronage.

#### Vietnam: reunification, 2nd FYP failure and early decentralisation (1976–85)

The 2nd FYP imposed heavy industry and collectivisation on the South, abolishing private trade (Mar 1978); fierce peasant resistance and lack of funds caused failure, prompting Directive 100 (1981) and the 3rd FYP.

- Only 0.6% of Mekong Delta households in cooperatives by 1982
- Rice 11.83m tons (1976) → 9.79m tons (1979)
- Harvest target 21m tons vs 12m achieved
- China cancelled aid (1978); US never paid promised $3bn; 50% of expenditure to defence after 1979
- 3rd FYP (1981–85): industry +~10%/yr, grain +~5%/yr

**Why it matters:** Shows the same socialist flaws as Burma (heavy industry first, collectivisation, ideology over expertise) – the baseline for Vietnam’s later pragmatism.

#### Thailand: the switch to EOI (1977–96)

Oil shocks, the end of US aid and rising debt led the 4th Plan (1977–81) to switch from ISI to EOI; export promotion, baht-peg management and the Plaza Accord boom drove industrialisation as the state scaled back.

- External debt 0% → 38% of GDP (1973–86); debt service ratio 19.7%
- 1985: export taxes abolished; 100% foreign ownership if exporting most output
- 1981 devaluation and 1984 basket peg; Plaza Accord (1985) made Thai exports cheap
- Export growth 14% in 1980s – highest of capitalist SEA; manufacturing 16.4% (1952) → 33.9% (2000)
- 3 main exports fell 28.7% → 23.3% – diversification

**Why it matters:** With Malaysia, the only capitalist state to make a successful sustained EOI transition by the mid-1980s; ‘the market compensated for government failures’.

#### Singapore: Second Industrial Revolution to services and regionalisation (1979–1990s)

Facing labour shortage, Singapore moved to high-value technology (1979), suffered the 1985 recession, then adopted manufacturing and services as twin engines and regionalised in the 1990s.

- Value-added per worker nearly doubled (1979–84)
- 1985 recession: high wages, global slowdown, protectionism; shipbuilding ¼ of manufacturing hit
- 1986 Economic Committee (Lee Hsien Loong): ‘total business centre’
- Services share of GDP 16% (1965) → 24% (1985)
- Direct investment abroad S$8.7bn → S$16.9bn (1989–90); Johor-Riau Growth Triangle, Suzhou Industrial Park

**Why it matters:** Falling manufacturing share reflects deliberate upgrading, not failure; also shows external vulnerability.

#### Malaysia: Look East, HICOM and privatisation (1981–1990s)

Mahathir’s state-led heavy industry (HICOM) built a bumiputera industrial class but incurred heavy losses, leading to privatisation, ‘Malaysia Incorporated’ and growth-focused industrial deepening.

- HICOM (1981): Proton, Perwaja Steel, Kedah Cement
- Public enterprises = 64.3% of public sector deficit (1980–85)
- 13 SOEs privatised by 1992; ringgit devalued (1985)
- Manufacturing = 60% of exports by 1990 (vs 20% a decade earlier)
- Rasiah: Mahathir not prepared to reach 30% equity ‘at the expense of checking the pace of industrialisation’

**Why it matters:** Shows growth was prioritised over equity once the NEP formally ended in 1990.

#### Indonesia: oil glut, deregulation and crony monopolies (1982–mid-1990s)

Falling oil prices forced deregulation and a turn to EOI; statistics improved, but deregulation largely transferred opportunities to Sino-Indonesian conglomerates and the Suharto family.

- Oil tax revenue plunged 27%; growth halved by 1985
- October 1988 package: 40+ new private bank licences in 2 years
- Non-oil exports 31% → 50%; manufactured exports <US$1bn (1982) → >US$9bn (1990)
- Krakatau Steel deal (1984): import monopoly added 25–45% to steel sheet costs
- Astra 40% and Indomobil 21% of auto production; cukong system after Malari (1974)

**Why it matters:** ‘Deregulation reinforced rather than undermined state power’ – partial EOI stunted by cronyism.

#### Burma: the 1985 and 1987 demonetisations (1985, Sept 1987)

To hit black marketeers, Ne Win voided banknotes in 1985 and, without any exchange, in Sept 1987, wiping out ordinary Burmese savings and triggering the 1988 crisis.

- 1985: 20/50/100-kyat notes declared worthless; ≤5,000 kyat exchangeable
- Sept 1987: 25/35/75-kyat notes voided with NO exchange mechanism
- Only 45/90-kyat notes (multiples of 9) remained legal tender
- Led to dissent (late 1987) → 8888 Uprising (Aug 1988) → SLORC
- Only 13 of ~40,000 private establishments employed >50 workers

**Why it matters:** The final straw of Ne Win’s mismanagement – reform in Burma came reactively, via regime collapse.

#### Vietnam: Doi Moi (1986–2000)

After Le Duan’s death, Nguyen Van Linh’s 6th Congress (Dec 1986) launched a ‘market economy with socialist direction’ modelled on Deng’s China, privatising agriculture and opening to FDI while keeping one-party rule.

- Foreign Investment Law (1987); central planning formally discarded by 1989
- Resolution 10 (April 1988): 10–15 year land tenure, quotas vanished
- 1986–90: growth 3% → 6%; trade $1bn → $2bn
- GDP growth 9.5% (1995–96), highest in the world; 3rd-largest rice exporter
- US embargo lifted Feb 1994; ASEAN membership 1995

**Why it matters:** THE key example of socialist pragmatism – re-balancing the three aims when the original mix failed.

#### Philippines: Aquino’s recovery and Ramos’ Philippines 2000 (1986–98)

Aquino dismantled crony monopolies but was crippled by debt and shocks, and CARP was diluted; Ramos liberalised trade, privatised and broke monopolies, though elites still dominated.

- Debt repayment consumed 10% of GDP annually under Aquino
- Gulf War, base closures (70,000 jobs), Mt Pinatubo (1991); growth fell below 3% (1990–91)
- Aquino left CARP details to a landowner-dominated Congress
- 1993 telecom liberalisation: 13 entrants but PLDT still far ahead; privatisation revenue $12bn
- By 1998: 4.6 million of 8.1 million hectares distributed

**Why it matters:** Shows the persistence of elite capture and Marcos-era debt; export concentration rose 27.4% → 64.7% – fragile industrialisation.

#### Burma: SLORC’s contradictory market opening (1988–97)

SLORC’s Foreign Investment Law (Nov 1988) opened Burma rhetorically to the market while state and army monopolies continued and repression brought Western sanctions.

- FIL: wholly foreign-owned enterprises / JVs (min. 35% foreign stake)
- Dec 1990 ‘open-door’ policy yet banned export of 28 goods by 1994
- Tucker: SLORC ‘lifted restrictions on everything except most things Burma produced and traded’
- By end-1997 only 5% of large firms privately owned; no large SOE privatised
- Rice exports 1 million tons (1994) → 353,000 tons (1995); joined ASEAN 1997

**Why it matters:** Contrast with Vietnam: incoherent, reactive reform plus pariah status explains why Burma failed where Vietnam succeeded.

#### The 1997 Asian Financial Crisis compared (1997)

Financial liberalisation without regulatory capacity left Thailand and Indonesia exposed to short-term capital flight; Malaysia suffered domestic asset overheating, while Singapore and the Philippines were sheltered by sound regulation.

- Thailand: BIBF (1993); foreign bank debt US$29bn → US$69bn, 70% short-term; baht floated 2 July 1997
- Indonesia: banks 124 → 244 (1988–94); Bank Central Asia 60% of loans to Liem firms
- Malaysia: KLSE 1,238 → 594 points (1996–97); property loans 45–55% of lending
- Singapore: 15.4% current account surplus (1997); no external debt since 1995
- Philippines: leverage 6.90, NPLs 3%; peso floated 11 July 1997 – ‘avoided the excesses’ (Delhaise)

**Why it matters:** Supports a trade-off argument: the factors behind explosive growth created vulnerability; the Philippines is the contrast case for nuanced AFC essays.

### Historians

- **Owen:** Categorises SEA economies as capitalist, socialist or ‘Third Way’.
- **Salvatore:** ISI and EOI are sequential, not alternatives; success depended on transitioning at the right time.
- **Tarling:** Government played two roles, planning and participation; Sukarno’s nationalist answer was economically disastrous.
- **World Bank (The East Asian Miracle, 1993):** A ‘recipe’ of high savings, pro-investment taxes, well-regulated finance, pro-export incentives and openness explained the miracle.
- **Drabble:** ‘Race became the leitmotiv’ of Malaysian politics, with direct economic consequences.
- **Rasiah:** Mahathir would not achieve the 30% bumiputera equity target at the expense of the pace of industrialisation.
- **Lim Chong Yah:** Singapore succeeded through free enterprise, outward orientation and able, honest government – state leadership was ‘crucial, critical, and indispensable’.
- **Rodan:** Singapore saw the ‘institutionalisation of interaction between capital and state’ through cooption of business.
- **Tucker:** SLORC’s market reforms were contradictory – it ‘lifted restrictions on everything except most things Burma produced and traded’, and the army kept control.
- **Than & Tan:** Burma’s decline is telling against neighbours with a similar resource base, so it is attributable to policy, not endowment.
- **Hla Myint (1971):** Investors care less about tax ‘handouts’ than reduced bureaucracy and long-term political/security certainty.
- **Duiker:** Post-1975 Vietnam had difficulty absorbing foreign aid even when it was provided.
- **Vasavakul:** Abolishing central planning was not synonymous with the decline of the state economic sector in Vietnam.
- **Hewison:** Phibun’s was ‘haphazard state-led development’; Hewison quotes the view that Thailand is a case where the market compensated for government failures.
- **Pasuk & Baker:** The Thai state helped start many enterprises from the mid-1930s, yet domestic (Thai) capital remained dominant.
- **Vatikiotis:** Indonesia was lucky with the 1970s oil boom; deregulation simply opened new areas for the larger business groups.
- **Robison:** New Order ‘bureaucratic capitalism’: officials treated offices as personal fiefs, filling the vacuum left by a weak national bourgeoisie.
- **Rodan, Hewison & Robison:** Indonesian deregulation reinforced rather than undermined state power and corporate concentration.
- **Morley:** The striking feature of Indonesian business after 1986 was the huge increase in wealth accruing to the Suharto family.
- **Doner & Ramsay:** Thailand’s lack of industrial upgrading caught up with it in 1996.
- **Akyuz:** The BIBF became a conduit for short-term foreign lending into Thailand.
- **Abinales & Amoroso:** Restored Philippine democracy was welcome but its ability to foster prosperity was found wanting; Marcos-era debt funded non-income-generating structures.
- **De Dios & Hutchcroft:** The Philippines suffered a ‘foreign debt hemorrhage’ that limited Aquino’s options.
- **Delhaise:** Philippine banks were among Asia’s best managed; the country missed explosive growth but avoided the excesses.
- **Montes:** Philippine governance was not good enough for faster growth but good enough to prevent large policy errors.

### Using this topic in the exam

- Weigh all three aims – never judge success on growth alone; use Ne Win’s Burma (equity and nationalism without growth = hollow) as the model weighing.
- Intervention was universal: argue about its NATURE and DIRECTION (market-stimulating Singapore/Thailand vs market-replacing Burma or crony-captured Indonesia/Philippines), not whether it occurred.
- Cleanest comparisons: Thailand (success) vs Philippines (failure) for ISI→EOI, with Indonesia as the oil-cushioned middle case; Burma vs Vietnam under socialism; Singapore vs Malaysia on resources and ethnic politics.
- Ethnic Chinese dominance was region-wide; what is arguable is whether it was stabilising (Thailand) or a source of instability (Indonesia, Philippines) – cite the Rigg 1980s table.
- For AFC essays use the comparative vulnerability table and the Philippines/Singapore as contrast cases to show a growth-vs-stability trade-off rather than a single cause.
- Don’t ignore starting points (Burma best endowed, Singapore with nothing) and always cite data – GDP growth rates, export % of GDP, equity ownership %.

## P2 Theme III · ASEAN

*Why was ASEAN formed in 1967, and how effective has it been in promoting regional economic cooperation and building regional stability and security?*

ASEAN was founded in Bangkok on 8 Aug 1967 by Indonesia, Malaysia, Philippines, Singapore and Thailand after earlier attempts failed: SEATO (externally initiated), ASA (sabotaged by the Sabah dispute) and Maphilindo (stillborn amid Konfrontasi). A transformed regional environment after 1965 — Singapore’s separation, Suharto’s New Order, Marcos’s moderation, British and US disengagement — made sustainable indigenous cooperation possible.

The motivation is contested. The official stance stressed economic, social and cultural aims, but founding fathers (Rajaratnam, Malik, LKY) privately confirm politico-security concerns were central. The notes conclude that security and economic aims were interlinked and each member had its own primary motivation.

ASEAN developed in three phases: loose confidence-building (1967–1976); increased security activity and formalisation after the 1976 Bali Summit and the Vietnamese invasion of Cambodia (1976–1992); and marked institutionalisation with economics at the fore after the 1992 Singapore Summit. Throughout, the ASEAN Way — informality, consensus, non-interference — was both its strength and its limit.

In economics, early schemes (PTA, AIP, AIC, AIJV) failed because of the ISI mindset, while AFTA cut tariffs sharply but left NTBs and a stagnant intra-ASEAN trade share. In security, ASEAN managed disputes without armed conflict and won standing in the Cambodian Crisis, but ZOPFAN stayed aspirational, the High Council was never used, and the South China Sea disputes persist.

**Reasons for the formation of ASEAN**

- Transformed regional environment: Singapore–Malaysia separation (Aug 1965), Suharto’s New Order ends Konfrontasi, Marcos recognises Malaysia (Jun 1966)
- External uncertainty: British ‘East of Suez’ withdrawal announced Jan 1968; Nixon Doctrine (1969)
- Anti-communist / politico-security motives: North Vietnam and PRC backing communist movements; Domino Theory; fear of US abandonment
- Economic, social and cultural aims of the Bangkok Declaration; Suharto’s Indonesia needed Western aid and investment
- Resolving inter-state tensions: a framework to ‘bury the hatchet’, esp. Indonesia–Malaysia and Malaysia–Philippines
- Different primary motivations by member: Indonesia (leadership, credibility), Malaysia (bilateral disputes, development), Philippines (Sabah forum), Singapore (ASEAN as ‘cage’ for larger neighbours), Thailand (communist threat)

**Growth, principles and the ASEAN Way**

- Three phases: 1967–1976 loose cooperation; 1976–1992 greater formalisation, especially in security; 1992 onwards institutionalisation with economics at the fore
- Bangkok Declaration (1967) seven aims; TAC (1976) six principles incl. non-interference, peaceful settlement, renunciation of force
- ASEAN Way (Severino): informality, personal relations among leaders, consensus rather than binding commitments
- Strengths: accommodates diversity, keeps all parties at the table, preserves sovereignty; weaknesses: vague agreements (‘plans more than action’ — Tay), slow, no enforcement
- Non-interference evolved inconsistently: Cambodia’s entry delayed (1997), Badawi advises Myanmar (Jun 1997), ‘flexible engagement’ becomes ‘enhanced interaction’ (1998), AICHR under 2008 Charter

**Regional economic cooperation**

- Early schemes (1976–1992) failed: ISI mindset meant members saw each other as competitors; exports (rubber, tin, timber, palm oil) went to developed-world markets (UNIDO 1983)
- Mahathir (1991): ASEAN’s record in economic cooperation ‘has been dismal’
- AFTA (1992) via CEPT driven by end of Cold War, rival blocs (EU, NAFTA, MERCOSUR) and the shift to EOI
- AFC (1997) led to ASEAN Surveillance Process, ASEAN+3 (1999) and Chiang Mai Initiative
- External relations: Dialogue Partners (from 1977), reluctant APEC membership (1989), ASEAN–EU ties strained by Myanmar

**Regional stability and security**

- Security context: UK withdrawal East of Suez, Tet Offensive, Nixon Doctrine, Sino-US rapprochement (1971), communist victories in Indochina (1975)
- Intra-ASEAN tensions persisted (Sabah 1968–69, Singapore–Indonesia 1968) but ASEAN meetings acted as ‘cooling-off’ platforms
- ZOPFAN (1971) and TAC/High Council (1976) set norms but were aspirational or unused
- Cambodian Crisis (1978–1991) showed both unity and disunity; ARF (1994) and SCS diplomacy show ASEAN’s convening power but limited leverage over China

### Case studies

#### SEATO (Sep 1954–1977)

A US-initiated anti-communist alliance modelled on NATO, the brainchild of John Foster Dulles, which failed and was formally dissolved in 1977.

- Only 2 SEA members (Thailand, Philippines) out of 8 — a misnomer by name
- Divergent national interests: Pakistan joined to counter India; Philippines cared about internal security; Thailand saw itself as frontline state
- US disengaged after Tet Offensive (1968) and Paris Peace Accords (1973)
- Could not stop Pathet Lao taking Laos and Khmer Rouge taking Cambodia (both 1975)
- Dissolved 1977 — a ‘paper tiger’

**Why it matters:** Shows why externally initiated cooperation failed and why Thailand, disappointed by SEATO’s unreliability, looked to a regional organisation.

#### Association of Southeast Asia (ASA) (Jul 1961)

An intra-regional grouping of Malaya, Thailand and the Philippines initiated by Tunku Abdul Rahman, sabotaged by the Sabah dispute but later the basis of ASEAN.

- Tunku’s slogan: ‘Asian solutions to Asian problems’
- Sukarno’s Indonesia opposed it as ‘anti-this and anti-that’ — its absence was fatal
- Philippine claim to Sabah (1962) caused a Malaysia–Philippines diplomatic rupture
- Revived March 1966; ASEAN built on ASA’s foundations
- Turnbull: ASA was a ‘new and significant step’ arising from local initiatives

**Why it matters:** Shows indigenous roots of regionalism and that cooperation was impossible without Indonesia and while bilateral disputes festered.

#### Maphilindo (Jul–Sep 1963)

A Philippines-convened loose confederation of Malaya, the Philippines and Indonesia (Manila Accord, 31 Jul 1963) that collapsed within 2 months.

- Manila Accord signed 31 Jul 1963
- Collapsed when Malaysia was formally created in Sep 1963; Malaysia–Philippines ties severed
- Formed against the backdrop of Konfrontasi (began Apr 1963)
- Chin Kin Wah: ‘stillborn given its hasty conception against the backdrop of Confrontation’

**Why it matters:** Contrast case: ASEAN was more promising because of the transformed environment after Konfrontasi ended and Indonesia was included.

#### Formation of ASEAN (Bangkok Declaration) (8 Aug 1967)

Indonesia, Malaysia, Philippines, Singapore and Thailand founded ASEAN in Bangkok with a deliberately vague declaration stressing economic, social and cultural cooperation.

- Catalysts: Singapore separation (Aug 1965), Suharto’s New Order, Marcos recognises Malaysia (Jun 1966), Konfrontasi terminated (Aug 1966)
- Bangkok Declaration aim: ‘to accelerate economic growth, social progress and cultural development’
- Rajaratnam (1992): ASEAN ‘was born… out of fear rather than idealistic convictions about regionalism’
- LKY: ‘We were banding together more for political objectives, stability and security’
- Thanat Khoman: ASEAN deliberately excluded military entanglement

**Why it matters:** Central to the primary-motivation controversy (common SBQ): security and economic aims were interlinked, and each member had its own priority.

#### Sabah tensions and the Corregidor Affair (1968–Dec 1969)

The Philippines secretly trained Sabah operatives (Corregidor Affair / Jabidah Massacre) only 7 months after ASEAN’s founding, and Malaysia–Philippines ties broke down.

- Corregidor Affair / Jabidah Massacre (1968)
- Sep 1968 Republic Act 5446 asserted sovereignty over Sabah → both withdrew ambassadors; ASEAN activities suspended
- Ties only restored Dec 1969 at 3rd AMM in Cameron Highlands
- ASEAN meetings used as ‘cooling-off’ platforms; informal diplomacy prevented armed hostilities

**Why it matters:** Supports the argument that ASEAN could not resolve disputes but managed them without armed conflict — the ‘early ASEAN Way of conflict management’ (Acharya).

#### Singapore–Indonesia tensions (Oct 1968)

Singapore executed the MacDonald House bombers, and the Singapore embassy in Jakarta was ransacked.

- October 1968 executions of MacDonald House bombers
- Jakarta embassy ransacked
- Tensions lasted years

**Why it matters:** Evidence that intra-ASEAN tensions persisted after 1967; useful for arguing ASEAN’s early limits.

#### ZOPFAN (27 Nov 1971)

The Zone of Peace, Freedom and Neutrality, championed by Malaysia (Tun Razak / Tun Ismail), declared SEA should be free from interference by outside powers.

- Signed 27 Nov 1971 in KL
- Stances: Indonesia strong supporter; Malaysia architect; Philippines and Thailand cautious; Singapore sceptical (balance of power, FPDA)
- Never fully implemented — Indochinese wars continued with US, USSR, China involvement
- SEANWFZ (1995) came 24 years later

**Why it matters:** Shows absence of common security interests; Narine says the Cambodian Crisis ‘symbolically undermined ZOPFAN’, but Leifer sees it as a long-term aspiration that kept leaders in contact.

#### South China Sea disputes (1974–2018)

Overlapping claims by China, Vietnam, Malaysia, the Philippines, Brunei (and Indonesia’s Natuna EEZ) led to repeated Chinese assertion and ASEAN attempts at a code of conduct.

- 1974: China seizes Paracel Islands from South Vietnam
- Mar 1988 Johnson South Reef: China sinks 3 Vietnamese ships; kills 64
- Jan 1995: China occupies Mischief Reef (Philippines EEZ); May 1995 China rejects ARF role
- 2002 Declaration of Conduct; Aug 2018 Single Draft Negotiating Text
- 2012: AMM fails to issue joint communiqué for FIRST TIME — Cambodia blocks SCS language

**Why it matters:** Shows the limits of the ASEAN Way and disunity; China prefers bilateral talks, and a Code of Conduct manages behaviour rather than settling sovereignty.

#### 1976 Bali Summit and the TAC (Feb 1976)

The first summit of the 5 heads of government, called after communist victories in Indochina, produced the ASEAN Concord, the TAC and a central Secretariat.

- Context: Fall of Saigon (1975), communist victories in Laos and Cambodia, US and British withdrawal
- Outputs: Declaration of ASEAN Concord; TAC with High Council; central ASEAN Secretariat; AEMM
- ‘Atmospherics of great consequence’ (Australian DFA officer)
- High Council never used — rules of procedure only adopted in 2001; Sipadan-Ligitan went to ICJ instead
- TAC acceded to by Vietnam and Laos (1992), then China, Russia, US

**Why it matters:** Watershed of formalisation (phase two); supports arguments on ASEAN norm-setting, but the unused High Council shows reluctance to judge fellow members.

#### Early economic schemes (PTA, AIP, AIC, AIJV) (1977–1983)

A series of trade-preference and industrial schemes after Bali that achieved little intra-ASEAN integration.

- PTA (Feb 1977): included ‘snow ploughs and nuclear reactors’; intra-ASEAN trade share stagnant at ~15%
- AIP (Mar 1980): members refused to limit competing production; Singapore withdrew
- AIC (1981): no projects where all could benefit
- AIJV (Nov 1983): only ~23 projects approved
- Root cause: ISI mindset — members saw each other as competitors

**Why it matters:** Key evidence for ASEAN’s weak economic record before 1992 — Mahathir (1991) called it ‘dismal’.

#### Cambodian Crisis (Dec 1978–1991)

Vietnam invaded Cambodia on 25 Dec 1978 and installed the Heng Samrin regime; ASEAN led diplomatic opposition and backed the CGDK coalition.

- 9 Jan 1979: Indonesian FM condemns Vietnam; 12 Jan 1979 Special FMs’ Meeting in Bangkok demands withdrawal
- ASEAN resolution adopted by UN General Assembly Nov 1979; Heng Samrin denied UN seat
- 1982 CGDK: FUNCINPEC (Sihanouk), KPNLF (Son Sann), Khmer Rouge — ‘morality versus realism’ (Prince Sirivudh)
- Disunity: Kuantan Declaration (Mar 1980), 5+2 Talks (Mar 1983), Chatichai’s ‘Battlefields to Marketplaces’ (1988)
- JIM I (Jul 1988) brought Vietnam to the table; final resolution came from P5 / UNTAC

**Why it matters:** Balanced case: swift unity and international standing (‘remarkable solidarity’ — Severino) versus internal divisions, moral failure and marginalisation in the final settlement.

#### 1992 Singapore Summit and AFTA (Jan 1992–2015)

The post-Cold War summit agreed AFTA via the CEPT scheme and upgraded the Secretariat, shifting ASEAN from security to economic focus.

- Target 0–5% tariffs in 15 years (2008); timetable accelerated Sep 1994
- Average tariff 12.76% (Jan 1993) → 3.2% (Jan 2002) → 2.36% (2003) — 6 years early
- Intra-ASEAN trade US$44.2bn (1993) → US$97.8bn (2000)
- NTBs surged 1,634 (2000) → 5,975 (2015); intra-ASEAN trade share hovering at 23–30%
- Context: EU, NAFTA, MERCOSUR blocs; all ASEAN economies now EOI

**Why it matters:** Main evidence of economic success after 1992, qualified by NTBs, a stagnant trade share and the CLMV two-tier problem.

#### ASEAN Regional Forum (ARF) (Jul 1994)

ASEAN established an Asia-Pacific security forum in Bangkok with 18 founding states including China, the US, Russia and Japan.

- Context: USSR dissolved; US bases in Philippines closed 1992; power vacuum
- Purpose: confidence-building, preventive diplomacy, conflict resolution — in that order
- ‘Preventive diplomacy’ only defined at 8th ARF (2001)
- China refuses to put the SCS on the ARF agenda

**Why it matters:** Shows ASEAN’s convening power (‘small powers steering large powers’ — Caballero-Anthony) but limited effectiveness (Leifer 1996: ‘confuses power and responsibility’).

#### Vietnam’s accession (Jul 1995)

Vietnam joined ASEAN, giving ‘final closure to political gulf between communist and non-communist SEA’.

- Vietnam’s motives: Doi Moi (1986), declining USSR aid, non-interference attractive for SCS, ARF to manage China and US
- ASEAN’s motives: Vietnam as buffer against China (1979 Sino-Vietnamese War), large market, ‘one Southeast Asia’
- Part of wider expansion: Brunei (Jan 1984), Laos and Myanmar (Jul 1997), Cambodia (Apr 1999)

**Why it matters:** Supports arguments about ASEAN completing ‘one Southeast Asia’, but also the expansion debate — no ideological cement in an expanded ASEAN.

#### Myanmar’s accession and non-interference debates (1997–1998)

Myanmar was admitted under ‘constructive engagement’ despite its military junta, while Cambodia’s entry was delayed and members debated loosening non-interference.

- Jul 1997: Laos and Myanmar join simultaneously
- 1997: Cambodia’s entry delayed over its domestic political situation — arguably violating non-interference
- Jun 1997: Malaysian FM Badawi advises Myanmar regime to dialogue with opposition
- 1998: Thai FM Surin’s ‘flexible engagement’ — only Philippines supports; becomes ‘enhanced interaction’
- EU and US relations damaged; ASEAN-EU FTA stalled

**Why it matters:** Evidence that non-interference was applied pragmatically but inconsistently; Severino counters that a two-tiered ASEAN beats a divided Southeast Asia.

#### Asian Financial Crisis and aftermath (1997–2000)

The AFC exposed ASEAN’s inability to manage volatile capital flows and led to new financial cooperation with China, Japan and South Korea.

- ASEAN Surveillance Process (1999): twice-yearly finance minister meetings; early warning system
- ASEAN+3 formalised 1999, replacing Mahathir’s EAEC concept
- Chiang Mai Initiative (2000): ASEAN+3 bilateral currency swap network
- Limitation: surveillance violates non-interference; poor data transparency; no common currency

**Why it matters:** Shows ASEAN adapting after crisis and widening external economic ties, but also the tension between deeper cooperation and non-interference.

### Historians

- **S. Rajaratnam (1992):** ASEAN was born ‘out of fear rather than idealistic convictions about regionalism’; ‘hang together or hang separately’; personal contacts between ministers are ASEAN’s glue.
- **Lee Kuan Yew (From Third World to First):** ASEAN’s real purpose was political and security — ‘we were banding together more for political objectives, stability and security’.
- **Thanat Khoman:** ASEAN deliberately excluded military entanglement; cooperative security was a means, not an end.
- **Chin Kin Wah (NUS):** Maphilindo was stillborn given its hasty conception amid Confrontation; ASEAN was more promising after Confrontation ended and Indonesia was included.
- **Hagiwara:** The PRC’s Cultural Revolution ‘dominated the consciousness of Asia’.
- **Michael Leifer:** ASEAN’s ‘central preoccupation was regional security even if only as a condition for development’; ZOPFAN was a long-term aspiration; the ARF ‘confuses power and responsibility’ (1996).
- **Rodolfo Severino:** The ASEAN Way’s informality and consensus is its defining feature; the ARF keeps rivals talking; ASEAN showed ‘remarkable solidarity’ over Cambodia.
- **Amitav Acharya:** TAC/High Council was a progressive step even if unproven; ASEAN managed Sabah through conflict avoidance — an ‘early ASEAN Way of conflict management’.
- **Shee Poon-Kim (1977):** ASEAN was the ‘product of a combination of common fears and weaknesses, not of common strength’.
- **Estrella Solidum:** The Philippines’ destiny shifted from West to East under multipolarity; ASEAN expressed an Asian identity.
- **Shaun Narine:** The Cambodian Crisis ‘symbolically undermined ZOPFAN’ because ASEAN took sides in a great power conflict.
- **Caballero-Anthony:** The ARF is ‘sui generis’ with small powers steering large ones, but ‘insufficient to address real security problems’.
- **Mary Turnbull:** ASA represented a ‘new and significant step’ arising from local initiatives for Southeast Asian needs.
- **Tay:** The ASEAN Way produces ‘plans more than action’.
- **Mahathir (1991):** ASEAN’s record in economic cooperation ‘has been dismal’.

### Using this topic in the exam

- Primary motivation controversy (common SBQ): official sources stress economic aims to avoid looking like a military bloc; founders privately confirm security was central — conclude the two were interlinked, with each member having its own priority.
- Membership expansion: ask whether it made ASEAN less coherent (no ideological cement, CLMV two-tier, Cambodia blocking SCS communiqués in 2012), balanced by Severino’s ‘two-tiered ASEAN better than a divided Southeast Asia’.
- Cambodian Crisis assessment: success (swift unified response, credibility), limitation (Thailand vs Indonesia/Malaysia), moral failure (backing the Khmer Rouge), final outcome (marginalised by P5/UNTAC).
- South China Sea: China prefers bilateral talks; ASEAN disunity; diplomacy raises the reputational cost of force; a Code of Conduct is not a resolution.
- Effectiveness essays: judge by domain (formation, institutions, security, economics, external relations, non-interference) using the master summary’s successes and limitations.

## P2 Theme III · Inter-state tensions

*What caused inter-state tensions in Southeast Asia — underlying structural factors or immediate triggers and the role of leaders — and what were the consequences for bilateral relations and for regional cooperation / ASEAN?*

The topic rests on one framework: underlying factors explain why some pairs of states are prone to clash (historical animosity, colonial-legacy borders, ethnic-religious rifts, ideology, shared borders and security, economic interests), while immediate factors explain why tension broke out at a specific time. Leaders matter separately — ‘States do not make policies, statesmen do’.

Konfrontasi (1963–1966) grew from Indonesia’s superiority complex, emboldened by success over West Irian and triggered by the Brunei Revolt; it ended only when Suharto displaced Sukarno. The Sabah dispute (1962–present) rests on the ambiguous 1878 pajakan agreement, was activated by Macapagal and inflamed by Marcos, and has been held in abeyance since the Ramos–Mahathir era.

The Third Indochina War (1978–1979) is the clearest multi-causal case: Vietnamese cultural superiority and expansionism, genuine security threats from the Khmer Rouge, the collapse of the Sino-Vietnamese alliance and the Soviet treaty ‘green light’ combined to produce the 25 Dec 1978 invasion and China’s Feb 1979 border war.

Consequences varied: disputes managed peacefully (the ASEAN Way) had minimal impact on regional cooperation, while spats and armed conflict were deleterious. Cross-case links include leaders’ domestic diversion (Sukarno, Marcos), resolution through leadership change, colonial borders splitting communities, great-power ‘green lights’, and divergent ASEAN threat perceptions of Vietnam.

**Underlying vs immediate factors**

- Underlying factors explain a pair of states’ propensity for tension; immediate factors explain why it escalated at a specific point in time
- A strong answer shows both the deep structural reason and the proximate trigger
- Duiker: the border dispute alone could have been resolved without violence — behind it lay the historic Cambodian fear of Vietnamese domination

**The six underlying/structural causes**

- Historical animosities — previous experiences of subjugation are most deeply imprinted on national memory
- Colonial legacy — vague agreements (the 1878 Sabah term) produce overlapping claims; borders split ethnic communities (Moro/Sabah Muslims)
- Ethnic-religious rifts — largely primordial; no SEA tension broke out purely over them
- Ideology — includes leaders’ worldviews (Sukarno’s ultra-nationalism); also a source of cooperation (anti-communism in 1967; ‘ASEAN’s finest hour’ in the 1980s)
- Shared borders and national security — but a shared border alone cannot fully explain tension
- Economic interests — most significant in the South China Sea, which is not a case study for this topic

**Role of leaders and reading sources in context**

- ‘States do not make policies, statesmen do’ — long-serving leaders (Suharto, Lee Kuan Yew, Tunku, Mahathir) shaped relations
- Four-part context checklist: pattern of interaction; domestic challenges; immediate bilateral issues; geopolitical considerations (Cold War, 1975 US withdrawal)
- Leaders externalised domestic discontent: Sukarno (Konfrontasi) and Marcos (Sabah)

**Consequences and resolution**

- Peaceful management/resolution (ASEAN Way) — minimal or even beneficial impact on regional cooperation
- Diplomatic spats or armed conflict — deleterious effects on regional cooperation
- Konfrontasi ended by regime change (1965–67); Sabah held in abeyance (1993 Ramos visit; 1998 shelving); Third Indochina War resolved through Doi Moi and the 1991 Paris Peace Conference

### Case studies

#### West Irian dispute (1950–1969)

Indonesia’s dispute with the Netherlands over Western New Guinea, settled by the New York Agreement of 15 Aug 1962, which transferred it to UN administration and on to Indonesia.

- Dutch resisted Indonesian claims since 1950; Sukarno threatened force backed by Soviet arms
- US-brokered transfer to UN administration in 1962; to Indonesia by 1 May 1963; 1969 plebiscite amid intimidation
- While unresolved, Subandrio’s UN speeches (9 & 20 Nov 1961) disclaimed any claim to Borneo and had ‘no objections’ to Malaysia

**Why it matters:** Explains the timing of Konfrontasi: having won West Irian by diplomacy plus the threat of force, Indonesia was emboldened to apply the same formula against Malaysia.

#### Macapagal’s Sabah claim (1962–1966)

President Macapagal formally claimed Sabah in 1962 after the Sultan of Sulu ceded his rights to Manila, breaking relations with Malaya/Malaysia.

- Claim rests on Sulu Sultanate rule 1704–1877 and the 1878 agreement, whose term pajakan meant ‘grant/cede’ to the British but ‘lease’ to Sulu
- Pro forma US claims in 1906 and 1920 show the claim predated 1962 — the timing needs explaining
- Motives: shed the ‘little brown brother’ image; frustration over US$73 million in unpaid war-damage compensation; raise the Philippines’ profile
- Froze ASA; Philippines rejected the UN fact-finding mission and refused to recognise Malaysia until 1966

**Why it matters:** Shows a genuine colonial-legacy basis activated by domestic political motives and leaders; Leifer called Macapagal’s public reasons ‘implausible’.

#### Brunei Revolt (Dec 1962)

Indonesian-backed insurgents under A.M. Azahari rose against the Sultan and Brunei’s inclusion in Malaysia and were rapidly crushed by British forces.

- Azahari proposed a North Borneo Federation of Brunei, Sabah and Sarawak
- His allies won all 16 elected seats in Brunei’s Legislative Council but were outvoted by the Sultan’s nominees
- The Sultan favoured Malaysia partly to keep absolute rule; disagreement over oil revenue

**Why it matters:** The immediate trigger of Konfrontasi: it gave Indonesia a grievance and showed Sukarno there were local sympathisers to mobilise.

#### Konfrontasi: motives and escalation (Jan 1963–Aug 1966)

Indonesia declared confrontation against Malaysia in late Jan 1963 and waged raids, incursions and sabotage until 1966.

- Underlying: superiority complex — size, revolutionary heritage (Aug 1945–Dec 1949), Indonesia Raya; Malaya’s independence ‘given on a platter’
- Five motives: strategic threat; suspicion over the 1958 Outer Island rebellion; Sukarno vs Tunku ideological clash; PKI threat perception; domestic diversion
- 12 Apr 1963: first raid on a police post at Tebedu, Sarawak
- 10 Mar 1965: MacDonald House bombing (3 dead, 33 wounded); Jan 1965 Indonesia withdraws from the UN
- AMDA (1957) brought in Britain, Australia and New Zealand; Indonesia achieved little militarily

**Why it matters:** Shows that tension is not mono-causal — historical animosity, ideology and leaders combined; leaders were ‘decisive’ (Sukarno/Suharto).

#### End of Konfrontasi through leadership change (1965–Feb 1967)

After the 1965 Gestapu coup, the anti-communist Suharto displaced Sukarno and ended Konfrontasi in Aug 1966.

- PKI destroyed after the failed coup
- Suharto needed to mend relations and repair an economy in shambles
- Konfrontasi ended Aug 1966; Sukarno ceded his position in Feb 1967
- Long shadow: 2014 Indonesian warship named after the MacDonald House bombers; Singapore barred it from its ports
- Last time Singaporean soldiers (1st and 2nd SIR) saw action in a major armed conflict

**Why it matters:** Underlying causes persisted yet conflict ended when the leader changed — strong evidence for the role of leaders; it opened the way to ASEAN in 1967.

#### Corregidor Incident / Jabidah Massacre (Mar 1968)

A covert Philippine commando unit trained at Corregidor to infiltrate and destabilise Sabah mutinied and was killed by Filipino troops; exposure suspended diplomatic relations.

- Trained under Marcos to manufacture a pretext for seizing Sabah
- Alternative account: trainees were deceived and murdered once the Sabah mission leaked — no conclusive evidence either way
- Placed severe strain on ASEAN, founded only the previous year
- Aquino (Oct 1968): Marcos would ‘conjure a foreign devil’ — ‘Why not Malaysia? Why not Sabah?’

**Why it matters:** Key evidence of domestic diversion; Aquino explicitly links Marcos’s use of Sabah to Sukarno’s Konfrontasi.

#### Collapse of the Sino-Vietnamese alliance (1968–1978)

China, Vietnam’s ‘rear base’ in the Second Indochina War, drifted into rivalry with Hanoi after the Tet Offensive and backed the Khmer Rouge as an anti-Vietnamese buffer.

- Hanoi’s talks with Washington (to the 1973 Paris Peace Accords) diverged from Chinese preferences
- Zhou Enlai (Apr 1968): US and USSR encircling China — ‘except [the part of] Vietnam’
- Armed clashes on the Sino-Vietnamese border and over SCS islands from 1973
- By 1974 China backed Pol Pot’s Khmer Rouge — the decisive move that placed China and Vietnam at war

**Why it matters:** The geopolitical layer of the Third Indochina War; Gilks stresses the danger of Soviet encirclement through Vietnam.

#### Malaysia–Philippines diplomatic confrontation (Jun 1968–Dec 1969)

Bilateral talks in Bangkok collapsed over the ICJ, the Philippines legislated sovereignty over Sabah and Malaysia boycotted ASEAN meetings until ties were restored.

- Jun–Jul 1968 Bangkok talks: Tan Sri Ghazali bin Shafie refused the ICJ and walked out — ‘nothing more to talk about’
- Sep 1968 Republic Act 5446 affirmed sovereignty over Sabah; the law remains in force
- Oct 1968 Philippine Foreign Secretary repeated the ICJ demand at the UN
- Diplomatic relations fully restored Dec 1969

**Why it matters:** Shows a diplomatic spat damaging early regional cooperation, and the persistence of the claim in Philippine law.

#### Sabah and the Moro rebels (1972–1980)

Sabah under Tun Mustapha served as a haven and supply base for Mindanao’s Moro separatists, linking the Sabah claim to Philippine internal security.

- MNLF revolt began 1972; 1973 Marcos constitution defined Sabah as Filipino territory
- Straits Times (11 Mar 1974): at least 200,000 rounds and 5,407 weapons from Malaysian sources
- Mustapha defeated Apr 1976; arms flow ceased by Dec 1976; Oct 1980 Philippine allegations of camps; Malaysia called claims ‘preposterous’
- Aug 1977 KL summit: Marcos promised steps to eliminate the claim, partly to make Malaysia cut ties with the MNLF
- Che Man: 160,000 to 200,000 evacuees in Sabah in 1983

**Why it matters:** Shows Malaysia was not innocent and that bilateral issues were inter-related bargaining chips; colonial borders split one Muslim community.

#### Ethnic Chinese exodus and the ‘Asian Cuba’ (1975–1978)

The exodus of ethnic Chinese from Vietnam accelerated in 1978, and China publicly protested and branded Vietnam an ‘Asian Cuba’.

- Exodus began as early as 1975
- 30 Apr / 24 May 1978: China protests; alleges 70,000 refugees have fled to China
- China called Vietnam a Soviet client state and pawn in global Soviet strategy

**Why it matters:** An intensifying immediate issue in Sino-Vietnamese relations before the 1979 border war.

#### The ‘special relationship’ and Vietnam’s Dec 1977 incursion (Apr 1977–Feb 1978)

After escalating Khmer Rouge attacks, Vietnam launched a large cross-border operation in Dec 1977; Phnom Penh broke off relations and rejected Vietnamese peace proposals.

- Apr–Sep 1977 Khmer Rouge attacks killed Vietnamese civilians; Vietnamese troops on the border rose from 60,000 to 100,000 by 1978
- 16 Dec 1977 invasion (30,000–60,000 troops; chronology gives ≈50,000) occupying a 450 km strip
- 31 Dec 1977: Radio Phnom Penh suspends relations and calls for a ‘people’s war’
- 5 Feb 1978 three-point peace proposal ignored; Feb 1978 demilitarised zone rejected by the China-backed Khmer Rouge
- Roots: nam tien (1620s), Minh Mang’s ‘Vietnamisation’ (1830s); ‘we should maintain it as a child’

**Why it matters:** Combines deep historical animosity with genuine security threats; the rejected DMZ proposal complicates a narrative of pure unprovoked Vietnamese aggression.

#### Soviet-Vietnamese Treaty of Friendship (3 Nov 1978)

The USSR and Vietnam signed a treaty whose Article 6 committed both to mutual consultation/action if either was attacked or threatened.

- Chang Pao-Min: the treaty ‘gave the final green light’
- Functioned as Vietnam’s ‘strategic reinsurance’ against Chinese retaliation
- Convinced China that Vietnam was a fully committed Soviet client

**Why it matters:** The final permissive condition for invasion — supports the argument that great-power patronage was enabling, not merely contextual.

#### Vietnamese invasion of Kampuchea (25 Dec 1978–8 Jan 1979)

150,000 Vietnamese troops invaded Democratic Kampuchea, overran its forces within two weeks and installed the pro-Vietnamese PRK.

- 3 Dec 1978: Vietnam sponsors KNUFNS as cover
- 8 Jan 1979: PRK established; UN continues to recognise Democratic Kampuchea
- Pol Pot’s regime responsible for the deaths of close to a quarter of all Cambodians (1975–Dec 1978)
- 22 Mar 1979 Lao-Kampuchea Agreement realised the ‘Indochinese Federation’ — ‘big powerism’
- US read it through Cold War containment (Oksenberg: ‘Hanoi’s genocidal policy’)

**Why it matters:** Clearest illustration of layered causation; Leifer: Vietnam was determined that Kampuchea be its satellite on geopolitical grounds.

#### China’s border war against Vietnam (17 Feb–Mar 1979)

About 200,000 PLA troops invaded northern Vietnam in a punitive, limited incursion and withdrew in March 1979.

- China shipped fresh military supplies to Kampuchea from 1 Feb 1978
- Hanoi Radio (10 Feb 1978) blamed ‘international reactionaries’
- PLA’s first major combat operation since the Korean War armistice of 1953
- Deng Xiaoping to Carter (Jan–Feb 1979): Soviet bases from Afghanistan and Iran to Vietnam

**Why it matters:** Shows how the Sino-Soviet rivalry and Cold War triangle turned a Vietnam–Kampuchea dispute into wider war.

#### Thailand–Vietnam relations and rapprochement (1979–1995)

Thailand became a frontline state against Vietnam, splitting ASEAN, until Doi Moi and the end of confrontation opened the way to rapprochement.

- Thailand, backed by Singapore, insisted on total Vietnamese withdrawal; Malaysia and Indonesia favoured dialogue
- Vietnam’s Communist Party had cut ties with its Thai counterpart in 1978
- 1986 Doi Moi; May 1988 Thirteenth Politburo Resolution ends confrontation with ASEAN
- Aug 1988 Chatichai: ‘from a battlefield to a market place’
- Oct 1991 Paris Peace Conference; Vietnam joins ASEAN 1995

**Why it matters:** Consequences for ASEAN cohesion and evidence that shared economic interest and geopolitical realignment can transform entrenched hostility.

#### Managing the Sabah dispute in abeyance (1986–1998)

After Marcos fell, the Philippines and Malaysia shelved the Sabah claim and pursued economic and military cooperation on a separate track.

- 1987: Aquino fails to convince Congress to drop the claim
- Feb 1993: Ramos’s state visit — first since 1968; Joint Commission established
- Feb 1994 Mahathir’s reciprocal visit; Sep 1994 defence cooperation pact
- 1998: Ramos convinces Congress to shelve the dispute
- Malaysia consistently refuses ICJ arbitration

**Why it matters:** The ASEAN Way of holding disputes in abeyance; de-escalation tracked leadership change (Marcos→Aquino→Ramos).

#### Lahad Datu standoff (Feb–Mar 2013)

200–300 armed men of the ‘Royal Army of Sulu’, acting for self-proclaimed Sultan Jamalul Kiram III, landed in Sabah and were defeated after a month of clashes.

- 80 dead (68 militants, 10 Malaysian security personnel, 2 civilians)
- Philippine government refused to back Kiram and told the militants to stand down
- Domestic Filipino protests show nationalist sentiment remains a live constraint
- Malaysia halted its annual payment to the Sultan of Sulu’s heirs (2013), revealed in July 2020

**Why it matters:** Confirms the durability of the status-quo management framework even when non-state actors tried to force the issue.

### Historians

- **Chandran Jeshurun (Malaysia: Fifty Years of Diplomacy):** The postwar political evolution of British-influenced Malaya and revolutionary Indonesia predetermined their uneasy relationship.
- **J.D. Legge (Sukarno: A Political Biography):** Indonesia, as the major regional power, felt entitled to consultation on territories near its borders, and Sukarno was sensitive to an imperial power disposing of its possessions to keep influence.
- **Matthew Jones (Conflict and Confrontation in Southeast Asia):** Animosity was fed by 1958 rebellion refugees in Malaya and Indonesian suspicion that Kuala Lumpur had irredentist designs on Sumatra.
- **Michael Leifer:** Macapagal’s public reasons for opposing Malaysia were ‘implausible’ but had mythological credence in the Philippines; Vietnam was determined on geopolitical grounds that Kampuchea be its satellite.
- **Maximo V. Soliven (Manila Times, 5 May 1968):** The Sabah ‘free’ elections were run by the British and Malaysians, who ‘ran circles around’ a handful of UN observers.
- **Benigno S. Aquino Jr. (Oct 1968):** Marcos revived the Sabah claim to divert public wrath, like Sukarno — ‘why not conjure a foreign devil?’
- **W.K. Che Man:** Tun Mustapha played a dominant role in assisting the Moro struggle, using Sabah as a training camp, supply depot and sanctuary in 1972–1976.
- **Isagani de Castro, Jr.:** The fall of Marcos allowed ties to be repaired; Ramos and Mahathir agreed to shelve the Sabah dispute so it would not obstruct economic ties.
- **David Chandler (A History of Cambodia):** Records Vietnamese condescension toward Khmer customs and the annals’ view of Cambodia as a ‘child’ to be maintained.
- **William J. Duiker (Vietnam since the Fall of Saigon):** The conflict was rooted in centuries of Khmer–Vietnamese animosity since the ‘march to the South’, and the border dispute alone could have been resolved without violence.
- **Chang Pao-Min:** The Soviet-Vietnamese Treaty of Friendship ‘gave the final green light’ for the invasion.
- **Anne Gilks (The Breakdown of the Sino-Vietnamese Alliance, 1970–1979):** After the war the danger of Soviet encirclement through Vietnam became more real, raising the stakes for China and the USSR.
- **Michel Oksenberg (4 Dec 1979):** Hanoi must face protracted resistance, Chinese military pressure and isolation — a Cold War view that applied ‘genocidal’ to Vietnam.

### Using this topic in the exam

- Use the underlying vs immediate distinction explicitly: show both the structural propensity and the proximate trigger for each dispute.
- Compare causes across the three cases with the comparative table: leaders decisive in Konfrontasi and Sabah; great-power role decisive in the Third Indochina War; colonial borders central to Sabah.
- Third Indochina War resists single-factor explanation — culture alone misses timing, security alone misses scale, Sino-Vietnamese rivalry alone misses why Cambodia, the Cold War alone misses Vietnamese motivation.
- Thematic links: domestic diversion (Sukarno, Marcos — Aquino draws the parallel); resolution through leadership change; great-power ‘green lights’; divergent ASEAN views of Vietnam (Malaysia/Indonesia vs Thailand/Singapore).
- Read sources in context with the four-part checklist; watch for Cold War bias (e.g. Oksenberg calling Vietnam’s occupation ‘genocidal’).
- Syllabus boundary: the South China Sea is not a case study for this topic.

## P2 Theme III · Singapore–Malaysia water

*Was the water dispute a symptom or a cause of changes in Singapore–Malaysia relations — a flashpoint for other tensions, or a vehicle for cooperation that helped mend relations?*

Singapore and Malaysia are geographically inseparable and mutually dependent, but relations have alternated between cordiality and tension. Water is best understood as BOTH a symptom of, and a cause of, changes in bilateral relations — not a standalone issue. The syllabus focus is pre-2000; post-2000 material is context for SBQ source provenance only.

The legal basis is the 1961 and 1962 agreements (to 2011 and 2061), guaranteed by the 1965 Separation Agreement. Singapore treats their sanctity as symbolic of its sovereignty and an existential matter, while Malaysia faces water insecurity, unfavourable optics of selling water ‘cheaply’, and leaders tempted to use water as leverage.

Causes combine the Separation legacy (an abang-adik syndrome), geography, race, Singapore’s scarcity and Malaysian domestic politics. Water became a flashpoint whenever other disputes arose — the 1986 Herzog visit and the post-AFC CLOB, CPF, CIQ and RSAF disputes — with leaders’ remarks and Mahathir’s ‘confrontational diplomacy’ amplifying tension.

Management shows water used constructively: the 1998 ‘funds for water’ deal and the ‘package’ approach ended the period on a broadly positive note. Singapore’s self-sufficiency drive (catchments, NEWater, desalination, the Indonesia attempt) reduced Malaysia’s leverage over time, while the dispute resurfaced under Mahathir in 2002–03 and 2018.

**Causes: pattern of bilateral relations**

- Legacy of Separation: bitterness of Merger (1963–65) as a long-term ‘pervasive overhang’; PAP meritocracy vs Alliance communalism
- Geography: 1km-wide Johor Strait, cross-Causeway kinship; FPDA (1971) as confidence-building measure and barometer of relations
- Race: Singapore as a majority-Chinese, successful scapegoat for Malaysian politicians (Badawi, 1990)
- Water as leverage: Tunku (9 Aug 1965) — Malaysia could pressure Singapore ‘by threatening to turn off the water in Johor’

**Causes: Singapore’s perspective**

- Scarcity: among the 8 worst water-stressed countries in Asia; 1963–1999 population 1.8m → 3.9m, total consumption up 4.7x
- WWII memory of the severed Causeway supply ties water to existential security
- Sanctity of agreements = sovereignty (Jayakumar); Johor did not request a review in 1986/87, so Singapore argues it lost the right
- SAF built as ‘credible insurance against a cut of supply of water’; ~5% of GDP on defence

**Causes: Malaysia’s domestic politics and leaders**

- Malaysia’s own water insecurity; contractually unable to raise the raw water price unilaterally
- Optics: selling water ‘cheaply’ while Johor (1990) and Malacca (1991) rationed water (Kog Yue Choong)
- PUB granted ad-hoc extra treated water requests — Malaysian threats more symbolic than practical
- Mahathir’s ‘confrontational diplomacy’ (Najib’s term): water-cutoff threats correlate with his terms (1981–2003, 2018–2020)

**Management and consequences**

- Constructive use of water: ‘funds for water’ (Nov 1998) and the ‘package’ approach (1998–2000)
- Alternative sources reduced dependence and therefore Malaysian leverage
- Two-direction framework: water as flashpoint (CIQ, CLOB, CPF) vs water cooperation as symptom of improved relations
- Interdependence: Singapore needs Johor’s raw water; Johor needs Singapore’s treated water — Goh: water a ‘symbol’ of an ‘interlocking relationship’

### Case studies

#### 1927 Gunong Pulai agreement (5 Dec 1927)

Singapore’s municipal commissioners and Sultan Ibrahim of Johor agreed the first water scheme after rapid 1920s population growth outstripped local catchments.

- Singapore paid 30 sen/acre rent on Gunong Pulai land; water otherwise free
- Earlier reliance on Thomson/MacRitchie, Pearl’s Hill, Lower Pierce catchments
- 1953: Sungei Tebrau Scheme completed — 1,600mm pipeline through the Causeway
- Overridden by the 1961 and 1962 Agreements

**Why it matters:** Background showing Singapore’s long-standing dependence on Johor water.

#### 1961 and 1962 Water Agreements (1961–1962)

After the 1961–62 and 1963–64 droughts exposed Singapore’s vulnerability, the Tebrau & Scudai (1961) and Johor River (1962) agreements set long-term supply and prices.

- 1961: exclusive right for 50 years (to 2011); rent RM5/acre + 3 sen/1,000 gallons raw water; Malaysia pays 50 cents/1,000 gallons treated
- Johor supplied treated water up to 12% of raw water drawn (min. 4 million gallons/day)
- 1962 (28 Sep): 99 years (to 2061); up to 250 million gallons/day; Johor treated water up to 2%
- Price review possible after 25 years (1986/1987); arbitration if negotiation fails

**Why it matters:** The legal core of the dispute; the 25-year review clause becomes central to the later argument over Malaysia’s ‘right to review’.

#### Merger, Separation and the 1965 Separation Agreement (1963–Aug 1965)

Bitter PAP–Alliance rivalry during Merger led to Separation on 9 Aug 1965; the Separation Agreement incorporated the 1961/62 water agreements.

- PAP’s failed 1964 federal bid for 9 seats (won only 1)
- July 1964 race riots after the Prophet Muhammad’s birthday procession (21 deaths, 460 injuries, official figures)
- 9 May 1965: PAP forms the Malaysian Solidarity Convention — the ‘final straw’
- 7 Aug 1965 Separation Agreement: water guarantee written into the Malaysian Constitution and registered with the UN
- Tunku (9 Aug 1965): could pressure Singapore ‘by threatening to turn off the water in Johor’

**Why it matters:** Supports the argument that the water dispute is a symptom of deep distrust — Irvin Lim’s abang-adik syndrome — and explains why Singapore links the agreements to sovereignty.

#### Reducing dependence: catchments, demand measures, desalination and NEWater (1963–2011)

Singapore built local catchments, cut demand and developed desalination and NEWater to reduce reliance on Malaysian water.

- Post-1963 PUB: damming of 7 rivers, SEA’s first stormwater collection system; S$1.9 billion capex (1963–1993)
- By 2002 half of Singapore’s land used for catchment; ~half of supply from catchments
- 10 Jun 1997: tariff restructuring, ‘Save Water’ campaigns, industrial conservation incentives; desalination plans announced 1997
- NEWater introduced 9 Aug 2002; goal of 55 mgd (257,000 m³) by 2010
- Singapore declared by 2002 it would not renew the 1961 agreement, which expired in 2011

**Why it matters:** Central to explaining WHY tensions eased after 2003 — reduced dependence meant reduced Malaysian leverage.

#### Pedra Branca dispute (1979–2008)

Malaysia’s 1979 map claimed Pedra Branca as part of Johor; after years of tension the ICJ awarded the island to Singapore in May 2008.

- Horsburgh Lighthouse built 1851; Singapore occupation since the 1840s; Malaysia raised no challenge 1847–1979; 1953 Johor disclaimed ownership
- Feb 1980 Singapore rejects the claim; May 1992 fishing-vessel harassment reports
- Sep 1994 Goh–Mahathir agree to refer to ICJ; Jun 1995 talks fail; submitted only 2005
- ICJ (May 2008): Pedra Branca to Singapore; Middle Rocks to Malaysia; South Ledge unresolved
- Feb 2017 Najib’s application to reverse; withdrawn after 2018; Feb 2019 added to East Coast GRC

**Why it matters:** Annex/context: part of the backdrop of deteriorating relations, and an example of a dispute settled by legal means rather than bilateral talks.

#### Chaim Herzog visit (18–20 Nov 1986)

Israeli President Chaim Herzog’s visit to Singapore angered Malaysia, which threatened to cut off water among other measures.

- Singapore had used Israeli help to build the SAF (Israelis code-named ‘Mexicans’)
- Mahathir’s anti-Zionist speech (aimed at outflanking PAS) preceded the announcement by 2 weeks; Singapore refused to cancel
- Malaysia recalled its High Commissioner; threatened to sever transport ties, expel Singapore from ASEAN, cut off water
- Suharto mediated in Feb 1987, symbolically crossing the Causeway

**Why it matters:** Shows the flashpoint dynamic — water threats used as leverage in an unrelated dispute, shaped by Malaysian domestic politics.

#### Singapore–Indonesia water agreements (1987–1991)

Singapore sought Indonesian water as an alternative to Malaysia, but the scheme never materialised.

- 1987: LKY publicly expresses interest
- 28 Aug 1990: Water Agreement signed
- 1991: agreement confirms 1,000 million gallons/day for 100 years from Sungei Kampar
- Derailed by post-AFC instability (Suharto → Habibie)
- Indonesian unhappiness over ecological damage; accusations Singapore ‘exploited’ Indonesia

**Why it matters:** Shows Singapore’s diversification efforts and that Indonesia had no contractual leverage, limiting escalation.

#### 1988 MoU and 1990 Supplementary Agreement (Jun 1988–Nov 1990)

After 6 years of negotiation, a 1988 MoU covered water, gas and a ferry, forming the basis of the 1990 Supplementary Agreement and the Linggiu Dam.

- 1988 MoU (27–28 Jun): sale of water and gas; Changi Point–SE Johor ferry; new bridge proposed but undecided
- 24 Nov 1990: Singapore may build the Linggiu Dam at its own cost
- Johor designates ~21,600 ha; Singapore pays RM320 million compensation + RM30/1,000 sq ft annual rent
- Mar 1990 water rationing in northern Johor; Linggiu project completed 1993

**Why it matters:** Evidence of water cooperation as a symptom of good relations, set against Malaysian optics of rationing while selling water to Singapore.

#### Relocation of the CIQ (1989–2011)

Singapore moved its CIQ to Woodlands and wanted Malaysia to co-locate; Malaysia reneged on the 1990 Points of Agreement in 1997.

- 1989: CIQ moved from Tanjong Pagar to Woodlands to curb drug smuggling
- Points of Agreement signed 27 Nov 1990 (LKY–Daim Zainuddin)
- 1997: Malaysia reneged, stayed at Tanjong Pagar
- Resolved 2010 (Najib–Lee Hsien Loong); land swap finalised 2011

**Why it matters:** A circumstantial (not directly AFC-related) dispute that fed the post-AFC flashpoint dynamic.

#### CLOB dispute (1990–1998)

Malaysia objected to the trading of Malaysian shares on Singapore’s CLOB and banned it during the AFC.

- CLOB established 1990, when SES split from the KLSE; KLSE warned of an ‘unrecognised market’
- Malaysia alleged CLOB shares were used to short the KLSE
- Aug 1998 National Economic Action Council called for closure; trading banned 31 August

**Why it matters:** Economic dispute directly related to the AFC; part of one of the worst periods of tension since Separation.

#### Operation Malindo Darsasa 3AB (9 Aug 1991)

A Malaysia–Indonesia joint exercise simulated an airborne assault 18km from Woodlands on Singapore’s National Day.

- Singapore responded with Operation Trojan: full SAF alert, partial reserve mobilisation, live ammunition
- Singapore’s defence budget ~5% of GDP, 25–30% of annual budget
- Doctrine: ‘Poisonous Shrimp’ (1970s–80s) → ‘Swift and Decisive Victory’ (1990s)
- Some Malaysian politicians threatened to ‘take back’ Pedra Branca (and even Singapore)

**Why it matters:** Shows Singapore’s insecurity and how disputes fed into and amplified one another.

#### Provocative remarks by Singapore leaders (1996–May 1998)

Remarks by Lee Kuan Yew and Lim Hng Kiang angered Malaysia and amplified calls to dissolve the water agreements.

- 1996: LKY says re-merger ‘easier’ if Malaysia were meritocratic
- 1997: LKY calls Johor crime-ridden in a defamation-suit affidavit; he apologised
- 1998: The Singapore Story angers the Malaysian elite — Malaysia skips an Oct 1998 FPDA exercise
- May 1998: Lim Hng Kiang questions the quality of Malaysian treated water

**Why it matters:** Supports the argument on the role of political leaders on both sides in escalating tension.

#### AFC-era financial disputes: interest rates and CPF (1998)

Malaysia saw Singapore’s high ringgit rates as sabotage and sought early CPF withdrawals for West Malaysian workers.

- Mahathir cut rates and imposed capital controls; Richard Hu said Singapore’s rates were market-determined
- Retrenched West Malaysians sought early CPF withdrawal; Singapore law allowed withdrawal only at 55/disability/permanent departure
- CPF request came just a week before water was raised (Jul 1998); Mahathir denied a link
- Jul 1998: Mahathir’s ambiguous statement on water

**Why it matters:** Shows water taking on a strategic dimension amid parallel disputes after the AFC.

#### RSAF airspace revoked (Sep 1998)

Malaysia revoked the RSAF’s use of southern Johor airspace.

- Both exacerbated tensions and was symptomatic of pre-existing mistrust
- Later included in the ‘package’ of bilateral issues

**Why it matters:** Illustrates the interrelatedness of disputes — security and water became linked bargaining items.

#### ‘Funds for Water’ deal (Nov 1998)

Goh Chok Tong agreed to Malaysian requests for loans in return for a guaranteed long-term water supply, negotiating parallel agreements.

- Goh visited KL on Mahathir’s invitation to discuss water, RSAF airspace and CIQ together
- Singapore agreed to loans for the depreciating ringgit as goodwill; sought supply with no price review
- As Malaysia’s economy recovered, Mahathir dropped the loan request and proposed linking ALL issues

**Why it matters:** Key evidence that water could be a vehicle for cooperation when both sides had incentives to de-escalate.

#### The ‘package’ approach and the Aug 2000 price request (1998–2000)

Mahathir proposed discussing all bilateral issues as one package; in Aug 2000 Malaysia sought to raise the raw water price to 45 sen/1,000 gallons.

- Price from 3 sen (1961 rate) to 45 sen/1,000 gallons
- Package also covered post-2061 supply, RSAF airspace, KTM/Bukit Timah land, up to RM3bn early CPF withdrawals
- 2000: Mahathir adds a Causeway-replacement bridge; SM Lee agreed to include it
- Not formally adopted due to further unilateral revisions in the early 2000s

**Why it matters:** Ends the syllabus period on a broadly positive note — mutual concessions and predictability — though the compromise later collapsed.

#### Abdurrahman Wahid’s remarks (Nov 2000)

Indonesian President Wahid called Singapore profit-minded and manipulative and revealed he had discussed jointly restricting water with Mahathir.

- Idea of Indonesia and Malaysia jointly restricting water to ‘teach Singapore a lesson’
- Stiffened Singapore’s resolve toward self-reliance
- Less serious than the Malaysia dispute as no plans were formally implemented

**Why it matters:** Comparison case: both show ‘abang/kakak’ dynamics, but only Malaysia had actual contractual leverage.

#### Re-emergence of the dispute (context only) (2001–2003)

Malaysia escalated price demands, Mahathir called off the package, and Singapore released the negotiation correspondence; the dispute subsided after Mahathir stepped down.

- 21 Feb 2001: 45 sen → 60 sen; Sep 2002: 60 sen → RM6.25
- Early 2002: Mahathir calls the agreements ‘unequal treaties’; 7 Oct 2002 calls off the package
- ‘Profiteering’ charge vs Singapore’s rebuttal: treatment costs RM2.40/1,000 gallons — a RM1.90 subsidy
- 24 Oct 2002: Rais Yatim says Malaysia could legislate to void the agreements
- 25 Jan 2003: Singapore releases correspondence; Mahathir steps down 31 Oct 2003

**Why it matters:** For SBQ provenance only — Singapore’s release of documents is a deliberate, defensive act; the timing correlates with Mahathir’s leadership.

#### 2018 renewed disagreements (context only) (2016–2020)

After Najib reaffirmed the 1962 Agreement, Mahathir on his return called the water price ‘ridiculous’ and sought renegotiation.

- Dec 2016 Najib affirms 1962-level supply; Jan 2018 Leaders’ Retreat joint statement
- Jul 2018 Mahathir seeks renegotiation of the 1962 Agreement
- Singapore MFA: the 1962 Agreement is guaranteed by the UN-registered 1965 Separation Agreement
- Stalls after Mahathir’s Feb 2020 resignation and COVID-19

**Why it matters:** Reinforces that the dispute resurfaces under Mahathir — correlating strongly with his personal leadership style.

### Historians

- **Irvin Lim Fang Jau (IDSS Monograph No. 3):** Malaysia’s perennial attempt to put Singapore in its place reflects an abang-adik syndrome with heavy historical baggage, and the SAF was ‘credible insurance against a cut of supply of water’.
- **Abdullah Ahmad Badawi (1990 speech):** An anti-Singapore bandwagon is popular in Malaysia because it involves race and religion, and the government must be seen to act.
- **Tunku Abdul Rahman (9 Aug 1965):** Malaysia could pressure Singapore by threatening to turn off the water in Johor.
- **S Jayakumar (Ministerial Statements):** The water price signifies Singapore’s existence as a sovereign nation and the sanctity of solemn agreements; Singapore released documents only when a win-win outcome was no longer realistic.
- **M.O. Ariff (1968):** Some treaties, explicitly the Singapore water treaty, are ‘so fundamental to existence of States’ that they cannot be dispensed with.
- **Lee Kuan Yew (From Third World to First):** Singapore built the SAF because it feared a ‘random act of madness like cutting off our water supplies’ and would go in, forcibly if need be.
- **Kog Yue Choong (IDSS Monograph No. 3):** Water is emotive because some Malaysian states suffer shortages while Johor seemed to put Singaporean needs first.
- **Najib Razak:** Termed Mahathir’s approach ‘confrontational diplomacy’.
- **Goh Chok Tong (Straits Times, 26 Jan 2001):** Continued Malaysian water sales signal that the two will be ‘interdependent forever’ and must co-exist happily.
