## Checked and left out

These looked like contradictions at first, but on a careful reading they are small or explained in the notes themselves.

- the Inter-State notes (B.1 Chronology) say the Malaysia idea was proposed in May 1961, but the ASEAN notes timeline (1.1) puts it in late 1961–62.  
  **Why it's fine:** The ASEAN row is a loose bundle of the Malaysia proposal with the 1962 Sabah claim; it differs from May 1961 by only a few months and both put the proposal in 1961.
- the ASEAN timeline says Malaysia–Philippines ties were severed in Sep 1963 when Malaysia was formed, but the ASA section of the ASEAN notes and Inter-State notes C.3 say the 1962 Sabah claim itself caused the break.  
  **Why it's fine:** The ASA bullet names the Sabah dispute as the cause, not the date of the rupture, and Inter-State C.1 has 1962 only as 'deteriorate', so a Sep 1963 break caused by the dispute fits; only C.3's vague 'once asserted' hints at an earlier break.
- the Inter-State notes say diplomatic relations were suspended when the Corregidor operation was exposed (Mar 1968), but the ASEAN notes link the withdrawal of ambassadors to Republic Act 5446 (Sep 1968).  
  **Why it's fine:** The notes stress different steps in the same 1968–69 escalation (relations suspended after Corregidor in March, ambassadors withdrawn after RA 5446 in September); both can be true together, and both end with ties restored in Dec 1969.
- the 3.1 timeline puts the ASEAN Surveillance Process in the 1997 row, but section 3.4 dates it to 1999.  
  **Why it's fine:** The 1997 row gives the ASP with an arrow as a result of the crisis and gives no date for it (the same row dates APT to 1999), so it does not actually date the ASP to 1997.
- sections 2.4 and 3.1 say the AEMM was set up at the Feb 1976 Bali Summit, but section 3.5 says “First AEMM (1978)”.  
  **Why it's fine:** The 1978 'First AEMM' sits under the ASEAN–EU entry, so it refers to the first ASEAN–EU ministerial meeting, a different body that happens to share the acronym; it is not the intra-ASEAN Economic Ministers' Meeting set up in 1976.
- the Section II timeline says the Pedra Branca case was brought to the ICJ in 1994, but the Annex (VIII.1.3) and the IV.4.2 table say only the referral was agreed in 1994 and the case was not submitted until 2005.  
  **Why it's fine:** The timeline's own '(referral agreed)' note limits the 1994 entry to the agreement to refer, which matches the other sections; the wording is just loose.
- §2 (and §12.8 and §23.3) start Peaceful Coexistence in 1953, but the §16.1 table ends Cold War I in 1955 and starts Peaceful Coexistence in 1955.  
  **Why it's fine:** Two periodisation tables start Peaceful Coexistence in 1953 vs 1955, but the notes themselves state that start/end years vary by historian, so this is an acknowledged alternative periodisation.
- the §11.3 and §23.3 timelines say Chinese troops crossed the Yalu and pushed UN forces south on 25 Oct 1950, but §11.7 dates China's entry into the war to 25 Nov 1950.  
  **Why it's fine:** §11.7 itself acknowledges Chinese contact in late Oct 1950 and dates only the massive counter-offensive/phase to late Nov, so the dates refer to the initial crossing versus the full-scale entry and are broadly compatible.
- the GATT Rounds table (E5) and the master timeline give the Uruguay Round as 1986–93, but the H4 cooperation table says the “Uruguay Round (1994) created the WTO”.  
  **Why it's fine:** Both sources agree the Uruguay Round created the WTO; 1994 versus 1986–93 is a one-year difference that reads as the round's conclusion/signing date rather than a different round period.
- the A2 periodisation ends the Golden Age in the late 1960s and treats late 1960s–1973 as a separate phase of reversal, but the B2 heading (and the A4 table) run the Golden Age through to 1973.  
  **Why it's fine:** The periodisations overlap compatibly: A2 marks the late 1960s as the start of slowing/strain within the broader 1950s–1973 growth era, and all sections treat 1973 as the turn to crisis; it is a labelling nuance, not incompatible claims.
- section 1.3 says the 1939 White Paper pacified the Arabs for a while, but section 1.7 says the Arabs rejected it because it postponed independence and did not fully stop Jewish immigration.  
  **Why it's fine:** Temporarily calming Arab unrest and formally rejecting the White Paper's terms are compatible; 1.7 adds detail rather than contradicting 1.3.
